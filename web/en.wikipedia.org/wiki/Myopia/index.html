Myopia

Other namesNear-sightedness, short-sightedness
SpecialtyOphthalmology, optometry
SymptomsDistant objects appear blurry; near objects clear; squinting; eyestrain; headaches
CausesCombination of genetic and environmental factors; eyeball too long or cornea too curved

Myopia, also known as near-sightedness and short-sightedness, is an eye disease where light focuses in front of, instead of on, the retina. As a result, distant objects appear blurry while close objects appear normal. Other symptoms may include headaches and eye strain. Severe myopia increases the risk of retinal detachment, cataracts, and glaucoma.

In myopia the eyeball is too long or the cornea too steeply curved, so distant images focus before the retina.

Causes

Myopia results mainly from the eyeball growing too long from front to back, so that the focal point of the lens falls short of the retina. Genetics plays a substantial role — children with two myopic parents are at much higher risk — but environmental factors matter: prolonged near work and, especially, insufficient time outdoors in childhood are associated with onset and progression. The global prevalence of myopia has risen sharply, exceeding 80–90% of young adults in some urban East and Southeast Asian populations; by some projections half the world's population will be myopic by 2050.

Diagnosis and classification

Myopia is diagnosed by an eye examination with refraction, and measured in dioptres as a negative value. "High myopia" is generally defined as −6.00 dioptres or worse; pathological myopia involves degenerative changes to the back of the eye.

Treatment

Blur is corrected with concave (minus) lenses in glasses or contact lenses, or by refractive surgery such as LASIK, which reshapes the cornea. In children, progression can be slowed with low-dose atropine eye drops, orthokeratology lenses worn overnight, and multifocal lens designs; increased outdoor time is recommended to delay onset.
