The Battle of Tondibi was fought on 13 March 1591 at Tondibi, north of Gao in the Niger Bend (present-day Mali), between an expeditionary force sent by the Saadian sultan of Morocco, Ahmad al-Mansur, and the army of the Songhai Empire under Askia Ishaq II. Though vastly outnumbered, the Moroccans destroyed the Songhai army thanks to their firearms, bringing down the last of the great medieval empires of the Western Sudan and reshaping the history of the region.

Background

The Songhai Empire — heir to Ghana and Mali — had reached its zenith under Askia Muhammad I (r. 1493–1528), ruling from Gao over Timbuktu, Djenné, and the salt mines of Taghaza, but had been weakened by succession disputes after his deposition. Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur of Morocco, enriched by his victory over Portugal at Alcácer Quibir (1578) and hungry for the gold of the Sudan and control of the trans-Saharan salt trade, quarrelled with Songhai over Taghaza and resolved on conquest. In October 1590 he dispatched an expedition under Judar Pasha — a Spanish-born eunuch in Moroccan service — of roughly 4,000 men, many of them Andalusian and renegade arquebusiers, with cannon, thousands of camels, and a great supply train, to cross the Sahara. The crossing took some 135 days and cost perhaps half the force, but in February 1591 the survivors reached the Niger.

The battle

Askia Ishaq II assembled a far larger army — tens of thousands of cavalry and infantry — at Tondibi, a cattle-grazing plain north of Gao, and, following tradition, placed a great herd of cattle before his lines. Judar arranged his arquebusiers in ranks with his small artillery. When the battle opened, Moroccan gunfire stampeded the Songhai cattle back into their own ranks and shattered the dense formations in front of them; the Songhai cavalry charged bravely but could not withstand volley fire, and the army dissolved in rout. Gao was occupied almost undefended, and the invaders moved on to Timbuktu and Djenné.

Aftermath

The Songhai state collapsed: Ishaq II fled and was killed within the year; a rump Songhai resistance under Askia Nouhou held out in Dendi (in the far southeast) for years, but the empire never recovered. The Moroccans installed a military government — the Pashalik of Timbuktu — whose garrisons, the Arma, gradually became a local hereditary caste. Contrary to al-Mansur's hopes, the conquest yielded little gold: the trade routes shifted toward the Atlantic coast and European ships, and the region entered a long political fragmentation. Timbuktu's scholarly culture suffered grievously — the jurist Ahmad Baba was deported in chains to Marrakesh with his library in 1594. Historians frequently compare Tondibi to the other gunpowder shocks of the age and treat 1591 as the dividing line between the medieval Sudanic empires and the early modern Sahel.
