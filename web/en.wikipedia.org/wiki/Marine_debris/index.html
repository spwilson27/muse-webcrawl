Marine debris

Also known asMarine litter
Main sourcesLand-based runoff, rivers, fishing activity, shipping
Major componentsPlastics, derelict fishing gear, glass, metal

Marine debris, also known as marine litter, is human-created solid waste that has deliberately or accidentally been released in a sea or waterway. Because most plastics do not biodegrade, they dominate modern marine debris, breaking down slowly into microplastics rather than disappearing.

Sources and accumulation

An estimated majority of marine debris originates on land, carried by rivers, storm drains, and wind; the remainder comes from activities at sea, including lost or discarded fishing gear ("ghost gear") and shipping losses. Ocean currents gather floating debris into five great subtropical gyres, most famously the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, where microplastic concentrations are elevated far from land.

Impacts

Wildlife ingest plastic or become entangled in nets and lines; hundreds of species, including seabirds, turtles, and marine mammals, are affected. Debris also transports invasive species, damages coral and benthic habitat, fouls beaches and fishing gear, and imposes cleanup and tourism costs on coastal communities. Microplastics have been detected throughout the water column, in sediments, and in seafood, though their human-health significance remains under study.

Mitigation

International rules such as MARPOL Annex V prohibit disposal of plastics at sea, and responses range from beach cleanups and river interception to product bans, extended producer responsibility, and large-scale ocean cleanup systems. Prevention at source is widely regarded as the most effective strategy.
