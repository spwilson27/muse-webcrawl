Swadeshi movement

The Swadeshi movement was an Indian economic and political movement promoting the use of domestically produced goods (swadeshi, "of one's own country") and the boycott of foreign — especially British — products. It became a mass movement in 1905 in protest against the partition of Bengal, and it remained a central strategy of the Indian independence movement through the Gandhian era.

Origins and the Bengal partition

Ideas of economic self-reliance were articulated in the 19th century by thinkers such as Dadabhai Naoroji, whose "drain theory" blamed British rule for India's poverty, and by early nationalists advocating indigenous industry. The movement ignited in 1905 when Viceroy Lord Curzon partitioned the province of Bengal, ostensibly for administrative efficiency but widely seen as a divide-and-rule measure. On 7 August 1905 the boycott was proclaimed at a Calcutta meeting; 16 October, the day partition took effect, was observed with fasting, processions, and the singing of "Bande Mataram", which became the movement's anthem.

Methods and spread

Protesters publicly burned foreign cloth, picketed shops selling imported goods, and promoted Indian alternatives: swadeshi mills, soap, matches, and banks, and national schools and colleges independent of government control. Leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai ("Lal-Bal-Pal"), and Aurobindo Ghosh extended the programme beyond economics toward swaraj (self-rule). In 1911 the British reversed the partition of Bengal, partly in response to the agitation.

Under Gandhi

Mohandas Gandhi made swadeshi a pillar of his programme from 1918 onward: the spinning wheel (charkha) and homespun khadi became symbols of self-reliance and dignity of labour, and boycotts of foreign cloth featured in the Non-cooperation (1920–22) and Civil Disobedience (1930–34) movements. The charkha even appeared on the flag of the Indian National Congress and, stylised, on independent India's national flag.

Legacy

Historians credit the Swadeshi movement with awakening mass political participation, stimulating Indian industry, and shaping the economic nationalism of post-1947 India. The term continues to be invoked in debates on self-reliance, from import-substitution policy to modern "Make in India" campaigns.
