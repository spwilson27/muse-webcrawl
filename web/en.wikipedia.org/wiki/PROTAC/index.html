PROTAC


Full name: proteolysis targeting chimera. First described: 2001 (Sakamoto, Crews, Deshaies et al.).

A PROTAC (proteolysis targeting chimera) is a heterobifunctional small molecule composed of two ligands joined by a linker: one binds a protein of interest, and the other binds an E3 ubiquitin ligase. By bringing the two together, a PROTAC induces ubiquitination of the target protein, marking it for destruction by the proteasome.

Mechanism

PROTACs work event-driven rather than occupancy-driven: after triggering ubiquitin transfer, the molecule is released and can catalytically direct the degradation of further target copies. Commonly recruited ligases include cereblon (CRBN) and von Hippel–Lindau (VHL). Because only a binding surface — not an active site — is required on the target, the approach promises to drug proteins long considered "undruggable," and to eliminate scaffolding functions that inhibitors cannot block.

Development

The concept was first demonstrated in 2001 by Kathleen Sakamoto, Craig Crews, Raymond Deshaies, and colleagues, using a peptide-based molecule. Fully small-molecule PROTACs followed in 2008, and the field expanded rapidly after 2015. Arvinas, founded by Crews, advanced the first oral PROTACs into clinical trials, including bavdegalutamide (ARV-110) and vepdegestrant (ARV-471) targeting the androgen and estrogen receptors in prostate and breast cancer.

Challenges

PROTACs are larger than typical small-molecule drugs, complicating oral bioavailability and cell permeability; efficacy also depends on ligase expression in the target tissue, and resistance can arise through ligase mutation or loss.
