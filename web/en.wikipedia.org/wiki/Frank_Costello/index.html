Frank Costello

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Frank Costello (Italian: [koˈstɛllo]; born Francesco Castiglia; January 26, 1891 – February 18, 1973) was an Italian-American crime boss of the Luciano crime family (later the Genovese family). A leading figure of the American Mafia for four decades, he was known as the "Prime Minister of the Underworld" for his preference for diplomacy over violence, his political connections, and his alliances across criminal and legitimate society.

Rise

Born in Calabria, Italy, Castiglia immigrated to New York with his family as a small child and grew up in East Harlem. After street-gang years and early convictions, he rose through bootlegging during Prohibition, partnering with Charles "Lucky" Luciano, Meyer Lansky, and others in rum-running and slot-machine operations. When Luciano was imprisoned in 1936 and then deported, Costello became acting boss of the family, expanding its gambling interests to Louisiana and Las Vegas and building deep ties to Tammany Hall politics.

Kefauver hearings and fall

Costello became a national figure in 1951 when he testified before Senator Estes Kefauver's committee investigating organised crime; at his lawyers' request his face was not shown, and television audiences saw only his nervous hands — images that fixed him in the public imagination. Convictions for contempt of the Senate and later tax evasion sent him to prison for much of the 1950s. In May 1957 he survived an assassination attempt ordered by Vito Genovese, shot in the head by Vincent Gigante in the lobby of his apartment building; Costello thereafter retired from family leadership, ceding power to Genovese.

Later years

Costello lived quietly at his Central Park West apartment and Long Island home, consulted occasionally as an elder statesman of the mob, and died of a heart attack in 1973. He was buried in Saint Michael's Cemetery in Queens. His career — emphasising bribery, gambling, and political influence over conspicuous violence — is often contrasted with that of his more ruthless contemporaries.
