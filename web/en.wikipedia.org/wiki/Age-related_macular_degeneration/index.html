Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a medical condition of the eye that results in blurred or lost vision in the centre of the visual field, caused by damage to the macula, the central part of the retina responsible for sharp, detailed vision. It is a leading cause of vision loss among people over 50 in developed countries, affecting on the order of 200 million people worldwide. Peripheral vision is usually preserved, so AMD rarely causes total blindness, but it can make reading, driving and recognising faces difficult or impossible.

Types and signs

AMD begins with the accumulation of drusen — yellow deposits beneath the retina — and changes in the retinal pigment epithelium. In the dry form (about 85–90 per cent of cases) the light-sensitive cells of the macula slowly break down (geographic atrophy in advanced disease), causing gradual central blurring. In the wet form, abnormal blood vessels grow from the choroid under the macula (choroidal neovascularisation); they leak blood and fluid and can destroy central vision within weeks if untreated. A characteristic early symptom is metamorphopsia — straight lines appearing wavy — which patients can monitor with an Amsler grid.

Causes and risk factors

Age is the dominant risk factor. Smoking roughly doubles to triples the risk and is the main modifiable factor. Genetics play a large role: variants in complement-system genes (notably CFH, identified in 2005) and the ARMS2/HTRA1 locus are strongly associated. Other associations include hypertension, obesity, diet low in antioxidants and omega-3s, and lighter eye colour and European ancestry.

Treatment

There is no cure for dry AMD; high-dose antioxidant and zinc supplements (the AREDS and AREDS2 formulations) slow progression in intermediate disease, and in 2023 the first drugs for geographic atrophy (the complement inhibitors pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol) were approved in the United States, slowing lesion growth. Wet AMD is treated with intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF agents such as ranibizumab, aflibercept and bevacizumab, which suppress vessel growth and leakage and can stabilise or improve vision, especially when started early. Low-vision aids, monitoring of the fellow eye, and not smoking are standard advice.
