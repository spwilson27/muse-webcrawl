A swamp is a forested wetland area. Swamps are considered to be transition zones because both land and water play a role in creating this environment. They vary in size and may be freshwater, brackish, or saltwater systems. Swamps are distinguished from marshes by their dominant vegetation: swamps are dominated by woody plants such as trees and shrubs, whereas marshes are dominated by soft-stemmed herbaceous plants.

Swamps occur alongside rivers, lakes, and coasts in low-lying areas where water collects and drainage is slow. They are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth, providing habitat for a wide variety of plant and animal species, filtering water, and protecting shorelines from flooding and erosion.

Characteristics

Swamp water may be fresh, brackish, or seaward. Freshwater swamps form in low-lying areas or basins where rainwater and runoff collect, while saltwater swamps — including mangrove swamps — occur along tropical and subtropical coastlines. Many swamps have slow-moving or stagnant water rich in decaying vegetation, which gives the water a dark, tea-like colour from tannins.

Because swamp soils are saturated and low in oxygen, decomposition is slow, and in some northern swamps this leads to the accumulation of peat. Bottomland hardwood swamps are common along the floodplains of large rivers such as the Mississippi.

Types

  • Freshwater swamps – found inland, often along rivers and lakes; examples include the Okefenokee Swamp in Georgia and Florida and the Atchafalaya Basin in Louisiana.
  • Saltwater swamps – coastal systems, frequently dominated by mangroves in the tropics; the Sundarbans of India and Bangladesh is the largest mangrove swamp in the world.
  • Shrub swamps – dominated by shrubs such as buttonbush, alder, and willow.
  • Forested swamps – dominated by trees such as bald cypress, water tupelo, red maple, and Atlantic white cedar.

Ecology

Swamps support rich biodiversity. Characteristic animals include alligators, beavers, otters, herons, egrets, frogs, snakes, and numerous fish and invertebrate species. Swamp plants are adapted to waterlogged, low-oxygen soils; bald cypress trees, for example, develop "knees" (pneumatophores) that project above the water. Swamps also serve as natural water filters, trapping sediments and absorbing nutrients and pollutants, and they store significant amounts of carbon.

Human use and cultural significance

Historically, swamps were often regarded as wastelands and were drained for agriculture, forestry, and development; a large fraction of the world's swamps has been lost this way. Swamps also feature prominently in folklore and literature as mysterious or dangerous places. In the United States, swamps served as refuges for escaped enslaved people, notably in the Great Dismal Swamp. Today many swamps are protected as wildlife refuges and Ramsar sites, and are valued for ecotourism, fishing, and flood control.
