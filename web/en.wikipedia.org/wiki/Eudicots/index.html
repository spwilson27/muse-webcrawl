Eudicots

Tricolpate pollen is the defining synapomorphy of the eudicots.

The eudicots are a large clade of flowering plants defined principally by the production of tricolpate pollen—pollen grains with three furrows or grooves—an innovation that appeared in the fossil record roughly 125 million years ago in the Early Cretaceous. The name, coined by James A. Doyle and Carol L. Hotton in 1991, denotes the "true dicots", since the older grouping Dicotyledons was shown to be paraphyletic.

Characteristics

Besides tricolpate pollen, eudicots typically have two seed leaves (cotyledons), net-veined leaves, flower parts in fours or fives, and vascular bundles arranged in a ring in the stem—though these features also appear in basal angiosperm groups and were inherited rather than newly evolved.

Classification

In the APG IV system the eudicots include early-diverging orders such as Ranunculales, Proteales, Trochodendrales and Buxales, and the vast core eudicots, which split into the superrosids (including rosids such as legumes, roses and oaks) and superasterids (including asterids such as daisies, mints and tomatoes). Eudicots account for roughly 75% of angiosperm species and include most familiar trees, garden plants and food crops.

Evolution

Molecular clocks and the abrupt appearance of tricolpate pollen in Barremian–Aptian sediments mark the eudicots' rapid Cretaceous radiation, which transformed terrestrial ecosystems and co-evolved with insect pollinators and herbivores.
