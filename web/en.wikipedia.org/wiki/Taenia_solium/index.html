Taenia solium, the pork tapeworm, is a parasitic flatworm (cestode) whose adult stage lives in the human small intestine and whose larval stage develops in pigs — and, aberrantly, in humans. It causes two distinct diseases: taeniasis, the usually mild intestinal infection with the adult worm acquired by eating undercooked pork containing larval cysts, and cysticercosis, the much more serious tissue infection acquired by ingesting the worm's eggs. When cystic larvae develop in the brain (neurocysticercosis), the result is one of the most common parasitic diseases of the central nervous system and a leading cause of acquired epilepsy in the developing world.

Morphology and life cycle

The adult worm is typically 2 to 3 metres long (occasionally up to 8 metres). Its head, or scolex, about 1 mm across, bears four suckers and a double crown of 22–32 hooks — the "armed" scolex that distinguishes it from the beef tapeworm Taenia saginata. Behind the scolex, the body (strobila) consists of up to about 1,000 segments (proglottids), the mature terminal ones filled with eggs and shed in feces.

When pigs ingest eggs or proglottids from human feces, the embryos (oncospheres) hatch, penetrate the gut, and spread through the blood to muscle and other tissues, where each becomes a fluid-filled cyst (cysticercus, or bladder worm) about 1 cm across. Humans eating raw or undercooked "measly pork" swallow the cysts; the scolex evaginates, attaches to the intestinal wall, and grows into an adult in about two months. Humans who instead ingest eggs — through fecally contaminated food, water, or hands, or by autoinfection — develop cysticercosis, with cysts forming in muscle, skin, eye, and brain.

Disease

Intestinal taeniasis is frequently asymptomatic or causes only vague abdominal discomfort. Cysticercosis ranges from silent to severe depending on cyst number and location. Neurocysticercosis commonly presents with seizures, headaches, and hydrocephalus; ocular cysts can cause blindness. Dead or degenerating cysts provoke intense inflammation, which paradoxically causes many of the symptoms. Diagnosis relies on imaging (CT and MRI showing cysts, sometimes with a visible scolex — the "hole-with-dot" sign), serology, and stool examination.

Treatment and control

Taeniasis is treated with praziquantel or niclosamide. Neurocysticercosis is treated with antiparasitics such as albendazole and praziquantel given together with corticosteroids to suppress inflammation, plus antiepileptic drugs; ocular cysts must be removed surgically and never treated medically first. Control depends on sanitation, hand and food hygiene, meat inspection, thorough cooking or freezing of pork, and treatment of tapeworm carriers; a vaccine (TSOL18) exists for pigs. The World Health Organization lists taeniasis/cysticercosis among the neglected tropical diseases targeted for intensified control.
