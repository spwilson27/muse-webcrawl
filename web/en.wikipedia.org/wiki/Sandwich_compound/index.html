Sandwich compound

In organometallic chemistry, a sandwich compound is a chemical compound featuring a metal bound by haptic covalent bonds to two arene (ring) ligands. The arenes have the formula CnHn, substituted derivatives, or heterocyclic analogues. Because the metal is usually situated between the two rings, it is said to be "sandwiched". A special class of sandwich complexes are the metallocenes.

Ferrocene, the prototypical sandwich compound

History

The term was introduced in 1956 by J. D. Dunitz, L. E. Orgel, and A. Rich, who confirmed the structure of ferrocene by X-ray crystallography. Ferrocene itself had been discovered (nearly simultaneously by two groups) in 1951; its remarkable stability and unusual bonding, with an iron atom held between two cyclopentadienyl anions, helped launch modern organometallic chemistry. Geoffrey Wilkinson and Ernst Otto Fischer received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1973 for their work on sandwich compounds.

Classes

Sandwich compounds include the metallocenes (metal between two cyclopentadienyl rings), bis(benzene) complexes such as bis(benzene)chromium, and mixed-ring species. "Half-sandwich" compounds, such as methylcyclopentadienyl manganese tricarbonyl, have only one planar organic ligand and are sometimes called "piano-stool" complexes. Bent sandwich compounds, in which the rings are not parallel, occur when additional ligands occupy the metal's coordination sphere, as in many zirconocenes used as polymerization catalysts.

Applications

Metallocenes and related compounds are important as catalysts — notably in olefin polymerization (e.g., Kaminsky catalysts) — and as stoichiometric reagents and standards in electrochemistry, ferrocene being a common internal reference.
