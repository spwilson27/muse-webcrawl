Helminthiasis is infection with parasitic worms (helminths). It is among the most common infections worldwide and a major group of neglected tropical diseases, disproportionately affecting the poorest communities in sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, and Latin America. Many infections are chronic and light, causing malnutrition, anaemia, stunted growth, and impaired cognition rather than acute illness.

Types

Soil-transmitted helminthiases — ascariasis, trichuriasis, and hookworm infection — affect well over a billion people, transmitted via eggs or larvae in soil contaminated with human faeces. Food- and water-borne infections include schistosomiasis (blood flukes, transmitted by freshwater snails) and tapeworm infections such as taeniasis and cysticercosis. Tissue and vector-borne filarial worms cause lymphatic filariasis (elephantiasis) and onchocerciasis (river blindness).

Diagnosis and control

Diagnosis is usually by microscopic examination of stool or blood for eggs and larvae. Control rests on preventive chemotherapy — periodic mass drug administration of anthelmintics such as albendazole and praziquantel to at-risk populations, especially school-age children — together with improved water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH). Some worms also infect livestock, making helminthiasis a major veterinary and economic problem.
