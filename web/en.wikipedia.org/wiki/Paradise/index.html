Paradise

In religion, paradise is a place of exceptional happiness and delight. Paradisiacal notions are often laden with pastoral imagery, and may be cosmogonical or eschatological, or both, often compared with the miseries of human civilisation: in paradise there is only peace, prosperity, and happiness. Paradise is a place of contentment, a land of luxury and fulfilment, and the opposite of hell.

Etymology

The English word derives from Old Persian pairidaēza, "walled enclosure" (from pairi, "around", and daeza, "wall"), used of the royal parks and pleasure gardens of the Achaemenid kings. It entered Greek as parádeisos through Xenophon's accounts of Persian gardens, and was used in the Septuagint to translate the Garden of Eden.

Abrahamic religions

In the Hebrew Bible the Garden of Eden is humanity's original paradisiacal home. In Judaism, paradise (Gan Eden) also names a part of the afterlife where the righteous rest. In Christianity, Jesus promises the penitent thief, "today you will be with me in Paradise", and Revelation describes a restored paradise with the tree of life. In Islam, paradise is Jannah — often called by names such as Firdaws — described in the Quran as gardens beneath which rivers flow, the reward of the faithful. In Zoroastrianism, from whose language the word comes, the righteous dead reach a house of song and light.

Other traditions and parallels

Analogous abodes include Elysium and the Isles of the Blest in Greek religion, the Pure Land in Mahayana Buddhism, and Svarga in Hinduism. The motif of a lost golden age of ease — also seen in the Greek myth of the ages of man — recurs across cultures.

Figurative use

"Paradise" is widely applied metaphorically to idyllic earthly places ("a tropical paradise") and to states of bliss, and appears in literature from Dante's Paradiso to Milton's Paradise Lost.
