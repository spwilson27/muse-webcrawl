Four Wangs

The Four Wangs were four Chinese landscape painters of the early Qing dynasty, all surnamed Wang, who dominated orthodox landscape painting in the late 17th and early 18th centuries. They were Wang Shimin (1592–1680), Wang Jian (1598–1677), Wang Hui (1632–1717), and Wang Yuanqi (1642–1715). The term was coined by later critics to group them as the leading exponents of the orthodox school of literati painting.

The painters

Wang Shimin and Wang Jian, the two elder Wangs, grounded their styles in the study of ancient masters, especially Dong Qichang, whose theories of landscape painting shaped the orthodox school, and the Yuan dynasty master Huang Gongwang. Wang Hui, a pupil of both elders, synthesized earlier styles into panoramic handscrolls produced under imperial patronage, most famously the monumental series depicting the Kangxi Emperor's southern tours. Wang Yuanqi, Wang Shimin's grandson, served at court and developed an abstract, structural approach to landscape composition.

Style and influence

The Four Wangs emphasized brushwork, the reinterpretation of old masters (fanggu), and the expression of scholarly cultivation over naturalistic observation. Their orthodoxy dominated Qing court and literati taste for generations, though later critics — and modern art historians — have sometimes viewed their influence as conservative, contrasting them with the individualist painters such as Shitao and Bada Shanren.
