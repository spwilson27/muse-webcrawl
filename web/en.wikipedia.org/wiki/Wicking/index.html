Wicking

Wicking
PhenomenonCapillary action in porous materials
ApplicationsCandles, oil lamps, sportswear, heat pipes, plumbing

Wicking is the movement of a liquid through a narrow space or porous material by capillary action, often against gravity. A wick — a strip or cord of absorbent material, traditionally cotton or linen — draws liquid fuel (such as oil, kerosene, or melted wax) up to a flame in candles and lamps. The physics of wicking is governed by surface tension, adhesion between liquid and solid, and the diameter of the pores or fibres.

Applications

Beyond lamps and candles, wicking is exploited in moisture-wicking sportswear fabrics that pull perspiration away from the skin; in heat pipes, where a wick returns condensed working fluid to the evaporator; in fountain pen feeds; in self-watering plant systems; and negatively in building science, where unwanted wicking of groundwater through masonry ("rising damp") must be prevented. In soldering, "wicking" describes molten solder flowing into a joint or along stranded wire.
