On Spirals (Greek: Περὶ ἑλίκων) is a treatise by Archimedes, written around 225 BC and addressed to Dositheus of Pelusium. It defines and studies the curve now called the Archimedean spiral — the locus of a point moving uniformly along a ray while the ray rotates uniformly about its origin.

Contents

Across 28 propositions, Archimedes proves results on the lengths of arcs and, most famously, on areas: the area swept out by the radius vector during the first turn of the spiral equals one third of the area of the circle whose radius is the final radius vector. He also shows how to draw a tangent to the spiral, a result equivalent to rectifying the circle.

Significance

The treatise is regarded as a high point of Greek geometry, anticipating integral calculus in its use of exhaustion arguments, and its tangent construction implicitly contains the idea of combining uniform linear and circular motions — a kinematic definition of a curve unusual in ancient mathematics.
