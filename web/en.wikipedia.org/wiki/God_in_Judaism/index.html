God in Judaism

In Judaism, God has been conceived in a variety of ways, but the religion's foundational conviction is strict monotheism: God is one, indivisible, the creator of the universe and the source of all morality, who entered into a covenant with Abraham and gave the Torah to Israel through Moses. The central prayer, the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4), proclaims "Hear, O Israel: the LORD is our God, the LORD is one." God is traditionally understood as incorporeal, eternal, omnipotent and omniscient, both transcendent and personally involved in history and in the lives of individuals.

Names of God

The most sacred name is the Tetragrammaton, YHWH (יהוה), revealed to Moses at the Burning Bush along with the phrase Ehyeh asher ehyeh ("I am that I am", Exodus 3:14). Out of reverence, Jews traditionally do not pronounce it, substituting Adonai ("my Lord") in prayer and HaShem ("the Name") in ordinary speech. Other names and epithets include Elohim, El Shaddai, Adonai Tzevaot ("Lord of Hosts"), and rabbinic titles such as HaKadosh Baruch Hu ("the Holy One, blessed be He") and Ribbono shel Olam ("Master of the Universe").

Nature and attributes

The Hebrew Bible portrays God as creator, lawgiver, judge and redeemer, who acts in history — in the Exodus from Egypt above all — and who displays the "thirteen attributes of mercy" proclaimed in Exodus 34:6–7. Rabbinic Judaism emphasised the interplay of God's justice (middat ha-din) and mercy (middat ha-rahamim) and the concept of the Shekhinah, God's indwelling presence that accompanies Israel in exile. Medieval Jewish philosophy, above all Maimonides, insisted on God's absolute incorporeality and unity, teaching that positive statements about God's essence are impossible: we can say only what God is not. His Thirteen Principles of Faith became a standard summary of Jewish belief, and his first principles concern God's existence, unity and incorporeality.

Later developments

Kabbalah elaborated a rich theosophy of the infinite Ein Sof and the ten sefirot through which God interacts with the world; Hasidism, from the 18th century, stressed God's immanence and the possibility of joyful communion (devekut) in everyday life. Modern Jewish thought has wrestled with the Holocaust — prompting theologies of a hidden or self-limiting God — and with movements ranging from Mordecai Kaplan's Reconstructionism, which redefined God as "the power that makes for salvation", to process theology and Jewish feminism's re-examination of masculine God-language. Across these differences, the covenant relationship and ethical demand remain central: God is known primarily through the commandments (mitzvot) and the pursuit of justice.
