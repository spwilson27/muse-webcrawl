Lactose

Lactose
IUPAC nameβ-D-galactopyranosyl-(1→4)-D-glucose
FormulaC₁₂H₂₂O₁₁
Molar mass342.30 g/mol
AppearanceWhite solid
TypeDisaccharide (milk sugar)

Lactose is a disaccharide sugar composed of galactose and glucose subunits, with the molecular formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁. It makes up about 2–8% of milk by weight and is often called milk sugar. The name derives from the Latin lac (genitive lactis), "milk", plus the suffix -ose for sugars. Lactose was isolated from whey in 1633 by Fabrizio Bartoletti and characterised as a sugar in the 18th century.

Chemistry and digestion

Lactose is formed by a β(1→4) glycosidic bond between β-D-galactose and D-glucose. In the human small intestine it is hydrolysed by the enzyme lactase (lactase-phlorizin hydrolase), most active in infancy. In most mammals lactase production declines after weaning (lactase non-persistence, the ancestral human state); adults who continue to produce it have lactase persistence, a trait that evolved independently in several dairying populations (notably in Europe, East Africa, the Middle East and South Asia) in one of the best-known examples of gene-culture coevolution. Undigested lactose in adults lacking lactase is fermented by gut bacteria, producing the symptoms of lactose intolerance — bloating, flatulence and diarrhoea — which affects the majority of the world's adults to varying degrees.

Uses

Commercial lactose is produced from whey, a by-product of cheese-making. It is used as a filler and excipient in pharmaceutical tablets, in infant formula, as a mildly sweet bulking agent and browning ingredient in foods, in bacteriological media (MacConkey agar differentiates lactose-fermenting bacteria), and historically in penicillin production. Fermentation of lactose by lactic acid bacteria produces yogurt and other cultured dairy products with reduced lactose content, and lactose-free milks are made by pre-treating milk with lactase.
