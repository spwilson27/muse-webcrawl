Googie architecture

Googie architecture is a type of futurist architecture influenced by car culture, jets, the Atomic Age and the Space Age. It originated in Southern California with the Streamline Moderne architecture of the 1930s, and was popular in the United States from roughly 1945 to the early 1970s. Googie-themed architecture was popular among motels, coffee houses and gas stations. The style later became widely known as part of the mid-century modern style, and the term Googie is also used in the discussion of such design more generally.

Etymology

The term "Googie" comes from Googie's, a West Hollywood coffee shop designed in 1949 by architect John Lautner. The name was a family nickname of Lillian K. Burton, the wife of the restaurant's owner, Mortimer C. Burton. Googie's was located at the corner of Sunset Boulevard and Crescent Heights in Los Angeles. The distinctive Lautner design — a sweeping, upswept roof, glass walls and bold geometric forms — became a prototype for the thousands of coffee shops, diners and roadside businesses that followed.

The term was popularized by architecture critic Douglas Haskell, who used it in a 1952 article in House and Home magazine. Haskell had spotted the style while driving through Los Angeles and is said to have exclaimed to his companion that the flamboyant roadside buildings were an entirely new kind of design, which he dubbed "Googie." Although Haskell used the term somewhat derisively, it stuck and is now used neutrally or even affectionately.

Characteristics

Googie architecture is characterized by features designed to catch the eye of passing motorists and to convey a sense of speed, motion and the future. Common elements include:

  • Upswept roofs and dramatic cantilevers
  • Geometric shapes such as boomerangs, parabolas, and atomic starbursts
  • Bold use of neon and large, theatrical signage integral to the structure
  • Plate glass walls connecting interior and exterior space
  • Space Age motifs: flying saucers, satellites, atoms and rocket shapes
  • Integration with the automobile: buildings designed to be seen from the road at speed, with ample parking

The style is closely related to the broader mid-century modern movement and to Streamline Moderne, but it is distinguished by its exuberant, commercial character and its embrace of thematic and symbolic design.

History

Origins

The roots of Googie lie in the streamlined architecture of the 1930s, built to attract motorists along the new highways. Architects such as Wayne McAllister designed drive-in restaurants in Los Angeles with cylindrical towers, neon and rounded corners. After World War II, returning prosperity, the growth of the suburbs and the explosion of car ownership created a boom in roadside businesses that needed to stand out from competitors.

Peak years

During the 1950s and early 1960s, Googie flourished. The style was applied to coffee shops, bowling alleys, car washes, motels, gas stations and amusement venues. Famous examples include the Theme Building at Los Angeles International Airport (1961), Seattle's Space Needle (1962, built for the World's Fair), the original Bob's Big Boy in Burbank (1949), Norms restaurants, and Pann's coffee shop in Los Angeles. The Las Vegas Strip also adopted Googie and related "casino modern" motifs in its signage and architecture.

Decline

By the late 1960s and early 1970s the style fell from fashion. The oil crisis, rising environmental consciousness, and a shift toward more restrained, corporate architecture made Googie's flamboyance seem dated or garish. Many Googie buildings were demolished or remodeled beyond recognition. Preservation efforts by groups such as the Los Angeles Conservancy have since saved a number of significant examples, and the style has been reassessed as an important expression of postwar American popular culture.

Influence and legacy

Googie's influence can be seen in later roadside and theme architecture, in the signage of Las Vegas, and in retro-styled diners and attractions. The style was famously evoked in the animated television series The Jetsons, and elements of Googie design appear in films and theme parks seeking to evoke the optimistic futurism of the postwar era. Architecture historians now regard Googie as a significant vernacular modernism, reflecting the era's faith in technology, mobility and progress.
