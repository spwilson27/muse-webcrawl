God the Father

God the Father is a title given to God in Christianity, where he is regarded as the first Person of the Trinity, distinct from but one in essence with God the Son (Jesus Christ) and God the Holy Spirit. The title expresses both God's role as creator and sustainer — the "Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth" of the creeds — and his paternal relationship to believers, established through Christ. In the New Testament, Jesus addresses God as "Father" (in Aramaic, Abba) and teaches his disciples to pray "Our Father", the opening of the Lord's Prayer.

Biblical basis

The Hebrew Bible occasionally calls God the father of Israel (e.g. Deuteronomy 32:6; Isaiah 63:16; Malachi 2:10), typically as creator and covenant partner rather than in a literal sense. In the New Testament the fatherhood of God becomes central: Jesus speaks of "my Father" with unique intimacy, promising that believers become children of God by adoption (Romans 8; Galatians 4). Christian doctrine holds that the Son is "begotten, not made", eternally generated from the Father, and that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father (in Eastern theology) or from the Father and the Son (the Western filioque).

Doctrine and theology

The Nicene Creed (325, revised 381) opens "We believe in one God, the Father Almighty", and classical Trinitarian theology assigns to the Father the role of source or fountainhead (fons divinitatis) within the Godhead: unbegotten, he begets the Son and breathes forth the Spirit, while all three persons are co-equal and co-eternal. In the economy of salvation, the Father is depicted as sending the Son into the world and raising him from the dead, and as the one to whom Christ hands over the kingdom. Theologians stress that "Father" is an analogy: God transcends sex and gender, and the tradition also employs maternal imagery (e.g. Isaiah 49:15; Matthew 23:37).

In other faiths and modern discussion

Judaism, from which the metaphor derives, does not regard God the Father as a distinct person of a Trinity. In Islam, calling God "Father" is rejected, since God "neither begets nor is born" (Qur'an 112:3). Within modern Christianity, feminist theologians have debated the exclusively masculine language for God, and some denominations now supplement traditional formulae with inclusive imagery, while others maintain that "Father" is revealed language that may not be set aside.

In art

Early Christian art avoided depicting the Father, showing only a hand emerging from heaven (manus Dei); from the later Middle Ages and Renaissance, influenced by images of the Ancient of Days (Daniel 7), Western art represented him as a majestic elderly man with a white beard, most famously in Michelangelo's The Creation of Adam (1512) on the Sistine Chapel ceiling. Eastern Orthodox tradition remains more restrictive, generally permitting the image only in the form of the "Otechestvo" iconography, whose canonicity has been disputed.
