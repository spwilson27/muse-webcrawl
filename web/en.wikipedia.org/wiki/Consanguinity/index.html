Consanguinity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Consanguinity (from Latin consanguinitas, "blood relationship") is the characteristic of having a kinship relationship with another person by being descended from a common ancestor. Many jurisdictions have laws prohibiting people with certain degrees of consanguinity from marrying or engaging in sexual relations. The degree of consanguinity also determines who may inherit property when a person dies without a will (intestacy).

Degrees of consanguinity

Two traditional methods compute degrees:

  • Civil (Roman) law method: count the steps from one person up to the common ancestor and back down to the other. Siblings are related in the second degree, an uncle and nephew in the third, and first cousins in the fourth.
  • Canon law method: count the generations from the common ancestor down the longer line. Siblings are in the first degree, first cousins in the second.

In genetics, relatedness is quantified by the coefficient of relationship: a parent and child share about 50% of their genes, siblings about 50%, and first cousins about 12.5%. The coefficient of inbreeding of a child equals the coefficient of relationship of its parents divided by two; for the child of first cousins it is 1/16 (6.25%).

Legal and social rules

Prohibited degrees vary widely. Nearly all societies prohibit parent–child and sibling unions. First-cousin marriage is legal in much of Europe, the Middle East, and parts of Asia — and is traditionally preferred in some cultures — but is restricted or banned in a number of US states and in China. The Catholic Church historically forbade marriage to a wide range of kin but has narrowed the prohibition; canon law currently bars marriage between relatives within the fourth degree of the collateral line (first cousins).

Health considerations

Children of consanguineous parents have an elevated risk of autosomal recessive genetic disorders, because both parents are more likely to carry the same harmful recessive allele inherited from a common ancestor. Studies of first-cousin offspring generally find a modest increase in congenital anomalies (on the order of 2–3 percentage points above the background risk). Repeated consanguineous marriage over generations raises the coefficient of inbreeding further, as in the historical Habsburg dynasty, whose last Spanish king, Charles II, had a higher inbreeding coefficient than the child of a brother–sister union.

Prevalence

Consanguineous unions are estimated to account for roughly 10% of marriages worldwide, with the highest rates in North Africa, the Middle East, and parts of South Asia, where cousin marriage has long been customary for social and economic reasons.
