Mutatis mutandis

Mutatis mutandis is a Medieval Latin phrase meaning "with things changed that should be changed" or "once the necessary changes have been made". It remains untranslated in English and is used especially in law, philosophy, economics, and mathematics to indicate that a statement, argument, proof, or comparison applies to another case after the obvious corresponding modifications have been made.

Usage

The phrase signals that a proposition established for one situation holds for another, mutatis mutandis, once names, parameters, orientations, or other incidental features are suitably adjusted. For example, a mathematical proof for functions of a real variable may be said to hold, mutatis mutandis, for functions of a complex variable.

In legal drafting, the phrase allows provisions of one document (such as a contract or judgment) to be applied to another without re-writing every clause, with the reader making the substitutions that context requires, such as names of parties or dates.

Grammar

The phrase is an ablative absolute in Latin: mutatis is the perfect passive participle of mutare ("to change") in the ablative plural, and mutandis is a gerundive ("things to be changed"), giving the sense "the things that needed changing having been changed".
