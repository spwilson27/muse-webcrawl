An antihero (sometimes spelled anti-hero) or antiheroine is a main character in a story who may lack conventional heroic qualities and attributes, such as idealism, courage, and morality. Although antiheroes may sometimes perform actions that most people consider morally correct, their reasons for doing so may not be idealistic; they often act selfishly, out of self-preservation, or for reasons that are morally ambiguous. Antiheroes have become especially prominent in modern literature, film, and television.

Characteristics

Antiheroes typically possess some combination of flaws such as cynicism, greed, dishonesty, cowardice, apathy, or a troubled past, while still occupying the structural position of protagonist and retaining the audience's interest or sympathy. Unlike a villain protagonist, an antihero usually is not committed to evil ends; unlike the classic hero, they doubt, stumble, and may fail. Scholars sometimes distinguish "classical" antiheroes — passive, ineffectual figures swept along by events — from modern antiheroes who actively pursue goals by morally questionable means.

History

The type has ancient roots: the trickster and the picaresque rogue of early modern Spanish fiction, and Miguel de Cervantes' Don Quixote (1605), a deluded would-be knight, are frequently cited early examples. Hamlet, indecisive and tormented, is another candidate. The Byronic hero of the nineteenth century added brooding charisma and a dark past. In the twentieth century, hardboiled detectives, film noir protagonists, existentialist narrators such as Meursault in Camus' The Stranger, and J. D. Salinger's Holden Caulfield carried the archetype into the mainstream. Later television dramas centred on criminal antiheroes such as Tony Soprano and Walter White made the figure a defining feature of prestige TV.
