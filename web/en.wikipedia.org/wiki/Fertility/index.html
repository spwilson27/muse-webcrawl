Fertility

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fertility is the ability to conceive a child; in demography it refers to the actual production of offspring, as distinct from fecundity, the biological potential for reproduction. The standard measure is the total fertility rate (TFR): the average number of children a woman would bear over her lifetime if she experienced current age-specific fertility rates. A TFR of about 2.1 children per woman is the replacement level in low-mortality societies — the rate at which a population exactly replaces itself from one generation to the next.

Global patterns

World fertility has fallen dramatically since the 1960s, when the global TFR was about 5 children per woman; by the early 2020s it had fallen to roughly 2.3 and continues to decline. The highest rates are found in sub-Saharan Africa — Niger has recorded rates near 7 — while much of Europe and East Asia is far below replacement: South Korea's TFR fell below 0.8 in the early 2020s, the lowest ever measured in a large country. Demographers describe this long-run decline, accompanying falling mortality, urbanization, and rising education, as the demographic transition.

Determinants

Fertility is shaped by both biology and social behaviour. Proximate determinants include age at marriage or union formation, contraceptive use, abortion, postpartum infecundability from breastfeeding, and the frequency of intercourse, as formalized in the Davis–Blake and Bongaarts frameworks. Behind these lie socioeconomic factors: women's education (the strongest and most consistent correlate of lower fertility), household income and the opportunity cost of children, child survival rates, urbanization, housing costs, cultural and religious norms, and government policy such as parental leave, childcare provision, and — in a few historical cases — coercive limits like China's former one-child policy.

Biology

Human female fertility peaks in the twenties and declines with age, slowly after about age 30 and more steeply after 35, as the quantity and quality of oocytes diminish; menopause, at a median age near 51, ends natural fertility. Male fertility also declines with age, though more gradually, through reduced sperm quality. About one couple in six worldwide experiences infertility at some point, for which treatments range from ovulation induction and intrauterine insemination to in vitro fertilization (IVF), first successful in 1978.

Consequences of low and high fertility

Sustained below-replacement fertility leads to population ageing and eventually population decline, raising concerns about labour supply, pension sustainability, and economic growth, and prompting pro-natalist policies in many countries — with historically modest effects. High fertility, conversely, strains schooling, health services, and employment creation in developing economies but produces a youthful age structure. Fertility is also a central variable in population projections issued by the United Nations, under which world population is expected to peak later this century.
