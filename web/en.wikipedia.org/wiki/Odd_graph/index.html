Odd graph

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In graph theory, the odd graphs are a family of symmetric graphs with high odd girth, defined and named by Manfred Bornhöft and studied extensively since the 1970s. The odd graph On has one vertex for each of the (n − 1)-element subsets of a (2n − 1)-element set; two vertices are adjacent when the corresponding subsets are disjoint.

Examples

O2 is the triangle graph K3. O3 is the famous Petersen graph. O4 has 35 vertices and 50 edges and can be drawn as the generalised Petersen graph GP(7,2) with additional structure; odd graphs for n ≥ 3 are triangle-free and have girth that grows with n (in fact their odd girth equals 2n − 1, which motivated the name).

Properties

The odd graph On is n-regular, vertex-transitive and edge-transitive, with a number of vertices equal to the binomial coefficient C(2n−1, n−1). The odd graphs are not Hamiltonian in general for large n — determining which odd graphs have Hamiltonian cycles was a longstanding problem related to the Lovász conjecture on Hamiltonian paths in vertex-transitive graphs; it is now known that O3, O4 and O5-type small cases behave differently and that large odd graphs can fail to be Hamiltonian. Odd graphs have been used as models for interconnection networks because of their symmetry and large girth.

Relation to Kneser graphs

Odd graphs are a special case of the Kneser graphs: On is the Kneser graph KG(2n−1, n−1). They are also related to the folded cube graphs and appear in the study of the chromatic number of Kneser graphs (the subject of Lovász's celebrated topological proof of the Kneser conjecture).
