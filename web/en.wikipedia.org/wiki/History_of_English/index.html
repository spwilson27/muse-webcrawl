History of English

The history of English traces the development of the English language from Germanic dialects brought to Britain in the 5th century AD to today's global lingua franca. It is conventionally divided into Old English, Middle English, Early Modern English and Late Modern English.

History of English
Origin: West Germanic, Angles/Saxons/Jutes
Periods: Old (450–1100), Middle (1100–1500), Early Modern (1500–1700), Late Modern (1700– )
Key events: Norman Conquest, Great Vowel Shift, printing

Old English

Anglo-Saxon settlers spoke inflected Germanic dialects; Old English had four main dialects (Northumbrian, Mercian, Kentish, West Saxon), grammatical gender, case endings, and a largely Germanic vocabulary, later borrowing from Old Norse during the Danelaw. Literature includes Beowulf and Alfred the Great's translations.

Middle English

The Norman Conquest (1066) made French the elite language for 300 years, adding some 10,000 French words and simplifying English inflections. English re-emerged in the 14th century in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales (London/East Midland dialect) and Wycliffe's Bible.

Early Modern English

Caxton's printing press (1476), the Renaissance influx of Latin/Greek words, the Great Vowel Shift (c. 1400–1700) reshaping long vowels, Shakespeare and the King James Bible (1611) fixed and enriched the language. Grammar standardised, thou gave way to you, and the progressive and do-support spread.

Late Modern and global English

Dictionaries (Johnson 1755), prescriptive grammars, industrial vocabulary, and British and American imperial and media expansion spread English worldwide, creating World Englishes — American, Indian, Nigerian, Australian and many others — and making English the most widely spoken language overall.
