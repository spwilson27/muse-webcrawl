Extensive farming or extensive agriculture (as opposed to intensive farming) is an agricultural production system that uses small inputs of labour, fertilizers, and capital, relative to the land area being farmed. It is most commonly practised where land is abundant and cheap relative to labour and capital, and includes large-scale sheep and cattle ranching, dryland wheat farming, and shifting cultivation.

Characteristics

Extensive systems produce low yields per hectare but can achieve high output per worker. Stocking densities are low, animals often range over large areas of natural or semi-natural pasture, and crop fields may lie fallow for long periods to restore soil moisture and fertility. Because inputs are thinly spread, profitability depends heavily on land prices, commodity prices and, in arid regions, rainfall.

Examples

  • Cattle ranching in the western United States, Argentina and Australia.
  • Sheep stations in Australia and New Zealand.
  • Nomadic and semi-nomadic pastoralism, such as reindeer herding in Eurasia.
  • Dryland grain farming on the Canadian Prairies and in Kazakhstan.
  • Shifting (slash-and-burn) cultivation in tropical regions.

Environmental aspects

Low-input extensive grazing can maintain species-rich grasslands and deliver ecosystem services, but overgrazing can cause erosion and desertification, and conversion of forest to extensive pasture is a major driver of deforestation in the Amazon basin.
