An open cluster (also called a galactic cluster) is a group of up to a few thousand stars that formed from the same giant molecular cloud and have roughly the same age. They are found in the discs and spiral arms of galaxies, where star formation continues, and are only loosely bound by mutual gravitation — most are disrupted by gravitational encounters within a few hundred million years, making long-lived open clusters relatively rare.

Formation and evolution

Open clusters form within molecular clouds that collapse into stars nearly simultaneously, so members share a common age and initial chemical composition. The cluster's most massive stars exhaust their fuel quickly and explode as supernovae, while interactions with giant molecular clouds and tidal forces from the galaxy gradually strip stars away — a process called evaporation. Stars that remain together for more than a billion years are exceptional; one famous old example is NGC 188 (about 7 billion years old).

Scientific importance

Because all members of a cluster formed together, open clusters are ideal laboratories for studying stellar evolution: the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram of a cluster shows stars of identical age at every stage of evolution, allowing precise tests of stellar models. They are also used as distance indicators — the technique of main-sequence fitting calibrates cosmic distances — and as tracers of Galactic structure and chemical enrichment.

Notable examples

  • The Pleiades (M45) — the brightest open cluster in the sky, in Taurus, about 444 light-years away.
  • The Hyades — the nearest open cluster (~153 light-years), forming the face of Taurus.
  • Praesepe (M44), the Beehive Cluster — one of the nearest clusters, known since antiquity.
  • The Double Cluster (h and χ Persei) — a spectacular pair of young clusters in Perseus.
