Hepatitis C virus

The hepatitis C virus (HCV) is a small, enveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus of the family Flaviviridae. It is the cause of hepatitis C and of some cancers, principally hepatocellular carcinoma and lymphomas. The virus can establish chronic infection in the majority of people it infects.

Taxonomy and structure

HCV is the type species of the genus Hepacivirus. Its virion is roughly 55–65 nm in diameter, with a lipid envelope studded with the glycoproteins E1 and E2 surrounding a nucleocapsid containing the ~9.6 kb RNA genome. At least seven major genotypes are recognised, differing in geographic distribution and response to treatment.

Genome and replication

The genome contains a single open reading frame encoding a polyprotein that is cleaved into structural proteins (core, E1, E2) and non-structural proteins (p7, NS2, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, NS5A and the NS5B RNA-dependent RNA polymerase). The virus replicates in hepatocytes in association with intracellular membranes, forming a "membranous web".

Discovery and treatment

HCV was identified in 1989 as the agent of most cases of non-A, non-B hepatitis; Harvey Alter, Michael Houghton and Charles M. Rice received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for its discovery. Since 2013, direct-acting antivirals such as sofosbuvir have cured more than 95% of infections. No vaccine exists, owing largely to the virus's extreme variability.
