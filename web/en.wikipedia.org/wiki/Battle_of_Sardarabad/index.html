The Battle of Sardarabad (Armenian: Սարդարապատի ճակատամարտ) was a battle of the Caucasus campaign of the First World War, fought near Sardarabad, Armenia, from 21 to 29 May 1918 between Armenian forces and the Ottoman army. The Ottoman offensive aimed to crush the remaining Armenian resistance in the Caucasus, occupy Alexandropol (Gyumri) and Echmiadzin, and advance on Yerevan. The Armenian victory at Sardarabad halted the Ottoman advance and is widely credited with preserving the Armenian people from complete destruction and enabling the proclamation of the First Republic of Armenia days later.

Background

Following the Russian Revolution and the collapse of the Russian Caucasus front, Ottoman forces advanced into territories vacated by Russian troops in early 1918, retaking Erzurum, Van, and other positions despite the armistice of Erzincan. Hundreds of thousands of Armenian refugees had fled eastward after the genocide of 1915, and by May 1918 the Ottoman Third Army, under Wehib Pasha, was pressing toward Yerevan and the Ararat plain. The Armenian National Council in Tiflis scraped together a force of regular units, volunteers, and refugee militiamen under the overall command of General Tovmas Nazarbekian, with General Movses Silikyan commanding the Yerevan detachment.

The battle

On 21 May, Ottoman forces took Sardarabad's railway station and pushed toward Echmiadzin, seat of the Armenian Church, whose Catholicos ordered the church bells rung to summon the population to arms; clergy, teachers, and peasants joined the defence. Silikyan, initially ordered to retreat, instead counter-attacked. Over several days of fighting — supported by Armenian victories at Bash Abaran to the north and a stand at Karakilisa — the Armenian forces drove the Ottomans back, inflicting heavy losses and recapturing Sardarabad and its station by 29 May. The Ottoman army retreated in disorder toward Alexandropol.

Aftermath

On 28 May 1918, while the battle was still being fought, the Armenian National Council proclaimed the independence of the Republic of Armenia in Tiflis. The Treaty of Batum, signed on 4 June under Ottoman pressure, left the new republic a small, landlocked territory, but the survival of an Armenian state at all is commonly attributed to the victories of May 1918, Sardarabad foremost among them. The battle holds a central place in Armenian national memory; a monumental memorial complex designed by architect Rafael Israelyan was erected at the site and inaugurated in 1968, on the battle's fiftieth anniversary.
