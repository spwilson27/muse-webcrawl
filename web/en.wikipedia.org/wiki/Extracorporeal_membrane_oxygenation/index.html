Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation

An ECMO circuit providing temporary heart–lung support.

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), also known as extracorporeal life support (ECLS), is a technique of providing prolonged cardiac and respiratory support to people whose heart and lungs are unable to provide an adequate amount of oxygen, gas exchange, or perfusion to sustain life. Blood is withdrawn from the body, passed through a pump and a membrane oxygenator that adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide, and returned to the circulation.

Configurations

In veno-venous (VV) ECMO, blood is returned to the venous system and only gas exchange is supported, for severe respiratory failure such as ARDS. In veno-arterial (VA) ECMO, blood is returned to the arterial system, supporting both heart and lungs, for cardiogenic shock or cardiac arrest (extracorporeal CPR). ECMO is a temporary support — typically days to a few weeks — used as a bridge to recovery, to surgery or transplantation, or to a decision about further care.

History and evidence

The technology descends from the heart–lung machine developed for open-heart surgery in the 1950s; the first successful prolonged ECMO treatment was reported by J. Donald Hill in 1971–72, and Robert Bartlett pioneered its use in newborns with respiratory failure. Trials such as CESAR (2009) and EOLIA (2018), and experience during the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic and the COVID-19 pandemic, established ECMO as a rescue therapy for carefully selected patients at specialised centres, coordinated internationally through the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO).

Risks

Complications include bleeding (anticoagulation is required), clotting within the circuit, infection, stroke, and limb ischaemia at cannulation sites. ECMO is resource-intensive and raises significant ethical questions about patient selection and futility.
