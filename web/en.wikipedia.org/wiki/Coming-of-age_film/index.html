A coming-of-age film is a genre of film that focuses on the psychological and moral growth of a protagonist from youth to adulthood. The transition commonly involves personal identity, first experiences of love and loss, conflict with parents or authority, and the discovery of one's place in society. The genre overlaps with the literary Bildungsroman tradition.

History

Early examples include Jean Vigo's Zéro de conduite (1933) and Satyajit Ray's Apu Trilogy (1955–1959). Nicholas Ray's Rebel Without a Cause (1955), starring James Dean, became an archetype of teenage alienation. François Truffaut's The 400 Blows (1959) launched the French New Wave with its semi-autobiographical portrait of Antoine Doinel.

The genre flourished in the 1980s with the films of John Hughes, including The Breakfast Club (1985) and Ferris Bueller's Day Off (1986). Later landmarks include Stand by Me (1986), Boyhood (2014) — filmed over twelve years with the same cast — Moonlight (2016), winner of the Academy Award for Best Picture, Lady Bird (2017), and Call Me by Your Name (2017).

Characteristics

Coming-of-age films typically feature a young protagonist, a pivotal summer or school year, rites of passage such as first romance or the death of a loved one, and a narrative structured around the loss of childhood innocence. Dialogue often explores themes of friendship, sexuality, and rebellion against parental values.
