The Inca Empire, called Tawantinsuyu ("the four regions together") in Quechua, was the largest empire in pre-Columbian America. Arising from the Peruvian highlands in the early 13th century around the city of Cusco, it expanded explosively after 1438 under the emperor Pachacuti and his successors, eventually stretching along the Andes and Pacific coast from modern Colombia to central Chile and Argentina, governing perhaps 10 million people speaking many languages.

Society and engineering

The empire was a tightly organised state without money, markets on the European model, wheeled transport, or (in the usual sense) writing: labour tax (mit'a) built roads, terraces, fortresses and cities, and records were kept on knotted cords (quipu). Its ~40,000 km road network crossed some of the world's most extreme terrain — the Andes — with rope suspension bridges and way stations. Inca stonework, fitted without mortar so precisely that a blade cannot enter the joints, survives at Sacsayhuamán and Machu Picchu, the royal estate rediscovered by the outside world in 1911. Agriculture used terraces, freeze-dried potatoes (chuño) and state storehouses to buffer the famines of a vertical landscape.

Conquest

European diseases, especially smallpox, raced ahead of the Spanish, killing the emperor Huayna Capac and triggering a civil war between his sons Atahualpa and Huáscar. Francisco Pizarro, with fewer than 200 Spaniards, captured Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532, ransomed him for a room of gold, and executed him in 1533; Cusco fell soon after, though Inca resistance held out at Vilcabamba until 1572. The conquest transferred the Andes' silver — above all Potosí — into the world economy and devastated the Indigenous population, while Quechua survived as a major living language.
