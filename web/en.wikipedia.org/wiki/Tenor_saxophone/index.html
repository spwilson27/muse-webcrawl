The tenor saxophone is a medium-sized member of the saxophone family, a group of instruments invented by Adolphe Sax in the 1840s. The tenor and the alto are the two most commonly used saxophones. The tenor is pitched in the key of B♭, and written as a transposing instrument sounding a major ninth lower than written.

Characteristics

The tenor saxophone uses a larger mouthpiece and reed than the alto, producing a mellower, richer tone. It is distinguished from the alto by a small bell flare and a curved "gooseneck" at the top. Its written range typically runs from B♭ below middle C to F or F♯ above the staff, though advanced players extend this with the altissimo register.

History and repertoire

Originally developed for military bands, the saxophone found its defining role in jazz, where the tenor became the genre's emblematic solo instrument. Coleman Hawkins established the tenor as a serious solo voice in the 1920s and 1930s; Lester Young developed a contrasting light, lyrical style. In the bebop and hard bop eras, players such as Sonny Rollins, John Coltrane and Stan Getz expanded the instrument's expressive range, and Coltrane's work in particular made the tenor central to modern jazz. The tenor is also ubiquitous in rhythm and blues, rock and roll, and soul horn sections.
