Yat

Yat (Ѣ, ѣ) is the thirty-second letter of the old Cyrillic alphabet. It represented a vowel sound inherited from Proto-Slavic, commonly transliterated as ě. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, yat remained part of the orthographies of Russian, Bulgarian, and Rusyn, although its original pronunciation had long since merged with other vowels in most dialects.

History

In early Slavic, yat represented a distinct vowel, probably a long open [æː] or diphthong. Over time its pronunciation merged: in Russian it came to be pronounced like the letter е, and in Ukrainian like і in many positions. Because the sound had merged, generations of Russian schoolchildren had to memorise by rote which words were written with yat and which with е — a notorious difficulty of pre-reform spelling.

Orthographic reforms

The Russian orthography reform of 1918 abolished yat (along with і, ѳ, and the final hard sign) as part of the Bolsheviks' spelling simplification, though the change had been proposed by linguists decades earlier. Bulgarian retained yat until 1945, where it marked a real dialectal boundary — the famous "yat border" dividing eastern and western Bulgarian dialects. Today yat survives in Church Slavonic texts and is occasionally revived for stylistic or historical effect in signage and branding.
