Grade separation

[Image placeholder: Highway interchange, a common form of grade separation]

Grade separation is a method of aligning a junction of two or more surface transport axes at different heights (grades) so that they will not disrupt the traffic flow on other routes when they cross each other. The composition of such transport axes may be roadways, railways, canals, airfield runways, or pedestrian paths. Bridges (or overpasses, also called flyovers), tunnels (or underpasses), or a combination of both can be built at a junction to achieve the needed grade separation.

Roads

On roads, grade separation is achieved at interchanges, where ramps connect roadways at different levels and traffic passes from one to another without stopping. Fully grade-separated roads with no at-grade intersections are freeways or motorways. Grade separation removes conflict points, increasing both capacity and safety, but at considerable construction cost and land take.

Railways

For railways, grade separation eliminates level crossings, where roads cross tracks at the same level. Modern railway construction, particularly for high-speed lines, is entirely grade-separated, using bridges, viaducts, tunnels, and flying junctions — in which one railway line crosses another on a bridge — to avoid conflicting train movements.

Pedestrians and cyclists

Footbridges and pedestrian tunnels separate people on foot from vehicular traffic. Such structures are common around railway stations, busy urban roads, and airports, though they are sometimes criticised for accessibility and personal-security drawbacks compared with at-grade crossings.
