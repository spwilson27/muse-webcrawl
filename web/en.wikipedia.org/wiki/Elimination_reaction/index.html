Elimination reaction


The E2 elimination mechanism

An elimination reaction is a type of organic reaction in which two substituents are removed from a molecule in either a one- or two-step mechanism. The one-step mechanism is known as the E2 reaction; the two-step mechanism is known as the E1 reaction. (The numbers refer not to the number of steps but to the kinetics of the reaction: E2 is bimolecular, second-order; E1 is unimolecular, first-order.) Elimination contrasts with substitution reactions, with which it often competes, and with addition reactions, of which it is the reverse.

Common examples

In most organic eliminations, a proton is removed from a carbon adjacent to the carbon bearing a leaving group (such as a halide or a hydroxyl group), forming a carbon–carbon double bond — an alkene. The dehydration of alcohols under acidic conditions and the dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halides by strong base are textbook examples.

Regiochemistry

When more than one alkene can form, the more substituted, more stable alkene usually predominates, a rule known as Zaitsev's rule; bulky bases can favour the less substituted product, the Hofmann product. Stereochemistry matters too: E2 eliminations proceed most readily when the departing groups are anti-periplanar, and that geometry determines which alkene isomer is produced.

Related reactions

Variants include the E1cB mechanism, in which a carbanion intermediate forms first; eliminations producing alkynes from dihalides; and thermal syn-eliminations such as the Cope and Chugaev eliminations, used to prepare alkenes under mild conditions.
