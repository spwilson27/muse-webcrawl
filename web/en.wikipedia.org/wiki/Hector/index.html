In Greek mythology, Hector (Ancient Greek: Ἕκτωρ) is a Trojan prince, the eldest son of King Priam and Queen Hecuba, and the husband of Andromache. He is the greatest warrior and chief defender of Troy in the Trojan War, and one of the central figures of Homer's Iliad.

In the Iliad

Hector commands the Trojan forces and is the only Trojan hero who matches the Greek champions. The poem's most human scene is his farewell in Book 6 to Andromache and their infant son Astyanax, who is frightened by his father's plumed helmet. When the war resumes, Hector kills Patroclus, who is wearing Achilles' armour — an act that draws Achilles back into battle. In Book 22, Hector waits alone outside the walls, flees three times around the city, and is deceived by Athena into making a stand; Achilles kills him, and Hector dies prophesying Achilles' own death.

Aftermath

Achilles drags Hector's body behind his chariot and refuses burial, but in the Iliad's final book Priam ransoms the corpse, and Troy mourns Hector with full funeral honours. Later tradition adds that after Troy's fall his son Astyanax was thrown from the walls and Andromache enslaved.

Reception

Hector has been admired since antiquity as the most sympathetic figure of the Iliad — a devoted husband, father, and citizen who fights not for glory but to protect his city, knowing it is doomed. In the medieval Nine Worthies he was counted the first of the three pagan exemplars of chivalry, alongside Alexander the Great and Julius Caesar, and his name became a byword for dutiful courage (and, by an ironic English corruption, "to hector" came to mean to bully).
