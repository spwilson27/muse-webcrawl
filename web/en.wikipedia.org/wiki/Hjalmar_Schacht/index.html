Hjalmar Horace Greeley Schacht (22 January 1877 – 3 June 1970) was a German economist, banker, centre-right politician, and co-founder of the German Democratic Party. He served as Currency Commissioner and President of the Reichsbank under the Weimar Republic. He was a fierce critic of his country's post-World War I reparations obligations. He was also central in helping to create the Rentenmark, a currency that helped end the hyperinflation of 1923.

Schacht served in Adolf Hitler's government as President of the Reichsbank (1933–1939) and Minister of Economics (August 1934 – November 1937). While Schacht was for a time a positive figure who helped clear some of the huge debts Germany owed following the First World War and the Great Depression, his role would become increasingly marginalised and he became critical of the German re-armament programme, which led conflicts with Hitler and other prominent Nazis. He was arrested and interned in concentration camps in 1944 after the 20 July plot.

Early life

Schacht was born in Tingleff, Schleswig-Holstein (now Tinglev, Denmark) to William Leonid Schacht, a postal and insurance official, and Baroness Constanze Justine Sophie von Eggers, who was of Danish origin. His parents had spent a number of years in the United States, and his middle names, Horace Greeley, were in honor of the American journalist and editor of the New-York Tribune. He studied medicine, philology, and political science before settling on economics, receiving his doctorate from the University of Kiel in 1899.

Weimar Republic

After the First World War, Schacht worked at the Dresdner Bank, rising to a position on its board. In November 1923, Chancellor Gustav Stresemann appointed him currency commissioner; within weeks he became president of the Reichsbank. He played a central role in ending the hyperinflation crisis through the introduction of the Rentenmark and participated in negotiations that led to the Dawes Plan of 1924 and the Young Plan of 1929, both of which restructured Germany's reparations payments.

Nazi era

Schacht supported Hitler's appointment as Chancellor in 1933 and was reappointed to the Reichsbank presidency. As Minister of Economics he devised the "New Plan" for trade and used MEFO bills—bills of exchange guaranteed by the state—to finance rearmament off-budget. Growing disputes with Hitler over the scale and speed of rearmament spending, and clashes with Hermann Göring over control of economic policy, led to his resignation as economics minister in 1937 and dismissal from the Reichsbank in 1939.

Arrest, trial, and later life

Schacht had loose contacts with resistance circles and was arrested after the failed 20 July 1944 plot to assassinate Hitler; he spent the remainder of the war in concentration camps, including Ravensbrück, Flossenbürg, and Dachau. At the Nuremberg trials he was acquitted by the International Military Tribunal in 1946. A German denazification court later sentenced him to eight years in a labour camp, but he was acquitted on appeal in 1948. He subsequently worked as a financial adviser to developing countries and founded a private bank in Düsseldorf. He died in Munich in 1970.
