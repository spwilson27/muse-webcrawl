Holy Grail

The Holy Grail (French: Saint Graal, Breton: Graal Santel) is a treasure that serves as an important motif in Arthurian literature. Different traditions describe it as a cup, dish, or stone with miraculous healing powers, providing eternal youth, happiness or nourishment. By the later tradition, it is the cup used by Jesus at the Last Supper and/or used by Joseph of Arimathea to collect Jesus's blood at the Crucifixion.

Holy Grail
First appearance: Chrétien de Troyes, Perceval, c. 1180s
Associated knights: Perceval, Galahad, Bors
Possible relics: Valencia Chalice, Genoese Sacro Catino

Origins and literature

The Grail first appears in Chrétien de Troyes's unfinished romance Perceval, the Story of the Grail (c. 1181–1190) as a mysterious graal — a serving vessel — carried in procession at the Fisher King's castle. Robert de Boron gave it Christian meaning as the vessel of the Last Supper and Crucifixion. Wolfram von Eschenbach's Parzival made it a stone (lapis exillis). In the Vulgate Cycle and Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, the pure knight Galahad achieves the Grail Quest with Perceval and Bors.

Theories and relics

Scholars link the Grail to Celtic cauldrons of plenty, Byzantine relic culture and Eucharistic devotion. Churches claim Grail chalices, most famously Valencia Cathedral's agate cup, recognised for papal use, and the Sacro Catino in Genoa. No claim is historically verifiable.

Modern culture

The Grail endures in Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975), Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade (1989), The Da Vinci Code (which reimagined it as a bloodline), Wagner's Parsifal, and the idiom "holy grail" for any ultimate, elusive goal — as in high fantasy quests that descend from this tradition.
