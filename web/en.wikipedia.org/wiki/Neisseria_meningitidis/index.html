Neisseria meningitidis

Neisseria meningitidis, often referred to as meningococcus, is a Gram-negative bacterium that can cause meningitis and other forms of meningococcal disease such as meningococcemia, a life-threatening sepsis. It is a major cause of illness and death in young children in both developed and developing countries.

Biology

The meningococcus is a round, oxidase-positive diplococcus that colonises the human nasopharynx; humans are its only host. Asymptomatic carriage is common — roughly one in ten people carry the bacterium harmlessly — while invasive disease is rare. Thirteen serogroups are recognised based on the polysaccharide capsule, of which A, B, C, W, X and Y cause most disease.

Disease and prevention

Meningococcal disease can progress with frightening speed, causing bacterial meningitis and septicaemia with a characteristic non-blanching petechial rash; even with treatment, case fatality is around 10–15%. Vaccines are available against several serogroups, including conjugate vaccines for A, C, W and Y and protein-based vaccines for serogroup B. Epidemics of serogroup A disease historically swept the "meningitis belt" of sub-Saharan Africa, where mass vaccination campaigns have since greatly reduced incidence.
