Waitomo is a rural community in the King Country region of the North Island of New Zealand, best known for the extensive karst cave system beneath the surrounding hills — above all the Waitomo Glowworm Caves, one of New Zealand's most famous tourist attractions. Its name comes from the Māori words wai, "water", and tomo, "hole" or "shaft".

The caves

The caves were formed in Oligocene limestone over millions of years by groundwater dissolution. The glowworm cave follows the subterranean Waitomo Stream and is celebrated for its population of Arachnocampa luminosa, a fungus gnat species endemic to New Zealand whose larvae produce blue-green bioluminescent threads that hang from the cave ceiling to trap prey, creating a "living ceiling" likened to a starry sky. Visitors tour the cave on foot and by boat along the underground river.

The cave was known to local Māori for generations; it was explored by chief Tane Tinorau together with English surveyor Fred Mace in 1887, and Tinorau and his wife Huti began guiding visitors soon after. The Crown took over administration in 1906; in 1989 the cave and surrounding land were returned to the descendants of the original owners under a Treaty of Waitangi settlement arrangement, with the site managed in partnership and leased for tourism.

Neighbouring caves in the Waitomo system include Ruakuri Cave, famed for its spiral entrance and glowworms, Aranui Cave, known for its stalactites and stalagmites, and the enormous Gardnergut and Mangapohue systems used by adventure tourists for black-water rafting (cave tubing) and abseiling.

The district

Waitomo District, administered from Te Kūiti, covers some 3,500 square kilometres of mostly sheep and beef farmland; the village of Waitomo Caves itself has only a few hundred residents but hosts hundreds of thousands of visitors annually.
