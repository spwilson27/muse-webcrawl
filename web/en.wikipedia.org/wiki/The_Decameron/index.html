The Decameron (Italian: Decameron) is a collection of short stories by the 14th-century Italian author Giovanni Boccaccio. The book is structured as a frame story containing 100 tales told by a group of seven young women and three young men — the brigata — who shelter in a secluded villa outside Florence to escape the Black Death, which rages through the city in 1348.

Structure and themes

Over ten days, each member of the company tells one story per day, yielding one hundred tales. The stories range from the erotic and farcical to the tragic, and they satirise the clergy, celebrate wit and resourcefulness, and explore love, fortune, and human folly. A king or queen chosen each day sets the theme for most days' tales.

Influence

The Decameron is a landmark of Italian prose and of the novella form, influencing writers including Chaucer, whose Canterbury Tales adopts a similar frame, as well as Shakespeare and many later authors. Boccaccio's vivid account of the plague in Florence remains a key historical source.
