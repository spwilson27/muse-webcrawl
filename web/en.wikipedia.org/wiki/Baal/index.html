Baal

Baal (also rendered Baʿal) was a title meaning "lord" or "owner" applied to various gods in the ancient Levant, and pre-eminently the name of the storm and fertility god Hadad, worshipped by the Canaanites and Phoenicians. As the bringer of rain, Baal was essential to agriculture in the region, and his cult is a prominent adversary of Yahweh worship in the Hebrew Bible.

Mythology

The main source for Baal's mythology is the Baal Cycle, a set of Ugaritic clay tablets from the 14th–13th centuries BCE. The cycle narrates Baal's conflict with Yam, the god of the sea, his construction of a palace on Mount Zaphon, and his descent into the underworld in struggle with Mot, the god of death and drought, followed by his return — a myth often read as reflecting the seasonal cycle of rains and dry summers.

In the Hebrew Bible

In the Bible, Baal worship is repeatedly condemned: the prophet Elijah's contest with the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18) is the most famous episode, and kings such as Ahab and Jezebel are criticised for promoting the cult. Local manifestations were worshipped under compound names such as Baal-Peor and Baal-Zebub (the origin, via later demonology, of "Beelzebub").
