PSR B1913+16 (also known as PSR J1915+1606) is a pulsar in a binary system with another neutron star, in the constellation Aquila. It was discovered in 1974 by Russell Alan Hulse and Joseph Hooton Taylor Jr. using the Arecibo Observatory, and was the first binary pulsar found.

Discovery and significance

Hulse and Taylor observed that the pulsar's period varied regularly, indicating that it orbits a companion star with an orbital period of about 7.75 hours. Careful timing showed that the orbit is decaying: the two neutron stars spiral slowly toward each other at exactly the rate predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity for a system losing energy by emitting gravitational waves. This provided the first indirect evidence for the existence of gravitational waves and a stringent test of general relativity.

For this discovery, Hulse and Taylor were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1993. The system is also known as the Hulse–Taylor binary pulsar. Over decades of observation, the measured orbital decay has matched the general relativity prediction to within a fraction of a percent, and the periastron advance of the orbit (about 4.2 degrees per year) was also measured.

Properties

The pulsar rotates about 17 times per second (period 59 milliseconds) and has a mass of approximately 1.44 solar masses; its unseen companion, also a neutron star, has a mass of about 1.39 solar masses. The two stars will eventually merge in roughly 300 million years.
