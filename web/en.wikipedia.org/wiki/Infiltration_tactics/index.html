Infiltration tactics

Infiltration tactics are a form of military tactics in which small, lightly equipped infantry units penetrate enemy lines and outposts, bypassing strong points and striking rear areas, headquarters, artillery positions and supply lines. Rather than assaulting the enemy's strongest defences head-on, infiltrating units seek gaps and weak spots, slip through them in darkness, fog or broken terrain, and create confusion behind the front.

The tactics were developed to a high degree during the First World War, most famously by the German Sturmtruppen (stormtroopers) in 1917–1918, building on earlier experiments by officers such as Willy Rohr and on French and British raiding practice. In the German spring offensives of 1918, stormtrooper units armed with light machine guns, grenades, flamethrowers and trench mortars advanced rapidly through British and French lines, leaving pockets of resistance to be reduced by follow-on infantry.

Principles

The core principles of infiltration include surprise, speed and decentralised initiative. Assault detachments were trained to keep moving, never to stop and consolidate on an intermediate objective, and to exploit success without waiting for orders. Supporting artillery fired a short, intense hurricane bombardment rather than a long preparatory one, preserving surprise. Communication with the rear was often sacrificed in favour of momentum.

Later development

Between the wars the concept influenced the development of armoured breakthrough doctrine and, in the Second World War, the deep operations of motorised forces. Modern special forces, light infantry and airborne units continue to employ infiltration by land, sea and air, and the term is also used for the clandestine insertion of agents or guerrillas across a frontier.
