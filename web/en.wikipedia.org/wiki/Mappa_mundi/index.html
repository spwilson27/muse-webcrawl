Mappa mundi (Latin: "cloth of the world") is a general term for medieval European maps of the world. Around 1,100 mappae mundi are known to have survived from the Middle Ages, ranging from small schematic diagrams in manuscripts to enormous wall maps. The most famous surviving example is the Hereford Mappa Mundi (c. 1300), drawn on a single sheet of vellum about 158 cm by 133 cm.

Purpose

Mappae mundi were not intended as navigational aids. They were didactic and theological images of the world, combining geography with biblical history, classical legend, and natural marvels. Places from scripture — the Garden of Eden, the Red Sea, the lands of Gog and Magog — appear alongside cities and peoples, and the world's history from Creation to the Last Judgment is laid out in space as well as time.

T-O maps

The simplest and most influential scheme is the T-O map, derived from the description of the world in Isidore of Seville's Etymologiae. A circle (the O) representing the world ocean encloses a T formed by the Mediterranean Sea, the Nile, and the Don, dividing the earth into the three known continents: Asia (the largest, at the top, since maps were oriented toward the east), Europe, and Africa. Each continent was traditionally assigned to the descendants of one of Noah's sons — Shem, Japheth, and Ham.

Complex maps

Larger mappae mundi elaborated this framework with hundreds of place-names, pictures of biblical scenes, mythical creatures, and monstrous races at the edges of the world. Jerusalem is frequently placed at or near the centre of the Hereford map, and Paradise appears at the very top as a walled island. The lost Ebstorf Map (c. 1234), destroyed in the Second World War, was the largest of all, about 3.6 metres across, and depicted the world as the body of Christ.

Decline

From the later fifteenth century, the recovery of Ptolemy's Geography, the portolan charts of the Mediterranean, and the voyages of discovery displaced the theological world-picture, and the mappa mundi tradition faded, though its iconography influenced early modern maps for some time.
