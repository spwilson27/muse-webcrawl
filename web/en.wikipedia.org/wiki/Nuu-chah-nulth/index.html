Nuu-chah-nulth

The Nuu-chah-nulth (also spelled Nuučaan̓uł) are Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast whose traditional territories lie along the west coast of Vancouver Island and part of the Olympic Peninsula in present-day Washington State, in Canada and the United States. They were formerly known to Europeans as the Nootka, a name now considered outdated.

RegionsWest coast of Vancouver Island, British Columbia; Olympic Peninsula, Washington
LanguagesNuu-chah-nulth, English
Related groupsDitidaht, Makah, other Wakashan peoples

Culture

Nuu-chah-nulth society was organised around hereditary chiefs (ha'wiih), ranked families, and a rich ceremonial life centred on the potlatch. Whaling held a place of special prestige: chiefs' whaling crews pursued grey and humpback whales from large cedar canoes using harpoons with detachable heads and sealskin floats. Salmon, halibut, herring, and shellfish formed the dietary base, supplemented by cedar bark, berries, and roots. Monumental house posts, totemic crests, and woven cedar-bark regalia expressed family histories and privileges.

History

Contact with Europeans began in 1778, when Captain James Cook arrived at Nootka Sound, ushering in the maritime fur trade in sea otter pelts. The Nootka Crisis of 1789 nearly brought Britain and Spain to war over trading rights in the region. Colonisation, disease, and residential schools severely disrupted Nuu-chah-nulth communities in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Modern era

Today the Nuu-chah-nulth Tribal Council represents fourteen member First Nations in British Columbia. Nuu-chah-nulth nations have asserted Aboriginal rights and title in Canadian courts, notably over commercial fishing rights, and several have entered modern treaty arrangements such as the Maa-nulth Final Agreement, which came into effect in 2011.
