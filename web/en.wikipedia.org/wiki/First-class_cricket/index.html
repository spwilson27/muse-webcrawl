First-class cricket

First-class cricket Format: two innings per side, typically 3–5 days
Highest governing body: International Cricket Council (ICC)
Principal domestic competitions: County Championship (England), Ranji Trophy (India), Sheffield Shield (Australia)
Status defined by: ICC classification of matches

First-class cricket, along with List A cricket and Twenty20 cricket, is one of the highest-standard forms of cricket. A first-class match is of three or more days' scheduled duration between two sides of eleven players each and is officially adjudged to be worthy of the status by virtue of the standard of the competing teams. Matches must allow for each side to bat twice, although in practice the game may end early or be drawn.

Definition and history

The term entered use in the nineteenth century but was not formally defined until the Imperial Cricket Conference meeting of 1947, which defined first-class matches retrospectively in some cases. Test matches are automatically first-class; major domestic competitions such as England's County Championship (officially founded 1890), India's Ranji Trophy (1934), and Australia's Sheffield Shield (1892) form the backbone of the first-class game.

Playing conditions

Matches are played with a red ball in most countries (with pink-ball day/night fixtures also used), in whites, and with a minimum number of overs per day. The follow-on, declarations, and time-limited finishes distinguish first-class cricket from limited-overs formats, and drawn games remain possible.

Statistics

First-class records, including batting averages and wicket totals, are a major measure of a cricketer's career; W. G. Grace, Jack Hobbs, and Donald Bradman rank among the most prominent first-class batsmen, while Wilfred Rhodes took a record 4,204 first-class wickets.
