Twentieth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Twentieth Amendment to the United States Constitution establishes some of the basic rules governing the start and end of the terms of federal officials, and addresses what happens when there is no president-elect. It was ratified on January 23, 1933.

Provisions

Section 1 moved the beginning of presidential and vice-presidential terms from March 4 to noon on January 20, and of congressional terms to January 3, shortening the "lame duck" period in which defeated officials remained in office. Other sections provide that if the president-elect dies before taking office, the vice president-elect becomes president, and authorise Congress to legislate for cases in which neither a president-elect nor a vice president-elect has qualified.

Background

In the 18th and 19th centuries the four-month gap between election and inauguration reflected slow travel and communication, but by the 20th century it had become a liability — most acutely during the secession crisis of 1860–61 and the banking crisis winter of 1932–33. The amendment is therefore often called the "Lame Duck Amendment".
