Hiranyakashipu

Hiranyakashipu (Sanskrit: हिरण्यकशिपु, "clad in gold") is a powerful asura (demon) king in Hindu mythology, best known from the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, the Viṣṇu Purāṇa and the Mahābhārata as the tyrannical father of the devotee Prahlada and the adversary destroyed by Vishnu in his man-lion avatar, Narasimha.

The boon

To avenge his brother Hiranyaksha, whom Vishnu had slain in the form of the boar Varaha, Hiranyakashipu performed severe austerities and obtained from Brahma a boon designed to make him invulnerable: he could not be killed by man or beast, by day or by night, inside or outside, on earth or in the sky, by any weapon, nor by any being created by Brahma. Empowered by it, he conquered the three worlds and demanded that all worship be directed to himself alone.

Prahlada and the coming of Narasimha

His young son Prahlada, however, remained devoted to Vishnu. Enraged, Hiranyakashipu subjected the boy to repeated attempts on his life — poison, elephants, serpents, fire (the episode of his sister Holika, remembered in the festival of Holi) — all of which failed. When the king mockingly demanded whether Vishnu was present even in a pillar of the palace and struck it, Vishnu emerged from it as Narasimha, half man and half lion — neither man nor beast. At twilight, neither day nor night, on the threshold of the hall, neither inside nor outside, he placed the demon across his lap, neither earth nor sky, and disembowelled him with his claws, which were no weapon — thus honouring every condition of the boon while ending the tyranny.

Legacy

The story is a central devotional narrative of Vaishnavism, retold in regional literatures, dance-drama (such as Kerala's Prahlada Nataka traditions) and temple sculpture, and it is the mythic background to the spring festival of Holi.
