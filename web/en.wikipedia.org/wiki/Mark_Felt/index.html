William Mark Felt Sr. (August 17, 1913 – December 18, 2008) was an American law enforcement officer who worked for the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) from 1942 to 1973 and is best known for his role in the Watergate scandal. Felt was the associate deputy director, the FBI's second-highest-ranking post, when he leaked information to The Washington Post reporters Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein under the name "Deep Throat".

Career

Felt rose through the FBI under J. Edgar Hoover, specialising in counter-espionage. After Hoover's death in May 1972, Felt became the bureau's number two under acting director L. Patrick Gray — and was angered when President Richard Nixon passed him over for the directorship in favour of Gray, an outsider. Within the FBI, Felt supervised aspects of the Watergate investigation and saw the White House obstructing it.

Deep Throat

Woodward had known Felt from earlier reporting. In secret late-night meetings in a Rosslyn parking garage, Felt confirmed leads and told the reporters the scandal reached to the highest levels of government. His identity was kept secret for 33 years — known to Woodward, Bernstein and editor Ben Bradlee alone — until his family persuaded him to come forward: on May 31, 2005, Vanity Fair revealed that Felt was Deep Throat, and the reporters confirmed it.

Felt's motives remain debated: institutional defence of the FBI, pique at being passed over, and genuine alarm at White House abuses have all been cited. In a separate controversy, Felt was convicted in 1980 of authorising illegal break-ins ("black bag jobs") at the homes of associates of the radical Weather Underground during the early 1970s; he was pardoned by President Ronald Reagan in 1981.

Death

Felt died in his sleep of congestive heart failure at a hospice in Santa Rosa, California, on 18 December 2008.
