Outline of algebraic structures

Algebraic structures
FieldAbstract algebra; universal algebra
ObjectsSets equipped with operations satisfying axioms
Key examplesGroups, rings, fields, vector spaces, lattices

In abstract algebra, an algebraic structure is a set (the carrier or underlying set) equipped with one or more finitary operations—such as addition and multiplication—satisfying specified axioms. This outline surveys the principal types of algebraic structure, organised by the operations they carry and the axioms imposed on them. The comparative study of these structures is the subject of universal algebra.

Structures with one binary operation

  • Magma — a set with a single closed binary operation, no axioms.
  • Semigroup — a magma whose operation is associative.
  • Monoid — a semigroup with an identity element.
  • Group — a monoid in which every element has an inverse; a group with a commutative operation is an abelian group.
  • Quasigroup and loop — structures where division is always possible, without requiring associativity (a loop adds an identity).

Structures with two operations

  • Ring — an abelian group under addition with an associative multiplication distributing over it; adding multiplicative identity gives a ring with unity, and commutativity of multiplication gives a commutative ring.
  • Integral domain — a commutative ring with no zero divisors; a field is a ring in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.
  • Vector space — an abelian group of vectors acted on by scalars from a field; generalisations include modules over rings and algebras over a field.

Order and lattice structures

  • Lattice — a partially ordered set (or algebraic structure with meet and join operations) in which every pair of elements has a greatest lower bound and least upper bound.
  • Boolean algebra — a complemented distributive lattice, the algebra of logic.
  • Ordered groups, rings and fields — algebraic structures carrying a compatible total or partial order.

Study of structures

Algebraists study these structures through structure-preserving maps (homomorphisms), substructures, quotient structures and products, and through universal constructions. Category theory provides a unifying language, and results such as the isomorphism theorems apply across many varieties of algebra at once.
