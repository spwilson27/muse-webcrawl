Space warfare

Space warfare is combat in which one or more belligerents are situated in outer space. The scope of space warfare includes ground-to-space warfare, such as attacking satellites from the Earth, as well as space-to-space warfare, such as satellites attacking satellites. It does not include the use of space systems for enabling conventional warfare on Earth (such as satellite navigation and communications) — although disabling an adversary's such systems is a recognised space warfare mission.

History

Military interest in space began with the Cold War. Both the United States and the Soviet Union developed anti-satellite (ASAT) weapons from the 1960s onward; the USSR tested co-orbital interceptor satellites, and in 1985 a US F-15 destroyed the Solwind satellite with an air-launched missile. Concern over the vulnerability of satellites and the debris created by kinetic attacks grew after China's destruction of the FengYun-1C satellite in 2007, which produced thousands of tracked debris fragments, and India's Mission Shakti test in 2019.

Modern developments

Modern space warfare emphasises "soft-kill" methods — jamming, spoofing, dazzling sensors and cyber attacks — alongside directed-energy and kinetic weapons. The United States established the Space Force as a separate service branch in 2019, China organised its space forces within the PLA, and Russia maintains its Russian Space Forces. Critics of kinetic ASAT use cite the Kessler syndrome: cascades of collisions that could render orbits unusable.

Law

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty prohibits weapons of mass destruction in orbit and military installations on celestial bodies, but does not ban conventional weapons in space or military satellites. Debates continue over norms against debris-generating ASAT tests, which several states pledged to forgo beginning in 2022.
