Nymphaeum

A nymphaeum (Ancient Greek: νυμφαῖον nymphaion) was originally a monument consecrated to the nymphs, especially the nymphs of springs. In the Roman world the term came to denote an elaborate monumental fountain, often a tall, columned façade with niches, statues, and cascading water, serving both as a sanctuary and as a grand terminus for an aqueduct.


The nymphaeum at Jerash (Gerasa), Jordan, built in the late 2nd century AD.

Development

Early Greek nymphaea were natural grottoes or rock-cut shrines at springs. Under Rome, especially in the eastern provinces, they evolved into monumental public architecture: multi-storey scaenae-frons-like structures faced with marble, such as the nymphaea at Jerash, Side, Ephesus, and Aspendos, and the great Septizodium in Rome. In private villas and gardens, smaller nymphaea served as cool, water-filled grottoes for dining and retreat, a tradition revived in Renaissance and Baroque gardens — for example the nymphaeum of the Villa Giulia in Rome.
