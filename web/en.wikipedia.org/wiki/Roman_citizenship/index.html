Roman citizenship

Roman citizenship (civitas Romana) was a privileged political and legal status granted to free individuals in the ancient Roman state. It conferred rights such as the vote in assemblies, eligibility for public office and certain magistracies, legal marriage (conubium), property and contract rights under Roman law (commercium), and protection from certain punishments, including the right of appeal (provocatio).

Acquisition

Citizenship was normally inherited from a citizen father, but it could also be granted by the state to individuals and whole communities, earned through military service in the auxilia (rewarded with a bronze diploma at discharge), or acquired by a slave formally manumitted by a citizen master. Rome was unusually generous with citizenship by ancient standards, using it as a tool of integration and expansion.

Gradations of status

Below full citizens stood holders of Latin rights (ius Latii), free provincials (peregrini), freedmen (liberti), and slaves, who had no legal personality. Women could be citizens but could not vote or hold office.

Universal grant

The Social War (91–88 BC) forced Rome to extend citizenship to its Italian allies. In AD 212 the emperor Caracalla issued the Constitutio Antoniniana, granting citizenship to virtually all free inhabitants of the empire, completing a centuries-long process by which a city-state's status became universal. The apostle Paul famously invoked his citizenship to claim legal protections, declaring civis Romanus sum — "I am a Roman citizen."
