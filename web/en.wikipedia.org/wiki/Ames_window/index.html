Ames window

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ames window is an optical illusion first devised by American scientist Adelbert Ames Jr. in 1947. It consists of a trapezoid-shaped frame, painted to resemble a rectangular window viewed obliquely, mounted so that it rotates continuously on a vertical axis.

The illusion

To a stationary viewer, the rotating trapezoid appears not to rotate through a full 360 degrees, but instead to oscillate back and forth through less than 180 degrees. The visual system assumes the shape is a rectangle seen in perspective, and interprets the changing retinal image as a rectangular window swinging rather than a trapezoid turning. The effect is stronger under monocular viewing and when a rod or pen is passed through one of the "window" openings, an object that seems to bend or pass impossibly through the frame.

Explanation

The illusion demonstrates the dominance of learned assumptions about shape and perspective over raw sensory information: because windows are normally rectangular, the brain prefers an interpretation involving a rectangle changing orientation in depth over a trapezoid rotating. It is closely related to the Ames room, a distorted chamber used to create size illusions.
