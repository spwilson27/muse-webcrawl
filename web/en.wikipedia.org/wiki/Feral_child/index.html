Feral child

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A feral child (also called a "wild child") is a young human who has lived away from normal human contact from a very young age, with little or no experience of human care, language, or social behaviour. Such children have been reported — and often mythologised — throughout history, and their study bears on debates about language acquisition and the critical period in development.

Documented and alleged cases

The best-documented historical case is Victor of Aveyron, a boy found in the forests of southern France around 1800 and studied by the physician Jean Marc Gaspard Itard, whose patient educational efforts achieved only partial success; the case inspired François Truffaut's film L'Enfant sauvage (1970). Kaspar Hauser, who appeared in Nuremberg in 1828, remains an enigma. The famous Indian "wolf girls" Amala and Kamala (1920s) are now regarded with scepticism. In the modern era, cases such as "Genie", a Los Angeles girl rescued in 1970 after extreme isolation and abuse, are better understood as cases of severe neglect and confinement rather than life among animals — a distinction that applies to most reported "feral" children.

Scientific significance

Feral and isolated children have been taken as natural experiments on the role of early experience: their struggles to acquire language after puberty are cited in support of a critical or sensitive period for first-language learning, though pre-existing cognitive impairments make firm conclusions difficult. Myths of children raised by wolves, bears, or apes — from Romulus and Remus to Kipling's Mowgli and Burroughs's Tarzan — far outnumber verified cases and reveal more about cultural ideas of nature and civilisation than about child development.
