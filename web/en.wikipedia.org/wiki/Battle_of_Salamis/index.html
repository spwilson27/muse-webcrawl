Battle of Salamis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Greek triremes engaging Persian ships in the straits of Salamis, modern reconstruction painting]

The Battle of Salamis was a naval battle fought in September 480 BC between an alliance of Greek city-states under Themistocles and the Persian Empire under King Xerxes I, in the straits between the island of Salamis and the Attic mainland. Despite being outnumbered, the Greek fleet — some 370 triremes against perhaps 600–800 Persian ships — lured the enemy into the confined waters where Persian numbers and heavier vessels counted for little, and destroyed much of the fleet. The victory, with Plataea the following year, turned the tide of the Greco-Persian Wars and preserved Greek independence.

The battle

After Thermopylae and the evacuation of Athens, the allied fleet lay at Salamis while the Greek commanders quarrelled about retreating to the Isthmus. Herodotus tells how Themistocles, by a secret message (via his servant Sicinnus) claiming the Greeks were about to flee, tricked Xerxes into sealing the straits and attacking at dawn in the narrows. In the confined channel the lighter, handier Greek triremes rammed and boarded at will; Aeschylus, who fought in the battle, described the sea choked with wreckage in his tragedy The Persians. The Corinthian contingent's role — feigned flight or flanking attack — was already disputed in antiquity.

Significance

Xerxes, his fleet crippled and supply lines by sea untenable, withdrew to Asia with much of his army; the force left under Mardonius was destroyed at Plataea in 479 BC. Salamis became, with Marathon, the emblematic victory of free Greeks over despotism in Western memory — a reading modern historians treat with care, but the battle's strategic decisiveness is not in doubt: it saved the Greek coalition's cause at the moment of its greatest danger.
