Edward Drinker Cope (July 28, 1840 – April 12, 1897) was an American zoologist, paleontologist, comparative anatomist, herpetologist, and ichthyologist. He published over 1,400 scientific papers during his career and described more than 1,000 vertebrate species, including nearly 300 genera of fishes, amphibians, reptiles, and mammals. A founder of the Neo-Lamarckian school of thought in American biology, Cope is chiefly remembered today for his bitter rivalry with Othniel Charles Marsh, known as the Bone Wars.

Early life

Cope was born into a wealthy Quaker family in Philadelphia. A precocious naturalist, he published his first scientific paper at nineteen and studied anatomy at the University of Pennsylvania, though he never took a university degree. He travelled in Europe studying museum collections, then taught zoology at Haverford College from 1864 to 1867 before turning to field exploration in the American West.

The Bone Wars

Beginning in the 1870s, Cope and Yale paleontologist Othniel Charles Marsh competed ferociously to discover and name new dinosaur fossils in the western territories. The feud — marked by rushed publications, accusations of plagiarism and fossil theft, and the destruction of specimens — produced a flood of discoveries: between them the two men named more than 130 dinosaur species, including Camarasaurus, Allosaurus, Stegosaurus, and Triceratops. Cope's haste famously led him to reconstruct the plesiosaur Elasmosaurus with its head on the wrong end of the spine, an error Marsh gleefully publicised. The rivalry ruined Cope financially and consumed his inherited fortune.

Scientific ideas

Cope proposed "Cope's rule," the observation that lineages tend to increase in body size over evolutionary time. He championed kinetogenesis, a Neo-Lamarckian theory that mechanical forces during life directed evolution. He served as professor of geology and paleontology at the University of Pennsylvania from 1889 until his death in 1897. His enormous private fossil collection was purchased after his death by the American Museum of Natural History.
