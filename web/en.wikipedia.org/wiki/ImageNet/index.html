ImageNet

ImageNet is a large visual database designed for use in visual object recognition software research. As of 2016, over 14 million images had been hand-annotated by the project to indicate what objects are pictured, and in at least one million of the images, bounding boxes are also provided. ImageNet contains more than 20,000 categories, with a typical category such as "balloon" or "strawberry" consisting of several hundred images.

The database of annotations of third-party image URLs is freely available directly from ImageNet, though the actual images are not owned by ImageNet. The project was led by computer scientist Fei-Fei Li and colleagues, and was first presented in 2009. The images were organised according to the WordNet hierarchy of noun synsets, and annotated with the help of Amazon Mechanical Turk workers.

ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge

Since 2010, the ImageNet project has run an annual software contest, the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC), where software programs compete to correctly classify and detect objects and scenes. The challenge uses a trimmed list of one thousand non-overlapping classes.

The 2012 competition was won by AlexNet, a deep convolutional neural network by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton, which achieved a top-5 error rate of 15.3%, dramatically better than the runner-up. This result is widely credited with igniting the modern deep learning boom in computer vision. Subsequent winners, including ZFNet, GoogLeNet, ResNet and SENet, drove error rates below human-level performance on the classification task; ResNet's 2015 winning ensemble achieved a top-5 error rate of about 3.57%.

Significance and criticism

ImageNet became a standard benchmark and a source of pre-trained models for transfer learning. It has also drawn scrutiny over biases in its categories, labels derived from WordNet that include outdated or offensive person categories, and privacy concerns about images of people; in 2019 the project revised its person categories, and in 2021 it began blurring faces in the dataset.
