Fan culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fan culture, or fandom, refers to the shared practices, creative output, social organisation, and identity of communities of fans devoted to a cultural object — a book or film franchise, television series, musical artist, sports team, game, or celebrity. Fans do not merely consume media; they discuss, analyse, extend, and remix it, forming durable subcultures with their own norms, jargon, and hierarchies.

History

Organised fandom emerged in the early 20th century around science fiction: the first science fiction fanzines appeared in the 1930s, and fans founded clubs, conventions, and awards such as the Hugo Awards. Sherlock Holmes devotees of the same era, who treated the detective as real and wrote scholarly "Great Game" essays, are often cited as an early proto-fandom. Media fandom expanded in the 1960s–70s around Star Trek, whose fans produced zines and conventions in large numbers.

Practices

Characteristic fan activities include writing fan fiction, creating fan art and fan videos, cosplay, role-playing games, filk music, collecting, trivia expertise, and maintaining wikis and archives such as Fanlore. Fans organise through conventions, mailing lists, forums, and social media platforms. Scholars such as Henry Jenkins have characterised fans as "textual poachers" who appropriate and rework media texts, and have studied fans' growing influence over producers through campaigns, streaming metrics, and social media.

Communities and identity

Fandoms develop internal distinctions between casual and "hardcore" fans, gift economies in which creative works circulate without payment, and norms around crediting and spoiler etiquette. Fan identity can be a significant part of personal and social identity, and fan communities have been studied both as participatory cultures and as marketing targets in the attention economy.
