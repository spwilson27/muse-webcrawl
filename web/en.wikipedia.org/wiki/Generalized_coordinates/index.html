Generalized coordinates

In analytical mechanics, generalized coordinates are a set of parameters used to represent the configuration of a system relative to some reference configuration. The generalized coordinates are usually selected to be as few as possible and to incorporate the constraints of the system, so that the number of coordinates equals the number of degrees of freedom. They need not be lengths or angles in the ordinary sense — any independent quantities that uniquely specify the configuration may serve.

A double pendulum is naturally described by two angles as generalized coordinates

Examples

For a bead sliding on a wire, a single coordinate along the wire suffices. For a simple pendulum, the angle from the vertical fully specifies the position. A rigid body in space requires six generalized coordinates: three for the position of its center of mass and three (such as Euler angles) for its orientation. The polar coordinates (r, θ) of a particle moving in a plane under a central force are another standard example.

Generalized velocities and forces

The time derivatives of the generalized coordinates are called generalized velocities. By the principle of virtual work, one defines generalized forces such that their product with virtual displacements of the coordinates gives the work done by applied forces. In these variables, the dynamics of the system take the form of Lagrange's equations, which are typically far simpler to derive than force-balance equations when constraints are present, since constraint forces do no virtual work in suitable coordinates.

Holonomic constraints

Coordinates that absorb all constraints are possible when the constraints are holonomic — expressible as equations relating the coordinates (and possibly time). For nonholonomic systems, such as a rolling disk, generalized coordinates can still be used, but the number of coordinates then exceeds the number of degrees of freedom and additional methods (such as Lagrange multipliers) are needed.
