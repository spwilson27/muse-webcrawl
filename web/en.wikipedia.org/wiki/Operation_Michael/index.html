Operation Michael

Date: 21 March – 5 April 1918

Location: Picardy, France

Part of: Spring Offensive, Western Front, First World War

Belligerents: German Empire vs. British Empire, France

Result: German tactical gains; strategic failure

Operation Michael was a major German military offensive during the First World War that began the Spring Offensive (Kaiserschlacht) on the Western Front on 21 March 1918. It was launched from the Hindenburg Line in the vicinity of Saint-Quentin, France. The objective was to break through the Allied lines, split the British and French armies, and roll up the British line along the Somme before American forces could arrive in strength.

Background

The collapse of Russia in 1917 and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk allowed Germany to transfer dozens of divisions to the west. General Erich Ludendorff planned a series of offensives to win the war before American manpower became decisive. The attack employed stormtrooper infiltration tactics, developed under commanders such as Oskar von Hutier and refined by artillery chief Colonel Georg Bruchmüller, whose short, intense bombardment — mixing gas and high explosive — preceded the infantry assault.

The offensive

On 21 March, following a five-hour bombardment of over a million shells, some 74 German divisions attacked the British Fifth and Third Armies in thick fog. The British Fifth Army under Hubert Gough, holding a long, thinly defended sector recently taken over from the French, was overwhelmed; German troops advanced up to 40 miles (64 km) in the deepest penetration of the Western Front since 1914, capturing ground that included the old Somme battlefields and approaching Amiens, a vital rail hub. Amiens held, and the crisis prompted the Doullens Conference (26 March), which appointed Ferdinand Foch to coordinate the Allied defence.

Outcome

The offensive was called off on 5 April. German forces had captured about 1,200 square miles of territory and taken some 75,000 prisoners, but had failed in their strategic aim: the Allies were not split, Amiens and the Channel ports remained in Allied hands, and the salient created was difficult to supply and defend. German casualties, around 239,000, included many irreplaceable elite stormtroops. Ludendorff followed with further offensives (Georgette, Blücher–Yorck, Gneisenau, and Marne), each of which gained ground without decisive result, exhausting Germany's reserves before the Allied Hundred Days Offensive.
