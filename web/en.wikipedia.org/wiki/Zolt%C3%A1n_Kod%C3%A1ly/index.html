Zoltán Kodály (16 December 1882 – 6 March 1967) was a Hungarian composer, ethnomusicologist, pedagogue, linguist and philosopher. He is internationally known as the creator of the Kodály method of music education and, together with Béla Bartók, as one of the foundational figures of ethnomusicology in Hungary.

Life and music

Kodály studied composition at the Budapest Academy of Music, where he later taught for much of his life. From 1905 he travelled through Hungarian villages collecting folk songs on phonograph cylinders, work that paralleled and often complemented Bartók's fieldwork. His compositions draw deeply on Hungarian folk music; major works include the opera Háry János (1926) and its orchestral suite, the Psalmus Hungaricus (1923), the orchestral dances of Galánta and Marosszék, the Peacock Variations, and the Concerto for Orchestra, along with a large body of choral music.

Music education

Kodály believed that music education should begin with singing and with the folk music of the child's own culture. The approach later called the Kodály method uses movable-do solfège, rhythm syllables and hand signs to build musical literacy from an early age. His educational ideas transformed music teaching in Hungary and were adopted internationally after his death.

Legacy

Kodály received numerous honours, including the Kossuth Prize, and served as president of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences. The Kodály Institute in Kecskemét, his birthplace, continues to train music educators from around the world.
