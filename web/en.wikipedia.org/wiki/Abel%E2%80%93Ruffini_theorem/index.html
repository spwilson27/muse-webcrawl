Abel–Ruffini theorem

The Abel–Ruffini theorem (also called Abel's impossibility theorem) states that there is no solution in radicals to general polynomial equations of degree five or higher with arbitrary coefficients — that is, no formula using only arithmetic operations and root extractions that solves every quintic equation, as the quadratic, cubic, and quartic formulas do for lower degrees.

FieldAlgebra
ProvedPaolo Ruffini (partial, 1799); Niels Henrik Abel (1824)
RelatedGalois theory; solvable groups

History

After formulas were found for degrees three and four in the 16th century, mathematicians sought one for the quintic for nearly 300 years. Paolo Ruffini published a substantially correct but incomplete proof of impossibility in 1799. Niels Henrik Abel gave the first accepted proof in 1824, at the age of 21. Évariste Galois soon supplied the deeper criterion: a polynomial is solvable by radicals exactly when its Galois group is a solvable group, which explains both the theorem and which special quintics can still be solved.

Clarifications

The theorem does not say that quintic equations have no solutions — every quintic has complex roots by the fundamental theorem of algebra — nor that particular quintics cannot be solved in radicals (x5 − 2 = 0 is trivially solvable). Quintics can be solved using more powerful tools such as Bring radicals or elliptic modular functions, and numerically to arbitrary precision.
