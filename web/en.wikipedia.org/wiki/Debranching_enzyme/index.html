Debranching enzyme

SubstratesGlycogen, amylopectin
ActivitiesTransferase and glucosidase
Human geneAGL
DeficiencyGlycogen storage disease type III

A debranching enzyme is an enzyme that breaks down the branch points of glycogen and amylopectin during glycogenolysis. In animals, a single protein (encoded by the AGL gene) carries two catalytic activities: a transferase that moves a short chain of glucose units from a branch to the main chain, and an amylo-α-1,6-glucosidase that hydrolyses the remaining single glucose at the branch point.

Mechanism

Glycogen phosphorylase degrades glycogen from its outer ends but stops four residues from a branch point. The debranching enzyme's transferase activity relocates three of those residues to another chain, exposing the α(1→6) bond, which its glucosidase activity then cleaves, releasing free glucose and allowing phosphorylase to continue.

Clinical significance

Deficiency of the enzyme causes glycogen storage disease type III (Cori disease), in which abnormally branched glycogen (limit dextrin) accumulates in the liver and, in the common subtype IIIa, in muscle, causing hepatomegaly, hypoglycaemia, growth retardation and progressive myopathy.
