Muhammad Abduh (Arabic: محمد عبده; 1849 – 11 July 1905) was an Egyptian Islamic scholar, jurist, and religious reformer, regarded as a founder of Islamic modernism. He served as Grand Mufti of Egypt from 1899 until his death and is sometimes called "al-Ustadh al-Imam" ("the teacher and leader").

Life

Abduh was born in the Nile Delta and educated at al-Azhar University in Cairo, where he came under the influence of the itinerant reformer Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, who introduced him to philosophy, science, and political activism. After supporting the 'Urabi revolt against British intervention, Abduh was exiled in 1882; in Paris he joined al-Afghani in publishing the influential journal Al-Urwah al-Wuthqa ("The Firmest Bond").

Returning to Egypt in 1888, Abduh became a judge and then Grand Mufti. He worked to reform al-Azhar's curriculum, modernise the sharia courts, and reconcile Islam with reason and modern science, arguing that Islam properly understood was compatible with rational inquiry and social progress. His theological work Risalat al-Tawhid ("The Theology of Unity") and his unfinished Qur'an commentary, continued by his disciple Rashid Rida in Tafsir al-Manar, shaped generations of Muslim reformers.

Legacy

Abduh's ideas influenced reform movements across the Muslim world, secular nationalists, and later Salafi thinkers alike, though different successors drew sharply different conclusions from his call to return to the practice of the earliest generations of Islam (al-salaf) while exercising independent reasoning (ijtihad).
