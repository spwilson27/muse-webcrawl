Future Shock is a 1970 book by the American writer and futurist Alvin Toffler, written with his wife Adelaide Farrell. It defines the term "future shock" as a psychological state of individuals and whole societies: "the shattering stress and disorientation that we induce in individuals by subjecting them to too much change in too short a time". The book became an international bestseller, selling millions of copies and bringing its title phrase into common usage.

Content

Toffler argued that the accelerating rate of technological and social change overwhelms people, producing stress and disorientation analogous to culture shock. He examined the impermanence of modern life — disposable goods, transient relationships, frequent moves, modular organisations — and predicted developments including electronic cottages (working from home), cloning, throwaway products, and an "adhocracy" replacing rigid bureaucracy. He proposed new institutions, such as a "Council of the Future", to manage the pace of change.

Reception and influence

The book was widely translated and discussed, and its central term entered everyday language. Orson Welles narrated a documentary film adaptation in 1972. Toffler expanded the framework in The Third Wave (1980) and Powershift (1990). Critics praised the book's provocative synthesis while disputing some predictions; its core notion of information overload and change fatigue remains frequently invoked in discussions of digital life.
