Income inequality

[Image: a contrast between luxury high-rise apartments and informal housing in the same city]

Income inequality is the extent to which income is distributed unevenly among a population. It is measured by the Gini coefficient (0 for perfect equality, 1 — or 100 — for maximal inequality), by income shares of the top 1% or 10%, and by ratios such as the Palma index (top 10% to bottom 40%).

Patterns and causes

Within-country inequality fell in much of the West from the 1940s to the 1970s and rose thereafter — sharply in the US and UK, moderately elsewhere — while global inequality fell as China and India grew (the 'elephant curve' of Milanovic). Proposed causes include skill-biased technological change, Globalization, declining union density and minimum wages, superstar and financial-sector pay, and tax-and-transfer policy, which reduces inequality far more in Europe than in the US. Thomas Piketty's Capital in the Twenty-First Century (2014) argued that when the return on capital (r) exceeds growth (g), wealth and income concentrate. Economists debate inequality's effects on growth, health and politics; governments respond through progressive Taxation, social spending and labour-market rules — the distribution branch of Public finance.
