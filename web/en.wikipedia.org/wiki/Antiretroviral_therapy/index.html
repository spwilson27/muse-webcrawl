Antiretroviral therapy

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) is the use of a combination of antiretroviral drugs to treat infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Standard therapy consists of drugs from at least two classes that suppress viral replication so effectively that the virus becomes undetectable in blood by standard assays. ART does not cure HIV — viral DNA persists in latent reservoirs — but taken for life it prevents disease progression, restores life expectancy to near normal, and eliminates sexual transmission of the virus when viral load is durably suppressed ("Undetectable = Untransmittable", or U=U).

PurposeTreatment of HIV infection; prevention of transmission
Standard regimenTwo NRTIs plus an integrase inhibitor (e.g. dolutegravir-based)
GoalViral load below detection limit (<50 copies/mL)
When to startImmediately upon diagnosis (WHO recommendation since 2015)

History

The first antiretroviral, zidovudine (AZT), was approved in 1987 but used alone was quickly defeated by drug resistance. Dual therapy in the early 1990s gave modest gains. The breakthrough came in 1996, when triple combinations including the new protease inhibitors — termed highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) — were shown at the XI International AIDS Conference in Vancouver to reduce deaths and hospitalisations dramatically. Mortality from AIDS in high-income countries fell by more than half within two years.

Access in the developing world lagged because of drug prices, until generic production (notably the 2001 offer by the Indian manufacturer Cipla of a one-dollar-a-day triple combination), the Doha Declaration on TRIPS and public health, and funding from PEPFAR and the Global Fund transformed availability. The World Health Organization issued its first public-health treatment guidelines in 2002 and progressively simplified recommended regimens, culminating in single-tablet once-daily combinations and, for some patients, long-acting injectable therapy given every two months (cabotegravir plus rilpivirine).

When to start

Early guidelines deferred therapy until CD4 counts fell, balancing drug toxicity against benefit. The START trial (2015) showed that starting immediately at any CD4 count reduced serious illness and death, and the WHO has since 2015 recommended treatment for all people living with HIV regardless of CD4 count. Trials such as HPTN 052 also demonstrated that effective therapy prevents transmission to partners, grounding "treatment as prevention" strategies.

Monitoring and adherence

Treatment success is monitored by plasma viral load and CD4 count. Because HIV mutates rapidly, missed doses can allow resistant virus to emerge; adherence support, tolerable regimens, and fixed-dose single tablets are therefore central to programmes. Virological failure triggers resistance testing and a switch to a second-line regimen.

Preventive uses

Antiretrovirals are also used to prevent infection: pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) with tenofovir/emtricitabine or long-acting injectable cabotegravir for people at high risk, post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) after possible exposure, and treatment of pregnant women, which has reduced mother-to-child transmission to below 1% where fully implemented.
