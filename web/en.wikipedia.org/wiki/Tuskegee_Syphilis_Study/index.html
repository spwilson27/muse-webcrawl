The Tuskegee Syphilis Study, formally the "U.S. Public Health Service Syphilis Study at Tuskegee", was a clinical study conducted between 1932 and 1972 by the United States Public Health Service in Macon County, Alabama. Researchers followed the natural course of untreated syphilis in hundreds of impoverished African American sharecroppers who were told they were receiving free treatment for "bad blood". The men were never informed of their diagnosis, and effective treatment was deliberately withheld even after penicillin became the standard cure in the 1940s.

The study

The study enrolled about 600 men, of whom 399 had syphilis and 201 did not. Participants received free medical exams, meals, and burial insurance, but the "treatment" consisted of placebos such as aspirin and mineral tonics. Researchers actively prevented men from obtaining treatment elsewhere, including during Second World War draft examinations. The study was intended to last months but continued for forty years.

Exposure and aftermath

Venereal disease investigator Peter Buxtun raised internal objections from 1966 and ultimately went to the press; the story broke in July 1972, and the study was terminated that November. By then, dozens of participants had died of syphilis or its complications, wives had been infected, and children were born with congenital syphilis. A 1973 Senate hearing and a class-action lawsuit led to a $10 million settlement in 1974 and lifetime medical benefits for survivors and families.

Legacy

The scandal led directly to the National Research Act of 1974, the Belmont Report, and modern rules on informed consent and institutional review boards. In 1997 President Bill Clinton formally apologised on behalf of the United States. The study remains a central case in research ethics and a lasting factor in medical mistrust among African Americans.
