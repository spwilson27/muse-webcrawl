Sacred–profane dichotomy

The sacred–profane dichotomy is a concept central to the sociology of religion, most famously developed by Émile Durkheim in The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912). Durkheim argued that all religions organise the world into two opposed domains: the sacred — things set apart, forbidden, and surrounded by ritual — and the profane — the ordinary, everyday world. For Durkheim this division, rather than belief in gods, is the defining feature of religion, and sacred things symbolise the moral authority of society itself.

Development

The distinction has roots in Roman law and religion, where sacer denoted what belonged to the gods and profanum what lay "before the temple". Mircea Eliade later popularised a related contrast between sacred and profane space and time: sacred space (a temple, a holy mountain) is experienced as an irruption of the sacred — a hierophany — that founds and orientates the world, while profane space is homogeneous and neutral.

Criticism

Later anthropologists have questioned whether the dichotomy is universal. Some societies grade sanctity rather than splitting the world in two, and the boundary between sacred and profane is often porous and contested. Nevertheless the framework remains foundational in religious studies and continues to inform analysis of rituals, taboos and secular "sacred" values such as human rights.
