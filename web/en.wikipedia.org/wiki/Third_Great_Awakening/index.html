Third Great Awakening

Labelled placeholder: a revival prayer meeting of the Fulton Street type, 1857–58.

The Third Great Awakening refers to a historical period proposed by some historians (notably William G. McLoughlin) of religious activism in American history from the late 1850s to the early 20th century. It influenced pietistic Protestant denominations and had a strong element of social activism, sometimes called the "Social Gospel". It gathers together strands such as the 1857–58 Prayer Meeting Revival, the Holiness movement, the growth of foreign missions, and the beginnings of Pentecostalism.

Overview

The awakening is usually dated from the Businessmen's Revival of 1857–58, when noon prayer meetings begun by Jeremiah Lanphier on Fulton Street in New York spread across northern cities in the wake of a financial panic. Unlike the camp-meeting revivals of the Second Great Awakening, this revival was urban, lay-led and interdenominational.

Main currents

  • The Holiness movement within Methodism and beyond, teaching entire sanctification.
  • The Social Gospel, which applied Christian ethics to industrial poverty, labour conditions and temperance.
  • A surge in foreign missions, including the Student Volunteer Movement.
  • Pentecostalism, whose roots are often traced to the Azusa Street Revival of 1906 in Los Angeles.

Historiography

Some historians accept McLoughlin's "awakenings" framework; others argue that American Protestantism shows continuous revivalism rather than discrete nationwide awakenings, and treat the "Third Great Awakening" as a convenient label rather than a single event.
