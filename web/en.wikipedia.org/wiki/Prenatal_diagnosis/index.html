Prenatal diagnosis

Field: Obstetrics, medical genetics

Methods: Ultrasound, amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling, cell-free DNA screening

Prenatal diagnosis (or prenatal screening) is the testing of a fetus before birth to determine whether it has certain abnormalities, including genetic disorders. It is distinguished from prenatal screening: screening tests estimate risk, while diagnostic tests can confirm or rule out a condition.

Methods

Non-invasive techniques include obstetric ultrasonography, which can detect structural anomalies and measure nuchal translucency, and maternal blood tests for markers such as alpha-fetoprotein. Since 2011, non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) analyses cell-free fetal DNA circulating in maternal blood to screen for trisomies 21 (Down syndrome), 18, and 13 with high sensitivity.

Invasive tests

Chorionic villus sampling (CVS), usually performed at 10–13 weeks, samples placental tissue, while amniocentesis, typically performed after 15 weeks, samples amniotic fluid containing fetal cells. Both permit karyotyping, chromosomal microarray, and DNA testing with diagnostic accuracy, but carry a small procedure-related risk of miscarriage.

Purposes and ethics

Prenatal diagnosis allows parents and clinicians to prepare for a child with special needs, plan delivery at an appropriate facility, pursue in-utero treatment where available, or consider termination of pregnancy. The practice raises ethical debates concerning disability rights, informed consent, and the possibility of screening for non-medical traits.
