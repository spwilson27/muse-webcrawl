A bill of attainder (also known as an act of attainder or writ of attainder) is an act of a legislature declaring a person, or a group of people, guilty of some crime and punishing them, often without a trial. In contrast, a bill of pains and penalties imposes lesser punishments than death.

History in Britain

Bills of attainder were used extensively in England between the 14th and 18th centuries, particularly during the Wars of the Roses and the Tudor period, to dispose of political enemies. Victims included Thomas Cromwell (1540) and the Earl of Strafford (1641). An attainder carried "corruption of blood", forfeiting the condemned person's property and extinguishing their heirs' inheritance rights. The last bill of attainder in Britain was passed in 1798 against participants in the Irish Rebellion, and the practice fell into disuse; forfeiture for treason was abolished in 1870.

United States

The framers of the U.S. Constitution, familiar with the British abuse of attainders during the colonial period, prohibited them expressly. Article I, Section 9 forbids Congress to pass any bill of attainder or ex post facto law; Section 10 imposes the same prohibition on the states. The Supreme Court has applied the clause to legislative acts imposing punishment on identifiable persons without judicial trial, notably in United States v. Lovett (1946) and Nixon v. Administrator of General Services (1977), which set out historical, functional and motivational tests for identifying a forbidden attainder.
