The North Atlantic Gyre is one of the five great subtropical ocean gyres, a vast clockwise-circulating system of ocean currents in the North Atlantic Ocean driven by global wind patterns and the Coriolis effect.

Currents

The gyre is composed of four main currents:

  • The Gulf Stream, a powerful warm western boundary current flowing north-eastward along the North American coast;
  • The North Atlantic Current (North Atlantic Drift), its continuation toward northwestern Europe;
  • The Canary Current, a cool current flowing southward along the coast of northwest Africa;
  • The North Equatorial Current, flowing westward across the tropics to complete the circuit.

Wind stress curl between the trade winds and the westerlies piles water into the gyre's centre (Ekman transport), maintaining a mound of sea surface roughly a metre high that geostrophic currents flow around.

Sargasso Sea and ecology

The calm, slowly rotating centre of the gyre forms the Sargasso Sea, the only "sea" without a land coastline, defined entirely by the surrounding currents. It is carpeted in places by floating Sargassum seaweed and serves as a spawning ground for European and American eels and nursery habitat for loggerhead turtles, sharks and many fish.

Debris accumulation

Like other gyres, the North Atlantic Gyre concentrates floating debris. The North Atlantic garbage patch, first documented in the 1970s and mapped by long-term surface sampling, consists mostly of microplastic particles trapped within the gyre, at densities comparable to (though generally lower than) the Great Pacific garbage patch.

Climate role

The gyre redistributes heat from the tropics toward higher latitudes; the Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Current moderate the climate of western Europe. Variations in the gyre are linked to the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation and interact with the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, though the gyre's wind-driven circulation is distinct from the thermohaline overturning.
