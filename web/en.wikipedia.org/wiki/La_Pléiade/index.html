La Pléiade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

La Pléiade (French: [la plejad]) was a group of sixteenth-century French Renaissance poets whose principal members were Pierre de Ronsard, Joachim du Bellay and Jean-Antoine de Baïf. The name, borrowed from a group of Alexandrian poets named after the Pleiades star cluster, was also used for other circles of seven poets; the French group at various times included Étienne Jodelle, Rémy Belleau, Jean Dorat, Jacques Peletier du Mans and Pontus de Tyard.

Manifesto

In 1549 du Bellay published the group's manifesto, Défense et illustration de la langue française ("Defence and Illustration of the French Language"), which argued that French, then considered inferior to Latin and Greek, could become a literary language of equal richness if poets enriched it through imitation of classical models, invention of new words, and the revival of classical genres such as the ode, the elegy, the epic and the sonnet.

Work and influence

Ronsard, the group's leader, produced odes, hymns, the sonnet cycles Les Amours and an unfinished epic, La Franciade. Du Bellay wrote the sonnet sequence Les Regrets and L'Olive. The Pléiade poets transformed French verse, helped standardise French as a literary language, and were later regarded by the seventeenth-century classicists both as founders of modern French poetry and as examples of excess to be corrected.

The name Pléiade was later revived by the publisher Gallimard for the prestigious Bibliothèque de la Pléiade series of classic editions, founded in 1931.
