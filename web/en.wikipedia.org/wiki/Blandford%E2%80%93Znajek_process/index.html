The Blandford–Znajek process is a mechanism for the extraction of energy from a rotating black hole, introduced by Roger Blandford and Roman Znajek in 1977. It is widely regarded as the best explanation for how relativistic jets are powered in active galactic nuclei, quasars and gamma-ray burst engines.

Description

The process relies on magnetic fields threading the event horizon of a Kerr black hole. As in the Penrose process, energy is extracted within the ergosphere, the region outside the horizon in which all objects are forced to rotate with the black hole. A magnetic field maintained by currents in a surrounding accretion disk is wound up by the rotation of spacetime itself (frame dragging). The field lines act like wires in an electric circuit: the rotating black hole behaves as a unipolar inductor, generating an electromotive force between the pole and the equator of the horizon, on the order of 1020 volts for a supermassive black hole.

Electric currents flow along the field lines, carrying energy and angular momentum outward as a Poynting flux. At large distances this electromagnetic outflow can be converted into the kinetic energy of a collimated relativistic jet of matter and radiation. In the membrane paradigm picture, the horizon's rotation through the magnetic field drives a current through the horizon's effective surface resistance (about 377 ohms, the impedance of free space).

Power output

The extracted power scales approximately as the square of the black hole's spin parameter and the square of the magnetic flux threading the horizon, and linearly with the square of the black hole mass for a given field strength. For rapidly spinning supermassive black holes surrounded by strong fields, the luminosity can reach 1038–1040 watts, sufficient to power the brightest quasars. General relativistic magnetohydrodynamic simulations since the 2000s have confirmed that the mechanism works and that jet power correlates with black hole spin, as observed in the efficiency of jet production in microquasars and active galaxies.
