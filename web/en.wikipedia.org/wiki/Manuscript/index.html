Manuscript

A manuscript (abbreviated MS, plural MSS) was, traditionally, any document written by hand. The term derives from the Latin manu scriptum, "written by hand". In modern usage it also denotes an author's text submitted for publication, whether handwritten, typewritten or digital. Libraries and archives preserve manuscripts as primary sources, ranging from ancient papyrus scrolls and medieval codices to the working papers of modern authors.

History

Before the spread of printing, virtually all documents and books were manuscripts, copied by scribes on materials including papyrus, parchment and vellum, and later paper. Manuscript traditions developed distinctive scripts and decorative arts in many cultures: illuminated manuscripts in medieval Europe, calligraphic scrolls in East Asia, palm-leaf manuscripts in South and Southeast Asia, and richly decorated Qur'ans and Persian codices in the Islamic world. The monastic scriptorium was the principal centre of book production in early medieval Europe.

Study and preservation

The study of handwriting and scripts is palaeography; the study of manuscripts as physical objects is codicology. Scholars collate manuscript witnesses to reconstruct texts (textual criticism), since scribal copies introduce variants and errors. Major collections are held by institutions such as the British Library and the Vatican Library, and large-scale digitisation projects have made many manuscripts freely viewable online.

Modern usage

In publishing and academia, "manuscript" refers to the pre-publication version of a work — the stage at which it undergoes peer review and editing before typesetting.
