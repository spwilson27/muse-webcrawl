Circle map

In mathematics, a circle map is a map from the circle to itself. Circle maps arise in the study of dynamical systems, in particular in the modelling of driven oscillators and mode-locking phenomena. The best-known example is the Arnold standard circle map, introduced by Vladimir Arnold.

The standard circle map

The standard circle map is the family of maps

θn+1 = θn + Ω − (K/2π) sin(2πθn)  (mod 1),

where Ω is the bare rotation number and K measures the strength of the nonlinearity. For K = 0 the map is a pure rotation. As K increases, regions of parameter space called Arnold tongues appear, within which the motion is periodic and mode-locked to a rational rotation number.

Rotation number and mode locking

The long-term behaviour is characterised by the rotation number, the average advance of the angle per iteration. Rational rotation numbers correspond to periodic orbits; irrational ones to quasiperiodic motion. As parameters vary, the rotation number forms a "devil's staircase" of plateaux. Circle maps model physical entrainment phenomena such as synchronisation of coupled oscillators and the Josephson effect.
