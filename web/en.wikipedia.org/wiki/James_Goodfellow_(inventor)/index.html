James Goodfellow OBE (1937 – 2023) was a Scottish inventor credited with inventing the personal identification number (PIN) and with patenting the concept of the automated cash dispenser that became the automated teller machine (ATM).

Invention

Working in the 1960s for the printing company Smiths Industries (Kelvin Hughes), which had a contract connected with the Glasgow-based development of automated banking machinery, Goodfellow devised a system in which a machine-readable plastic card, encoded with the customer's account details, was combined with a memorised secret number — the PIN — to authorise the dispensing of a fixed amount of cash outside banking hours. His patent, filed in 1966 (British Patent 1,197,183, "Improvements relating to Customer-Operated Dispensing Systems"), described the combination of a token card and a personal code. A cash dispenser based on related work was installed by Barclays Bank in Enfield, London, in 1967; credit for the ATM's invention is shared among several pioneers, including John Shepherd-Barron and Don Wetzel, but Goodfellow's patent is regarded as the earliest description of the card-and-PIN system now universal.

Recognition

Goodfellow received little financial reward for the invention, as the patent rights belonged to his employer, and he was reportedly paid a modest bonus. He was appointed OBE in 2006 for services to innovation, and was inducted into the Scottish Engineering Hall of Fame.
