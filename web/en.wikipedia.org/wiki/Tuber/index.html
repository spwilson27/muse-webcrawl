Tuber

A tuber is a swollen, fleshy underground plant structure that stores nutrients, enabling the plant to survive unfavourable seasons such as winter or drought and to provide energy and nutrients for regrowth during the following growing season. Tubers also function in asexual (vegetative) reproduction. They are modified stems or roots: stem tubers bear buds ("eyes") from which new shoots arise, whereas root tubers (tuberous roots) lack nodes and buds on the tuber itself.

Stem tubers

A stem tuber forms from thickened rhizomes (underground stems) or stolons. The tops and sides of the tuber produce shoots that grow into a typical stem and leaves, while the underside produces roots. The potato (Solanum tuberosum) is the best-known stem tuber: its "eyes" are buds located in the axils of scale-like leaves, each capable of sprouting into a new plant. Other examples include the Jerusalem artichoke and some begonias.

Root tubers

Root tubers, or tuberous roots, are thickened lateral roots. They differ from stem tubers in having no nodes or internodes; new shoots arise from the proximal end near the crown of the parent plant. Important examples are the sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas), cassava (Manihot esculenta), and the dahlia. The sweet potato's edible storage organ is a true root, while the potato's is a true stem — a classic botanical distinction.

As food

Tubers are among the world's most important staple foods because they store large quantities of starch. Potatoes, sweet potatoes, cassava and yams (some of which form aerial bulbils as well as underground tubers) together supply a major share of global food energy. Many tuber crops are propagated vegetatively using "seed tubers", which preserves the genetic identity of desirable varieties but can also transmit viruses and other diseases between generations.
