Summary offences are tried in magistrates' courts

A summary offence (or summary offense in American spelling) is a category of criminal offence in common law jurisdictions that can be tried summarily — that is, quickly and without a jury — before a magistrate or a judge sitting alone. Summary offences are the least serious category of crime in the systems that use the term, and are contrasted with indictable offences, which are more serious and are tried on indictment before a judge and jury in a higher court.

Characteristics

Summary proceedings dispense with the formal committal and jury-selection steps of indictable trials. The accused is charged by way of a summons, complaint, or information, and the hearing usually takes place within weeks or months of the alleged offence. Penalties are capped by statute and are comparatively light: typically fines, community service, or imprisonment measured in months rather than years. Statutes commonly impose a short limitation period — for example, six months from the date of the offence under the Magistrates' Courts Act 1980 in England and Wales — within which summary proceedings must begin.

Examples

Common summary offences include most motoring and traffic offences, minor public order offences such as drunkenness and disorderly behaviour, low-value criminal damage, common assault, and regulatory offences such as breaches of licensing law. In many jurisdictions the overwhelming majority of criminal cases — often more than 90 per cent — are dealt with summarily, making magistrates' courts the workhorses of the criminal justice system.

Hybrid offences

Many legal systems also recognise a middle category of "either-way" offences (in England and Wales), "hybrid offences" (in Canada), or offences triable either summarily or on indictment. For these, the mode of trial is chosen by the court, the prosecution, or the defendant, depending on the jurisdiction and the circumstances of the case. The same conduct may therefore be punished either within the summary penalty limits or, if tried on indictment, with a substantially heavier sentence.

By jurisdiction

In England and Wales, summary offences are tried in magistrates' courts; defendants have no right to elect jury trial. In Canada, summary conviction offences under the Criminal Code carry a default maximum of two years less a day imprisonment and/or a fine, and are tried in provincial court. In Australia, summary offences are heard in local or magistrates' courts under state and territory law. In the United States, the closest analogue is the misdemeanor, particularly infractions and petty offences tried without a jury.
