Multiverse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Artistic impression of bubble universes budding off within a larger multiverse]
Eternal inflation spawning bubble universes.

The multiverse is the hypothetical set of all universes. Together, these universes are presumed to comprise everything that exists: the entirety of space, time, matter, energy, information, and the physical laws and constants that describe them. The different universes within the multiverse are called 'parallel universes', 'flat universes', 'other universes' or 'many worlds'.

Several distinct ideas carry the name: the Many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics (all measurement branches real); eternal inflation (bubble universes with possibly different constants, related to the string landscape); and cyclic or membrane cosmologies. Multiverse reasoning is invoked to explain fine-tuning — for example Steven Weinberg's anthropic prediction of the Cosmological constant — and is controversial: critics (including Roger Penrose and, in part, James Peebles) question whether untestable universes belong in science, while defenders argue the multiverse is a prediction of theories adopted for independent reasons, and that dismissing it would repeat the error of rejecting other galaxies as 'island universes' beyond observation.

Evidence and tests

No direct evidence exists. Proposed indirect tests include searching the cosmic microwave background for bubble-collision discs (none found), measuring spatial curvature (which constrains open-inflation models), and deriving probability measures over the landscape — a notoriously unsolved technical problem ('the measure problem'). The multiverse remains a frontier speculation with a serious, unresolved scientific debate around it.
