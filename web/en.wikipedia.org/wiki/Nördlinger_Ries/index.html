Nördlinger Ries

The Nördlinger Ries is an impact structure — a large circular depression — in western Bavaria and eastern Baden-Württemberg, Germany, centred on the town of Nördlingen. About 24 kilometres (15 mi) in diameter, it is one of the best-preserved and most thoroughly studied impact craters on Earth.

Formation

The crater was formed about 15.1 million years ago, in the Miocene, when an asteroid approximately 1.5 kilometres across struck the Alb foreland at an estimated 70,000 km/h. The explosion excavated a transient cavity several kilometres deep and scattered ejecta across central Europe; glassy tektites known as moldavites, flung as far as Bohemia, are part of this ejecta. A smaller crater, the Steinheim crater, some 40 km to the west, formed at nearly the same time, probably from a companion fragment.

Geology

For centuries the depression was interpreted as a volcanic caldera. In 1960 the American geologist Eugene Shoemaker and Edward Chao identified coesite — a high-pressure form of quartz produced only by impact shock — in the local rock called suevite, proving an impact origin; the Ries thereby became a classic site for impact geology and was used in training Apollo astronauts in 1970. Suevite in the crater contains microscopic diamonds formed from graphite under impact pressure, and buildings in Nördlingen, such as St. George's Church, are built partly of suevite said to contain hundreds of thousands of tiny diamonds. Today the crater floor, once occupied by a lake (the Ries-See), is a fertile agricultural plain, ringed by forested hills formed from displaced rock masses.
