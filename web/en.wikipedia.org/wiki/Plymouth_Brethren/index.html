Plymouth Brethren

The Plymouth Brethren are a low church, evangelical Christian movement that began in Ireland and England in the late 1820s. The movement takes its name from Plymouth, a large port city in Devon, England, where many Brethren lived and where an early and prominent assembly flourished. Brethren emphasise the priesthood of all believers, the authority of the Bible, weekly observance of the Lord's Supper ("breaking of bread"), and the absence of ordained clergy and formal denominational structures.

A Brethren meeting hall: Brethren worship is deliberately simple, without clergy or liturgy.

History

The movement traces its beginnings to meetings in Dublin around 1827–1828, where Christians from Anglican and dissenting backgrounds began meeting simply to break bread, dissatisfied with denominational divisions and clericalism. Edward Cronin and others were early figures in Dublin, while in England assemblies formed in Bristol — where George Müller became famous for his orphan houses — and in Plymouth, where Benjamin Wills Newton and John Nelson Darby were leading teachers.

The 1848 division

In 1848 the movement split over questions of discipline and fellowship between assemblies. The followers of Darby became known as the Exclusive Brethren, practising strict separation and centralised discipline, while those associated with Müller and Henry Craik at Bethesda Chapel in Bristol became the Open Brethren, who held that each local assembly is autonomous and welcomed other believers to communion. The two streams, and many subdivisions within each, persist to the present day.

Beliefs and practice

Brethren generally hold evangelical doctrines: the inspiration and authority of Scripture, salvation by faith, believer's baptism (in most assemblies), and the imminent return of Christ. Darby's teaching was foundational to dispensationalism, the system of biblical interpretation — with its scheme of successive dispensations and a pretribulation "rapture" of the church — that later became highly influential in American evangelicalism, not least through the Scofield Reference Bible. Assemblies typically have plural, unpaid elders rather than a pastor, and any brother may lead prayer, propose a hymn, or teach, in reliance on the guidance of the Holy Spirit.

Influence

Open Brethren became notable for vigorous foreign missions, sending workers to Africa, Asia, and Latin America from the nineteenth century onward; figures such as Hudson Taylor (founder of the China Inland Mission) were shaped by Brethren ideas. The Exclusive Brethren, particularly the branch now known as the Plymouth Brethren Christian Church, are more separatist, with distinctive rules governing association with outsiders. Well-known writers and teachers from the movement include John Nelson Darby, C. H. Mackintosh, William Kelly, and F. F. Bruce.
