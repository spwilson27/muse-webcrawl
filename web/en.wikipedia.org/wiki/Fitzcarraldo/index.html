Fitzcarraldo is a 1982 West German epic adventure-drama film written and directed by Werner Herzog and starring Klaus Kinski as the title character. It tells the story of Brian Sweeney Fitzgerald, an Irish rubber baron in early-20th-century Peru who dreams of building an opera house in the Amazonian city of Iquitos and, to finance it, attempts to reach an inaccessible rubber territory by hauling a steamship over a steep hill between two rivers.

Plot

Fitzgerald — "Fitzcarraldo" to the Spanish-speaking locals who cannot pronounce his name — is obsessed with opera and with the tenor Enrico Caruso. To reach rubber trees beyond impassable rapids on the Ucayali River, he buys a 320-ton steamship and, with the help of hundreds of indigenous Campa labourers, hauls it over a muddy isthmus to a parallel river. The scheme succeeds mechanically but collapses when the workers, believing the ship must be offered to the river gods, cut it loose; the boat is swept through the rapids it was meant to avoid. Fitzcarraldo returns to Iquitos ruined, but sells the ship and uses the proceeds to bring an opera troupe upriver for a single open-air performance, fulfilling a fragment of his dream.

Production

The film is famous for the extremity of its production. Herzog insisted on hauling a real steamship over a real hill without special effects, in a remote region of the Peruvian Amazon. The shoot was plagued by difficulties: original star Jason Robards fell ill and had to be replaced by Kinski after a large portion of the film had been shot; Mick Jagger, cast in a supporting role, left because of touring commitments and his character was written out; the production faced border disputes, accidents, and conflict with local communities. Several people were injured, and the harsh conditions became the subject of Les Blank's documentary Burden of Dreams (1982). Herzog's fraught collaboration with the volatile Kinski is also documented in his later film My Best Fiend (1999).

Themes and reception

Like Herzog's earlier Aguirre, the Wrath of God (1972), Fitzcarraldo portrays an obsessive, quixotic European confronting the indifference of the jungle. The film won Herzog the Best Director prize at the 1982 Cannes Film Festival and is frequently listed among the greatest films ever made. Its central image — the ship climbing the mountain — has become an emblem of artistic ambition pursued past the edge of reason, a quality critics note is shared by the film's making and its story.
