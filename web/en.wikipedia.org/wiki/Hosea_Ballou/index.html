Hosea Ballou (30 April 1771 – 7 June 1852) was an American Universalist clergyman and theological writer, regarded as one of the founders of American Universalism. Largely self-educated, the "farmer boy of Richmond" became the movement's most influential preacher and systematiser, arguing that all humanity would ultimately be saved and that punishment for sin is confined to this life.

Life and ministry

Ballou was born in Richmond, New Hampshire, to a Baptist family. Converted to Universalism as a young man, he began preaching in 1791 and was ordained in 1794. He served congregations in Massachusetts and Vermont, edited the Universalist Magazine, and from 1817 until his death was minister of the Second Universalist Church in Boston.

Theology

His Treatise on Atonement (1805) rejected the doctrines of the Trinity, original sin, and vicarious atonement, presenting God as a loving parent whose justice aimed at restoration rather than endless punishment. Ballou's denial of any future punishment — the "death and glory" or ultra-Universalist position — proved controversial even among Universalists, provoking the Restorationist controversy within the denomination. He also wrote hymns, sermons, and a history of the Universalist movement.

Legacy

Ballou's preaching and writing shaped the Universalist Church of America, one of the two denominations that merged in 1961 to form the Unitarian Universalist Association. His great-nephew Hosea Ballou II became the first president of Tufts College.
