Northern goshawk

The northern goshawk (Accipiter gentilis) is a species of medium-large bird of prey in the family Accipitridae, widely distributed across the temperate forests of North America, Europe and northern Asia. It is a powerful forest hawk, renowned for its speed and manoeuvrability when pursuing prey through dense woodland.

Description

Adults have slate blue-grey upperparts, finely barred pale underparts, a distinctive white supercilium (brow stripe) over fierce orange-red eyes, and a dark cap and ear coverts. Females are considerably larger than males, with a wingspan of 89–127 cm (35–50 in). Juveniles are brown above and streaked below, with yellow eyes that darken with age.

Behaviour and diet

Goshawks hunt birds and mammals — grouse, pigeons, corvids, squirrels and rabbits among them — taken by surprise in short, twisting pursuits through trees or by a swift glide from a perch. They nest high in mature trees, building large stick nests, and fiercely defend the nest area. The species is largely resident, though northern populations may move south in winter.

Falconry and culture

The goshawk has a long history in falconry, where it was prized as a versatile hunting bird; its name derives from the Old English gōsheafoc, "goose-hawk". The bird also features prominently in literature, including T. H. White's The Goshawk (1951) and Helen Macdonald's memoir H is for Hawk (2014).

Taxonomy note

Recent taxonomic authorities have split the American populations as a separate species, the American goshawk (Accipiter atricapillus), leaving A. gentilis for Eurasian birds, though usage varies between checklists.
