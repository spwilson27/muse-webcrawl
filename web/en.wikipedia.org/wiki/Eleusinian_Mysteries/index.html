Eleusinian Mysteries

The Eleusinian Mysteries (Greek: Ἐλευσίνια Μυστήρια) were initiations held every year for the cult of Demeter and Persephone based at Eleusis, northwest of Athens in ancient Greece. They are considered the most famous of the secret religious rites of ancient Greece, and among "the most important religious events in the Greek world". Their basis was an old agrarian cult, and there is evidence that they were derived from the religious practices of the Mycenaean period.

Initiates to the Mysteries believed that they would have a happy afterlife; the rites promised, in the words of the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, that "happy is he among men upon earth who has seen these mysteries; but he who is uninitiated... never has a lot of like good things once he is dead".

The myth

The central myth, told in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, recounts the abduction of Persephone by Hades, her mother Demeter's grief-stricken search during which agriculture withered, and Persephone's partial return, which explained the cycle of the seasons. During her search, Demeter came to Eleusis and was received by King Celeus; the cult at Eleusis was presented as founded by Demeter herself in gratitude.

The rites

Initiation had two stages. The Lesser Mysteries were held in Athens in spring (Anthesterion), and the Greater Mysteries at Eleusis in autumn (Boedromion), culminating in a great procession along the Sacred Way from Athens to Eleusis, baths in the sea, sacrifices, and finally the night-time ceremony in the Telesterion, a vast hall holding thousands. Initiates drank the kykeon, a barley drink, and were shown sacred objects. The vow of secrecy was enforced by law on pain of death, so the content of the central rite was never reliably recorded and remains debated.

History and end

The Mysteries were celebrated for roughly two thousand years, from the Mycenaean era into late antiquity, and under Rome drew initiates including emperors. In 170 CE the sanctuary was sacked by the Costoboci, rebuilt under Marcus Aurelius, and the rites finally ended in the late fourth century CE amid Christian suppression under Theodosius I; the sanctuary was destroyed during the invasion of Alaric in 395/396 CE.

Interpretations

Scholars have proposed many explanations of the secret experience — from dramatic re-enactment of the Demeter myth to, controversially, the use of a psychoactive kykeon, an entheogenic hypothesis advanced by Wasson, Hofmann and Ruck in The Road to Eleusis (1978). The hypothesis remains debated.
