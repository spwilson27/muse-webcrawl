Electoral college

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An electoral college is a body of electors established by a constitution or law, who are empowered to elect (formally) a candidate to a particular office, typically the head of state. The term is most associated with the United States Electoral College, which elects the President and Vice President: 538 electors, equal to the total of senators (100) and representatives (435) plus three for the District of Columbia, with 270 electoral votes required to win.

Operation and controversies

US electors are chosen by popular vote in each state (winner-take-all in 48 states; congressional-district allocation in Maine and Nebraska), meet in December in their state capitals, and occasionally vote against their pledge ("faithless electors" — states may penalise or replace them, per a 2020 Supreme Court ruling). The college can produce a president who lost the national popular vote (as in 2000 and 2016 — five times in US history), and elections turn on a handful of competitive "swing states". Other electoral colleges include the bodies electing the presidents of India (weighted state and national legislators) and Germany (the Federal Convention). Reform debates in the US centre on the National Popular Vote interstate compact, congressional-district plans, and constitutional amendment proposals.
