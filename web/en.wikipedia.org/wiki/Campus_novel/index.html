Campus novel

A campus novel, also known as an academic novel, is a novel whose main action is set in and around the campus of a university or college. The genre, which developed in the early 1950s, typically satirises academic life, focusing on the human weaknesses of professors, administrators and students rather than on scholarship itself.

Characteristics

Campus novels commonly feature a protagonist who is a teacher or student at odds with the institution: a new arrival, a visiting lecturer, or a disillusioned professor. Recurring themes include faculty politics, tenure struggles, departmental intrigues, romantic entanglements, generational conflict and the clash between the enclosed academic world and life outside it. The tone is usually comic or satirical, though the genre also includes serious works about education and intellectual life.

Notable examples

The genre is often traced to Mary McCarthy's The Groves of Academe (1952) and Kingsley Amis's Lucky Jim (1954), the latter a landmark of postwar British comic fiction. Vladimir Nabokov's Pnin (1957) portrays a Russian émigré professor in America. David Lodge's campus trilogy — Changing Places (1975), Small World (1984) and Nice Work (1988) — brought the genre to wide popularity, as did Malcolm Bradbury's The History Man (1975).

Later and more serious treatments include Donna Tartt's The Secret History (1992), Richard Russo's Straight Man (1997), Zadie Smith's On Beauty (2005) and Jeffrey Eugenides's The Marriage Plot (2011). Japanese examples include Haruki Murakami's Norwegian Wood (1987), which focuses on student life.

Subgenres

A related form is the student-centred novel of university life, and the "varsity novel" of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, which dealt more sentimentally with undergraduate experience at Oxford, Cambridge and the Ivy League. Dark academia, a later aesthetic movement, draws heavily on campus fiction.
