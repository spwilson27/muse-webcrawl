The canonization of the Romanovs refers to the glorification (canonization) of the last Russian emperor, Nicholas II, his wife Alexandra, and their five children — Olga, Tatiana, Maria, Anastasia and Alexei — who were murdered by the Bolsheviks at Yekaterinburg on the night of 16/17 July 1918.

Canonization

The Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia canonized the family in 1981 as martyrs, together with the servants killed with them. After long debate, the Russian Orthodox Church (Moscow Patriarchate) glorified them in 2000 as passion bearers — a category for those who faced death with Christian humility and forgiveness rather than dying explicitly for the faith. The servants and the family physician Eugene Botkin were recognised in subsequent years. Their feast day is 17 July.

Remains and commemoration

The remains of Nicholas, Alexandra and three daughters were exhumed in 1991 and reburied in St Peter and Paul Cathedral, St Petersburg, in 1998; the remains of Alexei and one daughter, found in 2007, were confirmed by DNA testing but remained in state archives amid Church reservations. The Church on the Blood in Yekaterinburg, built on the site of the demolished Ipatiev House, is the principal shrine. Icons of the "Holy Royal Martyrs" are widespread, and the canonization remains intertwined with post-Soviet debates about monarchy, repentance and national history.
