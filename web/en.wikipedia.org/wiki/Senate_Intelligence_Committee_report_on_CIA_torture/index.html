Senate Intelligence Committee report on CIA torture

The Committee Study of the Central Intelligence Agency's Detention and Interrogation Program is a report compiled by the Democratic staff of the United States Senate Select Committee on Intelligence (SSCI) into the Central Intelligence Agency's Detention and Interrogation Program and its use of torture ("enhanced interrogation techniques") during the presidencies of George W. Bush. The full report is about 6,700 pages long; a 525-page redacted executive summary was released to the public on December 9, 2014.

Background

After the September 11 attacks, the CIA detained at least 119 individuals at secret prisons ("black sites") around the world and subjected many to coercive interrogation techniques, including waterboarding, sleep deprivation, confinement in small boxes, stress positions, and "rectal feeding". The Senate investigation, initiated in 2009 and chaired by Senator Dianne Feinstein, reviewed more than six million pages of CIA documents.

Findings

The executive summary lists twenty key findings, concluding among other things that: the CIA's use of enhanced interrogation techniques was not an effective means of obtaining accurate intelligence; the CIA's justifications for the program rested on inaccurate claims about its effectiveness; interrogations were far more brutal than the CIA represented to policymakers; conditions of confinement were harsher than represented; the CIA repeatedly provided inaccurate information to the Department of Justice, Congress, the media, and the White House; and the program damaged the United States' standing in the world and resulted in significant monetary and non-monetary costs. At least 26 of the detainees were wrongfully held.

Reactions

The CIA disputed several conclusions, maintaining that the program produced valuable intelligence, while acknowledging mistakes. Former CIA officials organized a public rebuttal. Human rights organizations and UN officials called for prosecutions. President Barack Obama stated that the techniques "did significant damage to America's standing" and that "we tortured some folks". Senator John McCain praised the report's release.

Full report and controversy

The full classified report remains unreleased. Copies held by executive agencies became the subject of legal disputes; the committee transferred a copy to the National Archives to preserve it. The report also describes the CIA's 2010 unauthorized access dispute with committee staff computers (the "Panetta Review" affair), which led to a public apology by CIA Director John Brennan.
