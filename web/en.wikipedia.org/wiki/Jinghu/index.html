The jinghu (Chinese: 京胡; pinyin: jīnghú, literally "capital huqin") is a Chinese bowed string instrument in the huqin family, used primarily as the leading melodic instrument in the accompaniment of Peking opera (Beijing opera). It has a small cylindrical body made of bamboo covered on the front with snakeskin, two strings tuned in fifths, and a bamboo bow with horsehair passing between the strings. The jinghu is the highest-pitched of the huqin instruments, with a bright, penetrating tone that cuts through the orchestra and closely doubles and ornaments the singer's line.

Construction

The jinghu's body (qintong) is a short bamboo tube, smaller than that of the erhu, its open back partially closed by a soundboard arrangement and its front covered with thin python or other snakeskin. A bamboo neck carries two tuning pegs; silk strings were traditional, replaced in the 20th century by steel (or steel-and-nylon) strings for greater volume and stability. The instrument is played vertically, resting on the player's left thigh, with the bow hair permanently interlaced between the two strings, so that bowing direction selects the string — push-bow playing the outer string and pull-bow the inner string (or vice versa).

Playing technique

The left hand stops the strings without a fingerboard, fingers pressing the string in space; vibrato, slides (huayin), grace notes, and rapid trills imitate and decorate the vocal melody. Standard tunings are fifths — commonly sol–re (5–2) for the xipi melodic system and re–la (2–6) for erhuang — and the player switches between jinghu of different tunings during performance. Because the skin head and bamboo body produce a sharp attack, the jinghu's timbre is described as bright, tense, and "piercing," ideally suited to the stylised vocal delivery of Peking opera.

Role in Peking opera

The jinghu leads the wenchang (civil/melodic) division of the opera orchestra, alongside the yuehu (supporting fiddle), sanxian, pipa, and flutes, while the wuchang (percussion division — bangu clapper-drum, gongs, and cymbals) is directed by the drummer-conductor. The jinghu player must follow the singer with extreme precision, matching breath, ornament, and tempo, often improvising connective passages (guomen). Famous jinghu masters — such as Xu Lanpei, accompanist to Mei Lanfang, and later players including Li Muliang and Sun Yumin — are credited by name in programmes, reflecting the instrument's star status within the ensemble.

History

The jinghu developed with Peking opera itself, which coalesced in Beijing from Anhui and Hubei troupes after 1790. Its ancestor was the tihu/huqin types used in regional opera; the instrument took its present compact bamboo form in the 19th century as opera moved into teahouse theatres and demanded a louder, sharper lead fiddle. In the 20th century, instrument makers enlarged the body slightly and strengthened fittings for modern concert halls, and jinghu began appearing occasionally outside opera in Chinese orchestra arrangements and concert pieces, though its identity remains inseparable from Peking opera.
