Lange model

The Lange model (or Lange–Lerner theorem) is a neoclassical economic model of a socialist economy based on public ownership of the means of production and a trial-and-error approach to determining output targets and achieving economic equilibrium and Pareto efficiency. In the model, a central planning board simulates a market in capital goods by setting prices through trial and error, with the goal of achieving Pareto efficiency and marginal-cost pricing.

The model was first proposed by Oskar R. Lange in 1936 and 1937 and later expanded by economists including Abba Lerner and Fred M. Taylor. It was developed in the course of the socialist calculation debate, as a response to arguments by Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek that socialist calculation was impossible without market prices for capital goods.

Overview

In the Lange model, the economy consists of a public sector (state-owned enterprises), a private sector of small businesses and farms, and consumers who are free to choose their occupations and consumption goods. A Central Planning Board (CPB) sets the prices of producer goods and adjusts them by trial and error: if a good is in shortage, its price is raised; if there is a surplus, its price is lowered. Consumer goods and labour are allocated through genuine markets, while the CPB simulates a market for capital and intermediate goods.

Managers of state-owned firms are instructed to follow two rules: first, combine factors of production so as to minimize the average cost of production; and second, set output so that marginal cost equals the price set by the board. Because prices are parametric for managers, the resulting allocation can in principle reach a Pareto-efficient equilibrium equivalent to that of a competitive market economy.

History

Lange outlined the model in his two-part essay "On the Economic Theory of Socialism", published in the Review of Economic Studies in 1936 and 1937, and issued in book form in 1938 with a contribution by Fred M. Taylor. Lange argued that a socialist economy could use the same trial-and-error method that Walrasian theory attributed to markets, and that planners could in some respects outperform actual markets—for example by correcting for externalities and monopoly.

Abba Lerner contributed the rule that state firms should price at marginal cost, and the model is consequently sometimes called the Lange–Lerner model. Lange later acknowledged the importance of Hayek's emphasis on knowledge and incentives, and in his 1967 essay "The Computer and the Market" he suggested that electronic computers could solve the system's equations directly, though he maintained that the market remained a useful computing device for simpler problems.

Criticism

Critics, particularly from the Austrian School, argued that the model ignores the entrepreneurial function, the problem of incentives for managers who do not bear the risk of losses, and the dynamic process of competition. Hayek conceded that the scheme was logically coherent but doubted its practicality, since the board would need to adjust an enormous number of prices continually and managers would lack genuine market signals. Later critics noted that trial-and-error adjustment could be unstable and that the model assumes away the knowledge problem it purports to solve.
