Cardiomyopathy is a group of primary diseases of the heart muscle (cardiac muscle). Initially there may be few or no symptoms. As the disease worsens, shortness of breath, feeling tired, and swelling of the legs may occur, due to the onset of heart failure. An irregular heartbeat and fainting may occur. Those affected are at an increased risk of sudden cardiac death.

The term is generally reserved for diseases that primarily affect the heart muscle itself, rather than damage secondary to coronary artery disease, hypertension or valve disease — although in practice these frequently coexist. Cardiomyopathies are a major cause of heart failure and of sudden death in young people, including competitive athletes.

Types

  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM): The heart muscle thickens abnormally, especially the interventricular septum, sometimes obstructing blood flow out of the left ventricle. Usually caused by mutations in sarcomere genes (e.g., MYH7, MYBPC3) and inherited in an autosomal-dominant pattern. Affects roughly 1 in 500 people; a leading cause of sudden death in young athletes.
  • Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM): The heart chambers enlarge and the walls thin and weaken, reducing pumping ability. Causes include genetic variants (notably titin truncations), alcohol, viral myocarditis, pregnancy (peripartum cardiomyopathy) and chemotherapy. It is the most common cardiomyopathy and a leading indication for heart transplantation.
  • Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM/ARVC): Heart muscle, classically of the right ventricle, is progressively replaced by fat and scar tissue, predisposing to dangerous ventricular arrhythmias. Usually caused by mutations in desmosomal genes (e.g., plakophilin-2).
  • Restrictive cardiomyopathy: The walls become stiff and resist filling while pumping function is relatively preserved. Causes include amyloidosis (now recognised as common in older adults), sarcoidosis, haemochromatosis and radiation.
  • Unclassified/other: Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy — transient ballooning of the left ventricle triggered by intense stress; left ventricular non-compaction; and cardiomyopathies of metabolic storage diseases such as Fabry disease.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms may include breathlessness (especially on exertion or when lying flat), fatigue, palpitations, dizziness and syncope, chest pain, cough, and peripheral oedema. Some people — particularly with HCM — have no symptoms at all and are diagnosed after a murmur is heard, an ECG abnormality is found, or a relative is diagnosed and family screening is performed. The first manifestation may tragically be sudden cardiac death during exertion.

Causes and genetics

Many cardiomyopathies are genetic, with more than 100 associated genes identified, most affecting the sarcomere, cytoskeleton or desmosome. Family screening of first-degree relatives is recommended for most types. Acquired causes include chronic heavy alcohol use, viral infection and inflammation (myocarditis), certain cancer drugs (anthracyclines, trastuzumab), uncontrolled tachycardia, thyroid disease, diabetes, pregnancy, and infiltrative or storage disorders (amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, haemochromatosis). In many cases no cause is found ("idiopathic"), though genetic testing increasingly reclassifies these.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on imaging and electrical studies: an echocardiogram defines wall thickness, chamber size and function; cardiac MRI characterises tissue and scarring (late gadolinium enhancement) and is key in amyloidosis, sarcoidosis and ARVC; the ECG often shows distinctive abnormalities. Blood tests (including natriuretic peptides and, where relevant, iron studies and serum free light chains), genetic testing, exercise testing, rhythm monitoring and occasionally endomyocardial biopsy complete the work-up. Modern classification (notably the 2023 ESC guidelines) increasingly combines morphology, function, genetics and tissue characterisation rather than anatomy alone.

Treatment

Treatment depends on type and stage. General measures include treating heart failure with guideline-directed medicines (beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors or ARNI, mineralocorticoid antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors), anticoagulation where indicated, and avoiding triggers such as alcohol. HCM may be treated with beta-blockers, the myosin inhibitor mavacamten for obstructive disease, septal reduction therapy (surgical myectomy or alcohol septal ablation), and — where sudden-death risk is high — an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD). Cardiac amyloidosis due to transthyretin now has disease-modifying therapy (tafamidis and gene-silencing drugs). Advanced disease may require ventricular assist devices or heart transplantation. Athletes with certain cardiomyopathies are advised against high-intensity competitive sport, though shared decision-making about exercise is increasingly individualised.

Epidemiology

Together, cardiomyopathies affect millions worldwide. HCM was long estimated at 1 in 500 people, and genetic-era studies suggest the carrier frequency may be higher; DCM prevalence is estimated around 1 in 250–500. Cardiomyopathies account for a substantial fraction of heart failure cases and of sudden deaths under the age of 35.
