Louis Harold Gray FRS (10 November 1905 – 9 July 1965) was an English physicist and radiologist who worked principally on the effects of radiation on biological systems and is considered one of the founders of the science of radiobiology. The SI unit of absorbed radiation dose, the gray (symbol Gy), is named in his honour.

Career

Gray studied physics at Trinity College, Cambridge, and worked at the Cavendish Laboratory under Lord Rutherford, taking his doctorate on the absorption of gamma radiation. In 1933 he became hospital physicist at Mount Vernon Hospital in Northwood, London, one of the first physicists employed in a clinical setting. There, with William Bragg's ideas as a basis, he developed the Bragg–Gray cavity theory, which relates the ionisation measured in a small gas-filled cavity to the energy absorbed in the surrounding material — the theoretical foundation of modern radiation dosimetry. During the Second World War and after, he worked on the neutron dosimetry problems of the atomic energy programme.

Radiobiology

In 1954 the Medical Research Council established the British Empire Cancer Campaign Research Unit in Radiobiology at Mount Vernon under Gray's direction. The unit became a world centre for the quantitative study of radiation effects on living cells; among its contributions were foundational work on the oxygen effect in radiobiology — the discovery that well-oxygenated cells are markedly more sensitive to radiation than hypoxic cells — which had immediate implications for radiotherapy of tumours and was pursued by his colleague Oliver Scott and others. Gray also proposed, with others, the concept underlying the relative biological effectiveness (RBE) comparisons used in radiation protection.

Gray's laboratory was the cradle of much of British radiobiology, and the Gray Laboratory that bore his name continued at Mount Vernon, later becoming part of the University of Oxford's oncology research at the Gray Institute for Radiation Oncology and Biology. The International Council adopted the gray as the SI unit of absorbed dose in 1975, a decade after his death.
