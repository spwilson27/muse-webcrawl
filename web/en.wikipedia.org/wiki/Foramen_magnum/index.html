Foramen magnum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The foramen magnum (Latin for "great hole") is the large oval opening in the occipital bone at the base of the skull through which the spinal cord passes to connect with the brain. It is the largest of the skull's foramina and a landmark of central importance in anatomy and anthropology.

Structure and contents

The foramen magnum is bordered by the four parts of the occipital bone; its front margin lies near the clivus and the sphenoid. Through it pass the medulla oblongata and its meninges, the vertebral arteries, the anterior and posterior spinal arteries, and the spinal roots of the accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI). The occipital condyles on either side articulate with the atlas (first cervical vertebra), allowing the head to nod.

Clinical significance

Abnormalities around the foramen magnum include Chiari malformations, in which cerebellar tissue herniates through the opening, and tumours such as meningiomas of the craniocervical junction. Tonsillar herniation through the foramen magnum ("coning") is a life-threatening complication of raised intracranial pressure.

In evolution

The position of the foramen magnum is used by palaeoanthropologists as evidence of posture: in habitual bipeds such as humans it sits near the centre of the skull base, balancing the head atop the vertical spine, whereas in quadrupeds it lies farther back. Its forward position in early hominin fossils is cited as evidence of bipedal walking.
