Difference threshold

The difference threshold, also known as the just noticeable difference (JND), is the minimum amount by which a stimulus must change in intensity or strength for the change to be detectable — conventionally, the change detected on 50% of occasions. It is a core concept in psychophysics, alongside the absolute threshold for detecting a stimulus at all.

Weber's law

Ernst Heinrich Weber observed that the JND is a roughly constant proportion of the original stimulus intensity: ΔI / I = k. For example, the JND for lifted weight is about 2% — a person holding 100 g can just detect a 2 g increase, while one holding 1 kg needs about 20 g. This relationship, known as Weber's law, holds approximately for many senses in the middle ranges of intensity but breaks down near the absolute threshold. Gustav Fechner built his logarithmic law of sensation on Weber's findings.

Applications

JNDs are used in sensory science and marketing (e.g. sizing product or price changes below perceptual notice), audio and image compression (discarding differences below the JND), and ergonomics and user-interface design.
