X-linked dominant inheritance

X-linked dominant inheritance, also known as X-linked dominance, is a mode of genetic inheritance in which a dominant gene is carried on the X chromosome. As an inheritance pattern, it is less common than X-linked recessive inheritance, because dominance combined with X-linkage produces distinctive pedigree patterns.

Patterns of inheritance

In X-linked dominant inheritance, a person needs only one copy of the mutated gene on one X chromosome to express the trait or disorder. Because females have two X chromosomes and males have one, characteristic patterns arise:

  • An affected father passes the trait to all of his daughters (who receive his only X chromosome) and to none of his sons (who receive his Y chromosome).
  • An affected heterozygous mother passes the trait, on average, to 50% of her children of either sex.
  • Affected females are typically more numerous than affected males, roughly twice as many in a population, although the condition may be more severe — sometimes even lethal before birth — in males, which can further skew the sex ratio.

Examples

Examples of X-linked dominant conditions include vitamin D–resistant rickets (X-linked hypophosphataemia), Rett syndrome (in its classic form), incontinentia pigmenti, fragile X syndrome (often described as dominant with reduced penetrance), Alport syndrome in some families, and certain forms of Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease. Some X-linked dominant disorders, such as incontinentia pigmenti type 1 and Rett syndrome, are usually lethal in males, so they are seen almost exclusively in females.
