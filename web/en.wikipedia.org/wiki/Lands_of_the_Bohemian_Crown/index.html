Lands of the Bohemian Crown

The Lands of the Bohemian Crown (German: Länder der Böhmischen Krone; Czech: Země Koruny české; Latin: Corona regni Bohemiae) were the territories joined under the rule of the kings of Bohemia from the later Middle Ages until 1918. At their core stood the Kingdom of Bohemia, the Margraviate of Moravia, and the Silesian duchies, together with Upper and Lower Lusatia until 1635. The concept of the Crown of Saint Wenceslas expressed the idea that these lands formed an indivisible whole belonging to the Crown rather than to the person of the king.

Formation

Bohemia and Moravia were united under the Přemyslid dynasty. The decisive systematisation came under Charles IV, King of Bohemia and Holy Roman Emperor (r. 1346–78), who in 1348 issued charters defining the lands of the Crown: Bohemia as the head, with Moravia, the Silesian principalities (which had accepted Bohemian overlordship piecemeal in the early 14th century), Brandenburg briefly, and the Lusatias as members. Charles elevated Prague to an archbishopric and founded Charles University, making Bohemia a centre of the Empire; the Bohemian king was constitutionally first among the Empire's secular prince-electors.

Later history

After the Hussite Wars (1419–34), the Crown lands enjoyed a period of religious dualism between Utraquist and Catholic estates. In 1526 the estates elected Ferdinand of Habsburg, drawing the Crown lands into the Habsburg monarchy. The estates' revolt of 1618 opened the Thirty Years' War; after their defeat at White Mountain (1620), the Renewed Land Ordinance (1627) made Bohemia a hereditary Habsburg land and stripped the estates of power. By the Peace of Prague (1635) the Lusatias passed to Saxony. In 1742, by the Treaty of Breslau, most of Silesia was ceded to Prussia, leaving only Austrian Silesia. Within the Habsburg and later Austrian Empire, Bohemia, Moravia, and Austrian Silesia remained separate crown lands, a status that lasted until the monarchy's collapse in 1918, when they became the historic lands of Czechoslovakia — and today, with the same traditional lands (Bohemia, Moravia, Czech Silesia), of the Czech Republic.
