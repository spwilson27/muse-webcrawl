Distributed spacecraft

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A distributed spacecraft system is one in which the functions of a single large spacecraft are divided among two or more separate, cooperating spacecraft. Unlike a mere constellation of independent satellites, the elements of a distributed system exchange data and work together in real time to perform a shared mission — for example, acting as a single large instrument, such as an interferometer with a baseline far larger than any one vehicle could provide.

Concepts

  • Formation flying: multiple spacecraft maintain precise relative positions, from metres to thousands of kilometres apart, using relative navigation and coordinated control.
  • Fractionation: subsystems (power, communications, computation, payload) are placed on separate free-flying modules connected by wireless links, so that modules can be replaced or upgraded individually.
  • Clusters and swarms: larger numbers of simple spacecraft cooperate with looser coordination, gaining robustness through redundancy.

Examples

The GRACE and GRACE-FO missions fly two spacecraft in tandem to map Earth's gravity field by precisely measuring the distance between them. ESA's Proba-3 mission flies two spacecraft in formation to create an artificial solar eclipse for coronagraphy. Proposed missions include space interferometers and distributed-aperture telescopes, and the DARPA System F6 programme studied fractionated spacecraft architecture. CubeSat missions increasingly fly as coordinated pairs or swarms rather than singletons.

Advantages and challenges

Advantages include lower launch mass per element, graceful degradation (loss of one element need not end the mission), and instrument baselines or apertures impossible for a monolithic craft. Challenges include precise relative navigation and control, inter-satellite communication latency and bandwidth, autonomous coordination, and the complexity of testing a system whose parts only function together in orbit.
