Microvesicle

Microvesicles (also called microparticles, ectosomes, or shedding vesicles) are a type of extracellular vesicle released from the cell membrane of nearly all cell types. They range in diameter from about 100 nanometres to 1 micrometre and are formed by the outward budding and fission of the plasma membrane, in contrast to the smaller exosomes, which originate within multivesicular bodies.

Formation and contents

Microvesicle shedding involves rearrangement of the cell's cytoskeleton and membrane phospholipids, often triggered by calcium influx or cell activation. The vesicles carry a sample of their parent cell's contents: surface receptors, membrane and cytosolic proteins, bioactive lipids, and nucleic acids including messenger RNA and microRNA.

Functions

Microvesicles mediate intercellular communication, transferring proteins and RNA between cells. They play roles in blood coagulation (platelet- and endothelium-derived vesicles expose tissue factor and phosphatidylserine), immune modulation, inflammation, angiogenesis, and tissue repair.

Clinical significance

Elevated microvesicle levels are associated with cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, and autoimmune disorders. Tumour-derived microvesicles can promote metastasis and suppress immune responses, making extracellular vesicles active research targets both as biomarkers in liquid biopsies and as potential drug-delivery vehicles.
