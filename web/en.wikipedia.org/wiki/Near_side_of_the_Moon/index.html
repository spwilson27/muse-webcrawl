Near side of the Moon

The near side of the Moon is the lunar hemisphere that always faces Earth, owing to the Moon's synchronous rotation (tidal locking): the Moon rotates on its axis in the same time it takes to orbit Earth. Slight wobbling, called libration, allows observers to see about 59 percent of the lunar surface over time.

Features

The near side is marked by large dark plains, the lunar maria — including Mare Imbrium, Mare Serenitatis, Mare Tranquillitatis and the vast Oceanus Procellarum — formed by ancient basaltic lava flows. Bright highland regions and prominent craters such as Tycho and Copernicus are also visible. The far side, by contrast, has far fewer maria.

Exploration

All crewed Apollo landings took place on the near side so that direct radio communication with Earth was possible; Apollo 11 landed in Mare Tranquillitatis in July 1969. Robotic missions, including the first soft landing by Luna 9 in 1966, also targeted the near side.
