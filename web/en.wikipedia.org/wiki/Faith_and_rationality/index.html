Faith and rationality

The relationship between faith and reason has been debated since antiquity.

Faith and rationality exist in varying degrees of conflict or compatibility between faith and reason. Rationality is the use of reason, logic, and evidence in forming beliefs, while faith is often understood as belief held apart from, or beyond, such evidence. The question of how the two relate is a central topic in the philosophy of religion, epistemology, and theology.

Models of the relationship

Philosophers and theologians have proposed several models. Fideism holds that faith is independent of, or even opposed to, reason. Rationalism in this context holds that religious belief should be accepted only insofar as it can be rationally justified; evidentialism, associated with W. K. Clifford, insists it is wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence. Critical rationalism and various compatibilist views—such as the Thomist position that faith and reason are harmonious sources of truth that cannot ultimately contradict each other—occupy intermediate positions.

Historical views

Augustine and Anselm expressed the ideal of "faith seeking understanding" (fides quaerens intellectum). Thomas Aquinas held that some truths of faith can be demonstrated by reason (the "preambles of faith"), while others, such as the Trinity, exceed reason without contradicting it. In the Islamic tradition, al-Ghazālī and Averroes debated the standing of philosophy relative to revelation. Søren Kierkegaard famously championed a "leap of faith" beyond rational calculation. In the twentieth century, Reformed epistemologists such as Alvin Plantinga argued that belief in God can be "properly basic" and warranted without propositional evidence.

Contemporary debate

Debates continue over whether religious belief requires evidence, whether scientific and religious claims conflict, and how public reason should treat faith-based claims in pluralistic societies. Positions range from the conflict thesis associated with some New Atheist writers to Stephen Jay Gould's proposal of "non-overlapping magisteria" (NOMA).
