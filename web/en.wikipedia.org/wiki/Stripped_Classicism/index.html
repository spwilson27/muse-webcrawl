Stripped Classicism

Stripped Classicism (or Starved Classicism) is primarily a 20th-century classicist architectural style stripped of most or all ornamentation, frequently employed by governments while designing official buildings. It was adapted from the more elaborate Beaux-Arts and Neoclassical styles, retaining classical massing, symmetry, and proportions but with plain surfaces and minimal carved detail.

Characteristics and use

The style is characterized by a simplified classical vocabulary: columns may be suggested by flat pilasters, cornices reduced to simple bands, and entrances framed by plain surrounds. It became a default for public architecture in the 1920s–1940s, notably in the United States — where it is sometimes called PWA Moderne or Depression Moderne — in works such as the Federal Triangle in Washington, D.C. The architecture of Nazi Germany and Stalinist architecture also employed monumental stripped classical forms, though often at a much grander scale and with heavier proportions.

Notable practitioners include Paul Philippe Cret, whose Folger Shakespeare Library in Washington is a frequently cited example.
