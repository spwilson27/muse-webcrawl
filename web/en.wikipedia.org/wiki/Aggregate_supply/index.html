Aggregate supply

In economics, aggregate supply (AS) is the total supply of goods and services that firms in a national economy are willing and able to produce and sell at a given overall price level in a given period. It is normally depicted as an aggregate supply curve showing the relationship between the price level and the quantity of real output supplied.

Short run and long run

The short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve slopes upward: with some input prices (especially nominal wages) slow to adjust, a higher price level raises producers' real margins and induces greater output. The long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve is conventionally drawn vertical at the economy's potential output, determined by the quantities of labour, capital and natural resources and by technology — in the long run the price level does not affect the sustainable quantity supplied.

Shifts

The aggregate supply curve shifts with changes in input prices (such as oil or wages), productivity and technology, the size and quality of the labour force and capital stock, taxes and subsidies, and expectations. Adverse supply shocks — famously the oil price increases of the 1970s — shift SRAS leftward, raising prices while lowering output, a combination known as stagflation.
