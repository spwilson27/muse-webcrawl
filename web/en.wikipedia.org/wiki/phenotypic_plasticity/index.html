Phenotypic plasticity

Reaction norms: a single genotype can yield different phenotypes across environments.

Phenotypic plasticity refers to some of the changes in an organism's behavior, morphology and physiology in response to a unique environment. Fundamentally, phenotypic plasticity is the ability of one genotype to produce more than one phenotype when exposed to different environments. It is a property of individual organisms, in contrast to genetic variation, which is a property of populations.

Plasticity can be adaptive — increasing the organism's fitness in the environment that induces it — or non-adaptive. A graph of a trait value as a function of an environmental variable is called a reaction norm, and differences among genotypes in their reaction norms constitute genotype-by-environment interaction.

Examples

Classic examples include predator-induced defenses in Daphnia (water fleas grow protective helmets and spines when they detect predator cues), seasonal polyphenism in butterflies, temperature-dependent sex determination in some reptiles, the development of distinct castes in social insects from similar genomes through diet, and acclimatization of plants to sun versus shade leaves. In humans, altitude acclimatization and tanning are familiar plastic responses.

Evolutionary significance

Plasticity itself can evolve by natural selection, and it may facilitate colonization of new environments, buffer populations against environmental change, or precede genetic change through processes such as genetic assimilation, in which an initially induced trait becomes constitutive. Phenotypic plasticity is a central topic in evolutionary developmental biology and ecology, and is increasingly studied in the context of climate change.
