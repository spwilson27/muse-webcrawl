Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) was a Dutch philosopher of Portuguese-Jewish origin, one of the great rationalists of the 17th century. His Ethics, written in geometrical form and published after his death, argues that there is only one substance — God or Nature (Deus sive Natura) — of which minds and bodies are modes. Excommunicated from Amsterdam's Jewish community in 1656 for his unorthodox views, he earned his living grinding lenses.

Thought

Spinoza's monism denies a transcendent creator: God is not a person who chooses, but the infinite substance of which everything is a necessary expression. Human bondage, in his ethics, is slavery to passive emotions caused by inadequate ideas; freedom comes through understanding necessity, culminating in the 'intellectual love of God'. His Theological-Political Treatise (1670) pioneered historical criticism of the Bible and defended freedom of thought and democratic government. Long reviled as an atheist, he was later celebrated by the German Romantics and remains central to debates on pantheism and determinism.
