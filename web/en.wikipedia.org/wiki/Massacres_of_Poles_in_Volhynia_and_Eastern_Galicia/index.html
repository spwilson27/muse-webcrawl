Massacres of Poles in Volhynia and Eastern Galicia

The massacres of Poles in Volhynia and Eastern Galicia were a series of mass killings carried out in German-occupied Poland from 1943 to 1945 by the Ukrainian Insurgent Army (UPA), with support in places from Ukrainian peasants, against the Polish population of the regions of Volhynia and Eastern Galicia. The campaign is widely described by historians as ethnic cleansing, and by the Polish parliament in 2016 as genocide. Estimates of Polish victims range from about 50,000 to 100,000. Retaliatory attacks by Polish units killed an estimated 10,000–15,000 Ukrainians.

Background

Volhynia and Galicia, in interwar Poland, had mixed Polish, Ukrainian and Jewish populations and a history of tension over land, language and national status. Polish state policies in the 1930s alienated many Ukrainians, while the Organisation of Ukrainian Nationalists (OUN) pursued an integral nationalist programme. The destruction of the region's Jewish communities in the Holocaust of 1941–42, Nazi and Soviet violence, and the collapse of state authority created the conditions for inter-ethnic war.

The massacres

Attacks on Polish villages began in early 1943 in Volhynia under the OUN-B faction and its military arm, the UPA, commanded in the region by Dmytro Klyachkivsky. The violence peaked on the weekend of 11–12 July 1943 — remembered in Poland as "Bloody Sunday" — when UPA units attacked scores of Polish settlements in a coordinated operation. Villages were surrounded and inhabitants murdered, often with farm tools and axes; churches were attacked during Mass. In 1944 the campaign spread into Eastern Galicia. Most surviving Poles fled west or were evacuated.

Polish self-defence and reprisals

Poles organised self-defence bases, some supported by the Home Army; the 27th Home Army Infantry Division was formed in Volhynia in 1944. Polish forces and partisans also carried out reprisal attacks on Ukrainian villages, killing thousands of Ukrainian civilians, a cycle that continued in postwar Poland with events such as the Pawłokoma massacre and, in 1947, Operation Vistula deportations.

Memory and historiography

The massacres were suppressed in communist-era memory and became a major subject of Polish–Ukrainian historical debate after 1989. Poland's Institute of National Remembrance investigates and documents the crimes; in 2016 the Sejm designated 11 July a day of remembrance and described the events as genocide. Ukraine, where the UPA is honoured in many quarters as a liberation movement, has contested the terminology while many Ukrainian historians acknowledge the mass killings of Polish civilians as war crimes and ethnic cleansing.
