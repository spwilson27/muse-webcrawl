Food processing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Sacks of milled flour and grain at a traditional mill]

Food processing is the transformation of raw agricultural products into food, or of one form of food into another: milling grain, fermenting milk into cheese, curing meat, canning, freezing, extruding, emulsifying. Processing is as old as cooking itself — bread, cheese, wine, tofu and sausage are all processed foods — and as modern as the snack extruder. Its purposes are preservation, safety, convenience, flavour and transportability. Food scientists classify processing by degree: public-health frameworks such as NOVA distinguish unprocessed foods, culinary ingredients, processed foods, and ultra-processed formulations built largely from refined substances and additives.

History

Milling separated bran from flour in the ancient world; roller milling (1870s) made white flour cheap and universal — and removed nutrients that public-health law later forced millers to restore. The 19th century industrialised preservation: canning after Appert, condensed milk, margarine (1869, Hippolyte Mège-Mouriès, answering Napoleon III's call for a cheap butter substitute), and pasteurisation. Hydrogenation hardened liquid oils into shortening (Crisco, 1911) — and decades later its trans fats were regulated away. Extrusion cooking mid-20th century gave breakfast cereals and puffed snacks their shapes; HACCP food-safety management, designed with NASA to keep astronauts' food safe, became the global standard system.

The modern debate

Processing made food safe, cheap and abundant — and is now scrutinised for its far end of the spectrum. Ultra-processed foods dominate calorie intake in countries like the US and UK and are associated in study after study with obesity and disease, though the mechanisms (energy density, speed of eating, additives, formulation) remain argued. Food processing is thus two stories at once: millennia of ingenuity that preserved harvests and prevented hunger, and a modern industry whose most profitable products are engineered to be eaten past fullness.
