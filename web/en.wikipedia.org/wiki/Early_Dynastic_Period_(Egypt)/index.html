The Narmer Palette (c. 3100 BC), symbolising the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt

The Early Dynastic Period of ancient Egypt (usually dated c. 3150 – c. 2686 BC) immediately follows the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt and comprises the First and Second Dynasties. It is generally taken to begin with the reign of King Narmer and to end with the reign of Khasekhemwy, after which the Old Kingdom begins. During this formative period the institutions of pharaonic kingship, Egyptian writing, administration and monumental royal burial were established.

Unification

According to later Egyptian tradition the first king was Menes, credited with uniting the Two Lands. Most Egyptologists identify Menes with either Narmer or his successor Hor-Aha. The famous Narmer Palette, found at Hierakonpolis, depicts the king wearing the crowns of both Upper and Lower Egypt and triumphing over enemies, and is often read as a commemoration of the unification. The early capital was at Thinis, near Abydos, though Memphis, founded at the junction of the Two Lands, became the administrative centre.

First Dynasty (c. 3100–2890 BC)

The First Dynasty kings — Narmer, Hor-Aha, Djer, Djet, Den, Anedjib, Semerkhet and Qa'a — ruled from Thinis and were buried in monumental mud-brick tombs at Umm el-Qa'ab, Abydos. The reign of Den is particularly well documented: innovations include the first use of the title "King of Upper and Lower Egypt", the earliest known depiction of a king wearing the Double Crown, and administrative advances recorded on year labels. The royal tombs were accompanied by subsidiary burials of retainers, a practice abandoned by the end of the dynasty. Tomb U-j at Abydos, slightly earlier, yielded some of the earliest examples of Egyptian writing on bone and ivory tags.

Second Dynasty (c. 2890–2686 BC)

The Second Dynasty opened with Hotepsekhemwy, Raneb and Nynetjer. Later rulers include Peribsen, who set the Seth animal above his serekh in place of the Horus falcon — a change some scholars link to religious or political conflict — and Khasekhemwy, who used both Horus and Seth and appears to have reunified the country. Khasekhemwy built the great mud-brick enclosure at Hierakonpolis and the "Shunet el-Zebib" at Abydos, and his statue base records tens of thousands of northern rebels slain. He is regarded as the last king of the period; his son Djoser inaugurates the Third Dynasty and the Old Kingdom.

Culture and administration

During the Early Dynastic Period hieroglyphic writing matured from labels and pot marks into continuous texts; the civil service developed titles such as chancellor and treasurer; the biennial cattle census tracked taxation; and craftsmen produced masterpieces in ivory, ebony, copper and stone, including the schist palette tradition and fine flint knives. Trade reached the Levant (Byblos cedar) and Nubia. Royal ideology — the king as embodiment of Horus, guarantor of maat (order) — took the form it would retain for three thousand years.

Chronology

Radiocarbon studies published in 2013 by Michael Dee and colleagues, modelling Bayesian dates from hundreds of samples, placed the accession of Hor-Aha at roughly 3085–3115 BC and suggested the First Dynasty lasted some 200 years, broadly confirming the conventional chronology derived from king lists such as the Palermo Stone annals.
