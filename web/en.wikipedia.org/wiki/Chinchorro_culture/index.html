Chinchorro culture

The Chinchorro culture of South America was a preceramic culture that lasted from approximately 7,000 to 1,500 BCE. Its people were sedentary fishermen inhabiting the Pacific coastal region of the Atacama Desert in present-day northern Chile and southern Peru. The culture is famous for the Chinchorro mummies, the oldest examples of artificially mummified human remains in the world, predating the mummies of ancient Egypt by some two thousand years.

Settlement and subsistence

The Chinchorro lived in small settlements of circular semi-subterranean huts near the mouths of the valleys and ravines that cross the desert to the sea, notably around Arica and Camarones in Chile and Ilo in Peru. Although they lacked agriculture and pottery, they were sedentary because of the rich marine resources of the Humboldt Current: fish, shellfish, sea birds and sea lions, supplemented by wild plants. Their fishing technology included hooks made from shell, cactus spines and bone, harpoons, and nets weighted with stone sinkers; they also produced fine coiled baskets and reed mats.

Mummification

Around 5,000 BCE the Chinchorro began artificially mummifying their dead — children, including foetuses, as well as adults. Over time the techniques varied. In the "black mummy" technique, the body was disassembled, the flesh removed and the bones treated, the skeleton reassembled and reinforced with sticks, the body remodelled with clay and skin, and the whole coated with a black manganese paste, with a clay mask painted over the face. Later "red mummies" were coated in red ochre, and still later bodies were simply coated in mud. Remarkably, mummification was applied to all members of society regardless of age or status.

Scholars have suggested that the practice may relate to the high levels of arsenic in the local water, which caused high infant mortality and spontaneous abortion; elaborate treatment of dead infants may have been a communal response to grief.

Recognition

The Chinchorro mummies were first described scientifically by the German archaeologist Max Uhle in the early 20th century. In 2021 the UNESCO World Heritage List inscribed the "Settlement and Artificial Mummification of the Chinchorro Culture in the Arica and Parinacota Region", covering the sites of Faldeo Norte del Morro de Arica, Colón 10 and the Museo de Sitio Colón 10, along with the Camarones sites. Collections of Chinchorro mummies are held by the Museo Arqueológico San Miguel de Azapa in Arica.
