Body painting

Body painting is a form of body art where artwork is painted directly onto the human skin using temporary paints and pigments. Unlike tattooing, body painting is temporary, lasting from a few hours to several weeks depending on the medium. The practice has ancient roots: Indigenous peoples around the world have decorated their bodies with clay, charcoal, ochre and plant dyes for ritual, ceremonial, hunting and social purposes for thousands of years.

Traditional forms

Mehndi, the application of henna paste in intricate designs, is worn by brides across South Asia, the Middle East and North Africa. Aboriginal Australian peoples use ochre designs expressing Dreaming stories; Māori moko, though properly a form of tattooing, shares the tradition of meaningful body decoration; and many African, Amazonian and Pacific cultures paint the body for dances, rites of passage and mourning.

Modern revival

Body painting re-entered Western popular culture in the 1960s and grew into a festival art, with events such as the World Bodypainting Festival in Austria. Trompe-l'œil works that blend models into backgrounds or landscapes are widely shared in photography and media. Face painting is its most mainstream branch, common at fairs and sporting events, where fans wear team colours. Airbrush and special cosmetic-grade paints are now standard; only skin-safe, non-toxic products should be used.
