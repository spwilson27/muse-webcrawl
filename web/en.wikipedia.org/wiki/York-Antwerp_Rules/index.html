York Antwerp Rules

The York Antwerp Rules are a set of standardised rules governing general average in maritime law — the principle that when a voluntary sacrifice of ship or cargo, or extraordinary expenditure, is made in time of peril to save the whole maritime adventure, the resulting loss is shared proportionally among all parties with property at risk (shipowner, cargo owners, and freight interests).

History

The principle of general average is ancient, descending from Rhodian sea law through Roman law and medieval codes, but national rules diverged widely. International efforts at uniformity began at a conference in York in 1864 and continued at Antwerp in 1877, producing the first "York-Antwerp Rules." They were revised a number of times — notably in 1890, 1924, 1950, 1974, 1990, 1994, 2004, and 2016 — under the stewardship of the Comité Maritime International (CMI). The rules have no force of law by themselves; they apply because bills of lading, charterparties, and marine insurance policies incorporate them by contract.

Content

The rules consist of lettered rules (A–G) of general principle and numbered rules (I–XXIII) addressing specific situations. Rule A defines a general average act: an extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure "intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure." Specific rules cover jettison of cargo, extinguishing fire on shipboard, voluntary stranding, salvage remuneration, port-of-refuge expenses, wages of crew during detention, and loss or damage by sacrifices. Rule D preserves rights to recover even where the peril arose from a party's fault, without prejudice to defences; Rule Paramount (introduced in 1994) requires that sacrifices and expenditures be reasonable.

Revisions and practice

The 2004 revision, which excluded most salvage remuneration from general average and abolished commission, was poorly received by shipowners and was rarely incorporated, leaving the 1994 rules dominant for two decades. The 2016 revision, adopted at the CMI conference in New York, restored a compromise position and is now widely used. In practice, after a casualty the shipowner declares general average, cargo interests provide average bonds and guarantees before delivery of their goods, and a professional average adjuster prepares the statement apportioning contributions.
