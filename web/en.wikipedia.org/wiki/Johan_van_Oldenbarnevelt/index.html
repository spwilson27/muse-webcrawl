Johan van Oldenbarnevelt (14 September 1547 – 13 May 1619) was a Dutch statesman and jurist who played a key role in the Dutch struggle for independence from Spain. As Land's Advocate of Holland from 1586 until his death, he was effectively the political leader of the Dutch Republic, working in partnership — and ultimately in fatal conflict — with the stadtholder Maurice of Orange.

Career

Oldenbarnevelt served William the Silent and, after William's assassination in 1584, engineered Maurice's appointment as stadtholder while retaining political direction himself. He negotiated the alliances with France and England that sustained the revolt, and in 1602 he merged the competing Dutch trading companies into the Dutch East India Company (VOC). In 1609, against Maurice's wishes, he concluded the Twelve Years' Truce with Spain, which effectively recognised Dutch independence.

Downfall and execution

During the truce, religious conflict between Remonstrant (Arminian) and Counter-Remonstrant (Gomarist) factions became a struggle for power between Oldenbarnevelt's States party and Maurice. When Oldenbarnevelt authorised Holland's towns to raise their own troops (the Sharp Resolution of 1617), Maurice answered with a coup: Oldenbarnevelt and his allies, including Hugo Grotius, were arrested in August 1618. After a trial before a special court widely regarded as politically rigged, the 71-year-old statesman was beheaded outside the Binnenhof in The Hague on 13 May 1619. His reputed last words to the executioner were: "Make it short, make it short."

Legacy

Oldenbarnevelt is regarded as one of the founders of the Dutch state — a pragmatic, tireless administrator who built the Republic's finances, diplomacy and colonial trade. His execution remains a byword for judicial murder in Dutch history, and the conflict between his republican "regent" tradition and the Orangist stadtholders shaped Dutch politics for two centuries.
