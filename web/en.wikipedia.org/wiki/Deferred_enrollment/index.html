Deferred enrollment

Deferred enrollment, also known as deferred admission or a deferral, is an arrangement under which a student who has been admitted to a college or university postpones the start of their studies, typically for one year, while retaining their place. It is closely associated with the practice of taking a gap year between secondary school and higher education.

Many admitted students defer enrollment to take a gap year

Practice

Policies vary by institution and country. In the United States, many colleges allow admitted first-year students to request a deferral, usually by a deadline in the summer before matriculation and often conditional on the student not enrolling full-time at another institution in the meantime. In the United Kingdom, applicants apply through UCAS and may select a deferred-entry option at the point of application, particularly for oversubscribed courses.

Reasons

Students defer for many reasons: to travel, to work and save money, to undertake voluntary service or an internship, to complete military service, or for health and family reasons. In some countries, such as Israel and South Korea, compulsory military service makes deferral effectively automatic for many students. Business schools offering deferred enrollment programs, such as MBA programs that admit final-year undergraduates for entry after two or more years of work experience, use the mechanism to secure candidates early.

Effects

Admissions offices generally report that students who defer return with greater maturity and motivation, and surveys of gap-year participants frequently report positive effects on academic engagement. Deferred enrollment can, however, complicate institutional planning for class size and housing, and students who defer may lose momentum or decide not to enroll at all.
