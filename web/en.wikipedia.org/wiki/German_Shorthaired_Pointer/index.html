German Shorthaired Pointer

The German Shorthaired Pointer is a medium to large breed of pointing dog developed in 19th-century Germany for hunting. A versatile gun dog, it is expected to point game, retrieve on land and in water, and track wounded game — a "complete" hunter's dog. The breed remains one of the most popular sporting dogs in the world and is also widely kept as an energetic family companion.

History

German hunters in the 1800s set out to create a single all-purpose dog from the old German pointer, itself descended from Spanish pointers and scent hounds, crossing in English Pointers and other breeds for speed, nose, and style. Prince Albrecht zu Solms-Braunfels was an influential patron of the breed's standardization. A studbook was established and the breed was formally recognized in Germany in the 1870s. The first German Shorthaired Pointers reached the United States in the 1920s, and the breed was recognized by the American Kennel Club in 1930. In Germany the breeding club (Deutsch-Kurzhaar-Verband) still requires performance testing in field and water work.

Appearance

The breed is athletic and cleanly built, with a short, flat-lying coat that sheds moderately. The coat is solid liver (a deep reddish brown) or liver with white ticking and patches. The head is clean-cut with a long muzzle, the ears are broad and pendant, the eyes brown, and the tail is traditionally docked in working contexts where legal, carried horizontally when the dog is on point.

Temperament and use

German Shorthaired Pointers are intelligent, eager, and intensely energetic, bred to hunt all day in varied terrain. They form strong bonds with people and are generally friendly, but their exercise and mental stimulation needs are high, and under-exercised dogs may become destructive. In the field they search with a ground-covering gallop, freeze into the classic point on finding game birds, retrieve shot game including waterfowl from cold water, and can follow blood trails of wounded deer. The breed also competes in field trials, agility, and other dog sports, and is used for detection and search work.

Health

Generally robust, the breed can be prone to hip dysplasia, gastric torsion (bloat), certain cancers, entropion, and von Willebrand disease. Responsible breeders screen breeding stock for hips, eyes, and cardiac conditions.
