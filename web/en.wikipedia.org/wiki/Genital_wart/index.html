Genital warts
Specialty: Infectious disease, dermatology
Cause: Human papillomavirus (HPV types 6 and 11)
Transmission: Sexual contact
Prevention: HPV vaccine, condoms

Genital warts are a sexually transmitted infection caused by certain types of human papillomavirus (HPV). They are generally pink in color and project out from the surface of the skin. Usually they cause few symptoms, but can occasionally be itchy or cause a burning sensation. Without treatment, they usually resolve within 6 months to a year but can recur; genital warts are caused most commonly by HPV types 6 and 11.

Signs and symptoms

Genital warts appear as small, flesh-colored bumps or clusters of bumps in the genital and anal areas. In women they can occur on the vulva, cervix, and walls of the vagina; in men on the penis, scrotum, and around the anus. They may have a cauliflower-like appearance when clustered, and range from lesions too small to see to large masses.

Cause and diagnosis

HPV is spread by direct skin-to-skin contact, most often during vaginal, anal, or oral sex with an infected partner. Diagnosis is typically by visual inspection of the lesions; a vinegar (acetic acid) test can make lesions more visible, and biopsy may be used in atypical cases. The HPV types causing genital warts differ from the high-risk types (such as 16 and 18) associated with cervical and other cancers.

Treatment and prevention

Treatments include topical medications such as imiquimod and podophyllotoxin, and procedures such as cryotherapy, electrocautery, surgical excision, and laser treatment. Treatment removes visible warts but may not eliminate the underlying viral infection, so recurrence is common. The HPV vaccine (such as Gardasil) protects against types 6 and 11 as well as high-risk types and is recommended before sexual debut; condom use reduces but does not eliminate risk of transmission.
