Musée d'Orsay

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Musée d'Orsay's great hall under the barrel-vaulted glass roof, the golden station clock presiding over the sculpture nave]

The Musée d'Orsay is a museum in Paris, installed in the former Gare d'Orsay, a Beaux-Arts railway station on the Left Bank of the Seine opposite the Tuileries. Opened as a museum in 1986, it holds mainly French art dating from 1848 to 1914 — the world's largest and finest collection of Impressionist and post-Impressionist painting, including masterpieces by Monet, Manet, Degas, Renoir, Cézanne, Seurat, Gauguin and Van Gogh — together with sculpture, photography, furniture and decorative arts of the period.

The station, designed by Victor Laloux for the 1900 Exposition Universelle, served southwestern France until 1939; its conversion (architect Gae Aulenti among the designers of the interior) preserved the great train hall as the museum's spine.

The collection

Orsay was created to relieve the Louvre and the Jeu de Paume, telling the story of the half-century when Paris invented modern art: from Courbet's realism and the Salon system, through Impressionism's break, to Van Gogh's final rooms (the museum holds his Starry Night Over the Rhône, portraits and the Church at Auvers). The central nave's sculpture — academic marbles below, Rodin and the moderns above — makes the argument in three dimensions.

Visiting

Among the five most-visited art museums in the world (attendance around three to four million in strong years), Orsay is the essential complement to the Louvre across the river: where the Louvre ends in 1848, Orsay begins. The restored station clock and the restaurant in the former station hotel's dining room are attractions in themselves.
