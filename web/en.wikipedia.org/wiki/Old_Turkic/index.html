Old Turkic (also rendered Old Turkish) is the earliest attested form of the Turkic languages, written in the Old Turkic runiform script and, later, the Old Uyghur alphabet. It is known from inscriptions and manuscripts dating from the 7th to the 13th centuries found across Mongolia, Siberia and Central Asia.

Corpus

The most famous monuments are the Orkhon inscriptions of the Second Turkic Khaganate — the stelae of Tonyukuk (c. 720s), Kül Tigin (732) and Bilge Khagan (735) — discovered in Mongolia's Orkhon Valley in 1889 and deciphered by Vilhelm Thomsen in 1893. Later texts, chiefly from the Uyghur Khaganate and the Qocho kingdom, include Buddhist, Manichaean and Christian religious works, legal documents and the poem-filled manuscript tradition preserved at Turfan and Dunhuang.

Features

Old Turkic was an agglutinative language with vowel harmony, no grammatical gender, and a rich system of derivational and inflectional suffixes. It is not the direct ancestor of modern Turkish of Turkey (which descends from Old Anatolian Turkish, a western Oghuz variety); its closest modern relatives are among the Siberian Turkic languages. The runiform script of 38 or so characters, written right to left, was probably adapted from Aramaic-derived models via Sogdian intermediaries.
