Amano in 2014.

Hiroshi Amano (born 11 September 1960) is a Japanese physicist and inventor specialising in semiconductor technology. As Isamu Akasaki's doctoral student at Nagoya University, he developed the low-temperature buffer-layer technique (1986) that allowed high-quality gallium nitride crystals to grow on sapphire, and discovered how to activate p-type GaN (1989) — the achievements that, with Akasaki and Shuji Nakamura, earned the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics for the blue LED.

Amano has continued at Nagoya to lead GaN power electronics — high-efficiency transistors for power conversion that promise large energy savings in vehicles, data centres and grids — and deep-ultraviolet LEDs for sterilisation. The blue LED story, in which a student persisted with a material the world had discarded, has been an inspiration in Japanese engineering education.

The GaN breakthroughs

GaN cracks and defects had defeated industrial laboratories for twenty years. Amano's aluminium-nitride buffer layer, deposited at low temperature before the main crystal growth, tamed the lattice mismatch with sapphire; then, noticing that zinc-doped GaN brightened under electron microscopy, he and Akasaki realised the electron beam was activating the acceptors (by driving out hydrogen) — the first reproducible p-type GaN, completing the p–n junction Nakamura later exploited at Nichia.
