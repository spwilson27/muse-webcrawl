Silver chloride (AgCl) is a chemical compound with a white crystalline appearance, known for its low solubility in water and its sensitivity to light. It occurs naturally as the mineral chlorargyrite. Silver chloride has long been used in photography, in reference electrodes for electrochemistry, and as an antiseptic and laboratory reagent.

Silver chloride precipitate, formed by mixing solutions of silver nitrate and sodium chloride.

Preparation and solubility

Silver chloride is readily synthesized by mixing aqueous solutions of silver nitrate and sodium chloride, whereupon it precipitates as a white curdy solid: AgNO3 + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO3. This reaction is a classic qualitative test for chloride ions. The compound is unusual in that its solubility increases in the presence of excess chloride, ammonia, thiosulfate, or cyanide, owing to the formation of soluble silver complexes such as [AgCl2]− and [Ag(NH3)2]+. In concentrated hydrochloric acid it likewise becomes more soluble.

Photochemistry

Like silver bromide, silver chloride darkens on exposure to light as it is photochemically reduced to metallic silver, liberating chlorine. This property was central to early photographic processes and to printing-out papers, in which the image appears during exposure without chemical development. Molten silver chloride is also used to make infrared-transmitting optics and the "horn silver" electrodes of electrochemistry.

Applications

The silver/silver-chloride electrode is one of the most common reference electrodes in pH measurement and electrochemistry because of its stability and reproducibility. Silver chloride is also used in photochromic lenses, in bandages and wound dressings for its antimicrobial action, as a catalyst, and in the production of pottery glazes.
