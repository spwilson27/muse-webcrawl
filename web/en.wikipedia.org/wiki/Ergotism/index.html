Ergotism

Ergotism (pronounced /ˈɜːrɡətˌɪzəm/) is the effect of long-term ergot poisoning, traditionally due to the ingestion of the alkaloids produced by the fungus Claviceps purpurea — which infects rye and other cereals — and more recently by excessive use of ergotamine-derived medicines. The condition was known in the Middle Ages as St Anthony's fire, after the monastic Order of St Anthony, whose hospitals specialised in treating its victims and whose relics sufferers sought for a cure.

Symptoms and forms

Ergotism occurs in two classic forms. Gangrenous ergotism results from the potent vasoconstrictor action of ergot alkaloids: blood supply to the extremities fails, causing burning pain ("the fire"), swelling, and eventually dry gangrene and loss of fingers, toes, or limbs — sufferers sometimes described limbs as charred. Convulsive ergotism affects the central nervous system, with crawling sensations under the skin (formication), muscle spasms, convulsions, hallucinations, and mania. Diarrhoea, nausea, and headache accompany both forms.

History

Epidemics swept Europe repeatedly from the early Middle Ages onward, often following wet harvests that favoured the fungus and in regions where rye bread was the staple of the poor; a documented outbreak in Aquitaine in 994 is said to have killed tens of thousands, and the last major European epidemic struck France in 1951 at Pont-Saint-Esprit, where contaminated flour poisoned hundreds, though that incident's cause has been debated. Some historians have proposed — controversially — that ergot poisoning contributed to episodes of mass behaviour attributed to witchcraft, including the Salem witch trials of 1692–93.

Cause and modern relevance

The ergot alkaloids include ergotamine and lysergic acid derivatives — Albert Hofmann synthesised LSD from lysergic acid at Sandoz in 1938 while investigating ergot compounds. Modern grain cleaning and legal limits on ergot sclerotia in flour have nearly eliminated epidemic ergotism, but the condition still occurs from overuse of ergotamine migraine medication, where it can cause limb ischaemia, and in livestock grazing infected pasture. Treatment involves stopping the exposure and vasodilator therapy; gangrenous tissue may require surgery.
