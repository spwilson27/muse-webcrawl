Ground (electricity)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The earth symbol (⏚) used in circuit diagrams

In electrical engineering, ground or earth may be a reference point in an electrical circuit from which voltages are measured, a common return path for electric current, or a direct physical connection to the Earth.

Electrical circuits may be connected to ground for several reasons. Exposed conductive parts of electrical equipment are connected to ground to protect users from electrical shock hazards; if internal insulation fails, dangerous voltages must not persist on accessible parts. Connections to ground limit the build-up of static electricity when handling flammable products or electrostatic-sensitive devices. In some telegraph and power transmission circuits, the earth itself can be used as one conductor of the circuit, saving the cost of installing a separate return conductor.

History

Long-distance electromagnetic telegraph systems from the 1820s onwards used two or more wires to carry the signal and return currents. It was then discovered that the return current could be carried through the earth, substantially reducing installation costs. Electromagnetic interference from these systems drove the adoption of twisted-pair and balanced-line practices in telephony.

Power systems

In a mains electricity (AC power) wiring installation, the grounding conductor provides a path to earth of low resistance, so that in the event of a fault a large current flows, tripping a breaker or blowing a fuse and disconnecting the faulty circuit. Earthing systems are classified in the IEC 60364 standard with designations such as TN, TT and IT, according to how the supply source and exposed conductive parts are connected to earth.

Electronics

In electronic circuit design, "ground" is often an abstract reference node assigned zero potential, from which all other voltages are measured; it need not have any physical connection to the Earth. Signal grounds, chassis grounds, and earth grounds are distinguished to manage noise, safety, and electromagnetic compatibility. Poor grounding practice can create ground loops, a common source of hum and interference in audio and measurement systems.

Lightning protection

Lightning protection systems rely on low-impedance earth connections to dissipate the energy of a lightning strike safely into the ground, using arrays of driven rods, buried conductors, or foundation electrodes.
