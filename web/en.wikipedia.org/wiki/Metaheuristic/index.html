Metaheuristic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In computer science and mathematical optimization, a metaheuristic is a higher-level procedure or heuristic designed to find, generate, tune, or select a heuristic (partial search algorithm) that may provide a sufficiently good solution to an optimization problem, especially with incomplete or imperfect information or limited computation capacity. Metaheuristics sample a subset of solutions which is otherwise too large to be completely enumerated or otherwise explored.

Metaheuristics are generally applied to problems for which no satisfactory problem-specific algorithm exists, or where exact methods are computationally infeasible, such as NP-hard combinatorial problems like the travelling salesman problem, scheduling, and vehicle routing. They do not guarantee an optimal solution. The term was coined by Fred W. Glover in 1986 in connection with tabu search.

Common metaheuristics

  • Simulated annealing — probabilistic single-solution search inspired by metallurgical annealing.
  • Genetic algorithms and other evolutionary algorithms — populations evolve via selection, crossover, and mutation.
  • Ant colony optimization — agents lay and follow pheromone trails.
  • Particle swarm optimization — candidate solutions move through the search space influenced by their own and the swarm's best positions.
  • Tabu search — local search with memory structures forbidding recently visited solutions.
  • GRASP and variable neighbourhood search.

Properties

Metaheuristics typically trade exploration (diversification) against exploitation (intensification), use stochastic components, and have parameters that must be tuned. The no free lunch theorems show that, averaged over all problems, no metaheuristic outperforms any other, so success depends on matching the method and its operators to the structure of the problem at hand.
