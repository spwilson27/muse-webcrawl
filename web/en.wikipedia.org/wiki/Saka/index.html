Saka

The Saka were a nomadic Iranian people of the Eurasian Steppe who spoke Eastern Iranian languages and flourished from roughly the 8th century BC to the early centuries AD. Known in Old Persian inscriptions as Sakā, in Greek sources as related to the Scythians, and in Chinese records as the Sai, the Saka occupied territories spanning present-day Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Xinjiang and parts of Siberia.

Groups and history

Achaemenid inscriptions distinguish several Saka groups, including the Sakā haumavargā ("haoma-drinking Saka") and the Sakā tigraxaudā ("Saka with pointed hats"). Saka warriors served in Persian armies and fought against Alexander the Great in Central Asia. From the 2nd century BC, migrations pushed Saka groups southward into Bactria, the Hindu Kush and northwestern India, where the Indo-Scythian kingdoms were founded by rulers such as Maues and Azes. Other Saka kingdoms flourished in Khotan and Tumshuq on the southern Tarim Basin, where Khotanese Saka documents were written.

Culture

The Saka were renowned horse riders, archers and metalworkers. Kurgans (burial mounds) such as the Issyk burial in Kazakhstan, which yielded the "Golden Warrior" in a pointed helmet and gold-trimmed costume, reveal their animal-style art in gold and bronze. Their languages, attested mainly in Khotanese and Tumshuqese Buddhist texts, belong to the Eastern Middle Iranian family. The Saka-era calendar term and the Sanskrit Śaka era of India preserve their memory, as do the names of regions such as Sakastan (Sistan).
