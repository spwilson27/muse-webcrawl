Goncourt brothers

The Goncourt brothers were Edmond de Goncourt (1822–1896) and Jules de Goncourt (1830–1870), French writers who collaborated as a single literary partnership for most of their careers. They are remembered for their novels, their pioneering works of art history and social history, and above all for their Journal, a vast diary of Parisian literary and artistic life in the second half of the 19th century.

Life and work

Born into a comfortably wealthy family, the brothers devoted themselves from 1849 to art, history and literature. Inseparable in life — they signed their works jointly simply "Edmond and Jules de Goncourt" — they wrote studies of 18th-century French society and art, including Histoire de la société française pendant la Révolution (1854), L'Art du XVIIIe siècle, and biographies of Madame de Pompadour and Marie Antoinette.

Their novels, including Charles Demailly (1860), Sœur Philomène (1861), Renée Mauperin (1864), Germinie Lacerteux (1865), Manette Salomon (1867) and Madame Gervaisais (1869), were founded on close observation and extensive documentation, and helped to lay the groundwork for literary naturalism, which Émile Zola would develop further. Germinie Lacerteux, based on the double life of their own servant, is considered their finest novel.

The Journal and the Prix Goncourt

The brothers began their Journal in 1851; after Jules's death in 1870, Edmond continued it alone until 1896. Witty, malice-laced and rich in detail, it portrays writers and artists such as Flaubert, Gautier, Sainte-Beuve, Turgenev and Zola, and remains a primary source for the period.

In his will Edmond founded the Académie Goncourt, which each year since 1903 has awarded the Prix Goncourt, now France's most prestigious literary prize for prose fiction.
