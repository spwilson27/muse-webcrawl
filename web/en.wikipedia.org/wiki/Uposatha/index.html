Uposatha (Pali: uposatha; Sanskrit: upavasatha) is a Buddhist day of observance, held on the days of the full moon, new moon and the two quarter moons of each lunar month — roughly once a week. On uposatha days, lay followers intensify their practice by taking the Eight Precepts, while monks gather to recite the Patimokkha, the code of monastic discipline, in a ceremony that includes communal confession of offenses.

Origins

The observance derives from pre-Buddhist Indian practice, in which religious practitioners fasted and gathered on lunar observance days. The Buddha adopted the custom for the Sangha, instituting the fortnightly recitation of the monastic rules on the full and new moon days.

Practice

For laypeople, uposatha observance usually involves visiting a monastery, offering food to the monks, listening to Dhamma talks, meditating, and keeping the Eight Precepts — abstaining from killing, stealing, sexual activity, false speech, intoxicants, eating after midday, entertainments and adornment, and luxurious beds. In Theravada countries such as Thailand, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Cambodia and Laos, the full-moon uposatha (such as Vesak) may be a public holiday.

For monks, the central rite is the recitation of the 227 rules of the Theravada Patimokkha (or the corresponding number in other Vinaya traditions) from memory, preceded by confession. A chapter of at least four monks is required for a valid ceremony.

Calendrical significance

Major Buddhist festivals are fixed to uposatha days: Vesak (the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and death) falls on a full moon, as do Magha Puja and Asalha Puja. The three-month rains retreat, Vassa, begins and ends on full-moon observance days.
