Speech recognition

Speech recognition is an interdisciplinary subfield of computer science and computational linguistics that develops methodologies and technologies enabling the recognition and translation of spoken language into text by computers. It is also known as automatic speech recognition (ASR), computer speech recognition, or speech-to-text (STT).

History

Early systems such as Bell Laboratories' "Audrey" (1952) recognized spoken digits from a single speaker; IBM's Shoebox (1962) understood sixteen words. Research funding in the 1970s, including DARPA's Speech Understanding program, and the adoption of hidden Markov models (HMMs) in the 1980s, enabled continuous-speech systems with growing vocabularies. Consumer dictation software appeared in the 1990s. From about 2010, deep neural networks — first as hybrid DNN–HMM acoustic models and later as end-to-end architectures based on connectionist temporal classification, attention, and Transformers — dramatically reduced word error rates, and systems such as large multilingual models trained on hundreds of thousands of hours of audio approach human parity on clean read and conversational benchmarks.

Methods

A recognizer typically extracts acoustic features (historically mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, now usually log-mel filterbanks), applies an acoustic model scoring how likely sounds correspond to phones or subword units, and combines this with a language model capturing word-sequence probabilities, searching for the most likely transcription. Performance is measured by word error rate (WER), the proportion of substitutions, deletions, and insertions relative to a reference transcript.

Applications

Applications include voice assistants (Siri, Google Assistant, Alexa), dictation and transcription services, automatic captioning, call-centre analytics, voice control in vehicles and accessibility tools, and hands-free interfaces in medicine, logistics, and industry.
