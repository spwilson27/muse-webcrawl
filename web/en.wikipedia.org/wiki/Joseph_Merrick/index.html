Joseph Merrick

Born5 August 1862, Leicester, England
Died11 April 1890, Whitechapel, London, England
Known forSevere physical deformities; exhibited as the "Elephant Man"

Joseph Carey Merrick (5 August 1862 – 11 April 1890) was an English man known for his severe physical deformities. Exhibited at freak shows as the "Elephant Man", he later became a resident of the London Hospital in Whitechapel, where the surgeon Frederick Treves cared for him and where he was visited by members of Victorian high society, including Alexandra, Princess of Wales.

Early life

Merrick was born in Leicester, apparently healthy, but from about the age of five tumours and bony growths progressively distorted his head, right arm, hands and feet, and thickened his skin. His mother died when he was eleven; rejected by his father and stepmother, he worked as a hawker and in a cigar factory, and spent time in the Leicester workhouse before agreeing in 1884 to be exhibited by showmen in England and on the Continent.

At the London Hospital

Robbed and abandoned in Brussels, Merrick returned to London in 1886 and came to the attention of Treves, who had examined him two years earlier. After a public appeal raised funds, Merrick was given permanent rooms at the London Hospital. There he read widely, wrote letters and poetry, built an intricate cardboard model of a church, and received fashionable visitors. He converted to Christianity and took trips to the countryside, experiences he treasured.

Death and legacy

Merrick died on 11 April 1890, aged 27; the cause was judged to be asphyxia from the weight of his head as he lay down, possibly an attempt to sleep lying flat "like other people". The precise cause of his condition was long debated — first diagnosed as elephantiasis, then neurofibromatosis — and is now most often attributed to Proteus syndrome. His life inspired Bernard Pomerance's play The Elephant Man (1977) and David Lynch's 1980 film of the same name, in which he is called "John Merrick".
