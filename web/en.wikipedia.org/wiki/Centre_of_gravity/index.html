Centre of gravity

A body balances when supported at its centre of gravity.

In physics, the centre of gravity (CG) of a body is the point at which the entire weight of the body may be considered to act. For a body in a uniform gravitational field, the centre of gravity coincides with the centre of mass. In a non-uniform field, such as that of a planet acting on an orbiting object, the two points can differ slightly.

Definition

The centre of gravity is the average location of the weight of an object. Formally, it is the point where the resultant of the gravitational forces acting on all particles of the body acts, such that the torque due to gravity about that point is zero. For a system of particles with masses mi at positions ri, the centre of gravity in a uniform field is given by the weighted average of the positions, exactly as for the centre of mass.

History

The concept was first studied by the ancient Greek mathematician and engineer Archimedes of Syracuse, whose work On the Equilibrium of Planes determined the centres of gravity of triangles, parallelograms and other plane figures. Archimedes famously remarked that, given a place to stand, he could move the Earth with a lever — a statement resting on the same principles of balance and torque.

Determining the centre of gravity

For a flat object of arbitrary shape, the centre of gravity can be found experimentally by suspending the object from two different points and drawing vertical lines (plumb lines) from each suspension point; the intersection of the lines marks the centre of gravity. Engineers routinely calculate the CG of aircraft, ships and vehicles, since its position relative to the centre of lift or the support base determines stability. In aircraft, the centre of gravity must lie within certified limits for safe flight.
