Exchange rate regime
Main typesFloating, fixed (pegged), intermediate regimes
ClassificationIMF Annual Report on Exchange Arrangements and Exchange Restrictions

An exchange rate regime is the way a monetary authority of a country or currency union manages its currency in relation to other currencies and the foreign exchange market. Regimes range from freely floating rates, determined by market supply and demand, to hard fixed rates, in which a currency's value is rigidly tied to another currency, a basket of currencies, or a commodity such as gold. The choice of regime is a central decision of monetary policy and is constrained by the "impossible trinity": a country cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and an independent monetary policy.

Types of regime

Floating regimes include free floats, in which intervention is limited to moderating excessive volatility, and managed floats, in which the authorities lean against exchange-rate movements without a specific target. Intermediate regimes include soft pegs to a single currency or basket, crawling pegs that adjust gradually, pegged rates within horizontal bands, and stabilised arrangements. Hard pegs include currency boards, which back the domestic monetary base with foreign reserves, and dollarisation or membership of a currency union, in which a foreign currency is used as legal tender. Countries with no separate legal tender are at the most fixed end of the spectrum.

Choice of regime

Smaller and more open economies often peg to gain credibility and stable trading conditions, at the cost of giving up monetary independence. Large economies typically float. Pegged regimes can be vulnerable to speculative attacks and currency crises when the peg becomes inconsistent with economic fundamentals, as in the 1992 ERM crisis and the 1997 Asian financial crisis. The International Monetary Fund classifies members' de facto regimes and publishes them annually.
