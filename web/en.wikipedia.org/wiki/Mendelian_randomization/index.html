Mendelian randomization

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a method in epidemiology that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to assess whether an exposure (such as a biomarker or behaviour) has a causal effect on an outcome (such as a disease). Because genetic variants are assorted randomly at conception — Mendel's laws — they are largely independent of the confounders that plague observational studies, mimicking the design of a randomised controlled trial.

Mendelian randomization

The instrumental-variable logic of MR

Field: epidemiology, causal inference
Key tool: genetic instrumental variables
Assumptions: relevance, independence, exclusion restriction

Assumptions

MR rests on three core assumptions about the genetic instrument: (1) relevance — the variant is robustly associated with the exposure; (2) independence — the variant is not associated with confounders of the exposure–outcome relationship; and (3) the exclusion restriction — the variant affects the outcome only through the exposure (no horizontal pleiotropy). Violations, especially pleiotropy, are the main threat to validity, and a family of sensitivity methods (MR-Egger, weighted median, MR-PRESSO) has been developed to detect and correct for them.

Applications

MR has been widely used with data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS), often in a "two-sample" design combining exposure and outcome data from different cohorts. Landmark applications include showing that LDL cholesterol and lipoprotein(a) causally raise cardiovascular risk, that HDL cholesterol probably does not protect against heart disease despite observational associations, and clarifying causal roles for traits such as alcohol consumption, vitamin D and C-reactive protein.
