Communication theory

[Image: Shannon–Weaver model of communication]

Communication theory is a proposed description of communication phenomena, the relationships among them, a storyline describing these relationships, and an argument for these three elements. Communication theory provides a way of talking about and analyzing the key events, processes, and commitments that together form communication.

The field draws on a long history. Aristotle's Rhetoric analysed persuasion through the modes of ethos, pathos and logos. In the twentieth century the field coalesced around work such as Harold Lasswell's formula "who says what, in which channel, to whom, with what effect" (1948) and Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver's mathematical theory of communication (1948–49), which modelled communication as the transmission of a signal from an information source through a channel subject to noise to a receiver.

Models of communication

Models are commonly grouped into transmission models, which treat communication as the transfer of messages; constitutive or ritual models, following James W. Carey, which treat communication as the creation of shared meaning and culture; and critical models, which emphasise power and ideology. Key theorists include Marshall McLuhan ("the medium is the message"), Jürgen Habermas (communicative action and the public sphere), and Stuart Hall, whose encoding/decoding model (1973) reframed audiences as active interpreters of media texts.

Branches

The discipline is usually divided into areas including interpersonal communication, intrapersonal communication, group and organisational communication, mass communication, intercultural communication, rhetoric, and health and political communication. Related theory has been imported from psychology, sociology, semiotics, information theory and cybernetics.
