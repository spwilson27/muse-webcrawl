T Pyxidis is a recurrent nova and cataclysmic variable star in the constellation Pyxis. It is a close binary in which a white dwarf accretes hydrogen-rich material from a low-mass companion star; every few decades the accumulated layer ignites in a thermonuclear runaway, brightening the system from about magnitude 15 to magnitude 6–7 for several months.

Eruption history

Eruptions were recorded in 1890, 1902, 1920, 1944 and 1966 — intervals of roughly 12 to 24 years led astronomers to expect another around 1986, but none came until 14 April 2011, when amateur astronomers detected the outburst after a 45-year quiet period, the longest known gap for the star. The 2011 eruption was observed across the electromagnetic spectrum, including X-ray and gamma-ray instruments, and revealed complex, slow-moving ejecta.

Distance and supernova question

T Pyxidis has attracted attention because some studies argued it lies only about 3,300 light-years away and that its white dwarf is gaining mass fast enough to eventually explode as a Type Ia supernova; estimates associated with this scenario once suggested a (geologically near-term) event whose effects on Earth were publicly debated. Later astrometry, including analyses of the 2011 ejecta and Gaia-era measurements, generally places the system much farther — on the order of 10,000 light-years or more — and other researchers conclude the white dwarf is actually losing mass over each eruption cycle, ruling out an eventual supernova from this channel. The system's true distance and mass balance remain active research topics.

The surrounding shell

Hubble Space Telescope images show a clumpy shell of some two thousand gas knots around the binary, ejected by successive eruptions and sculpted by the system's winds — one of the most detailed views of a nova remnant.
