Vapor

In physics, a vapor (American English) or vapour (British English) is a substance in the gas phase at a temperature below its critical temperature, meaning that the vapor can be condensed into a liquid merely by increasing the pressure on it, without reducing its temperature. This distinguishes a vapor from a gas above the critical point, which cannot be liquefied by pressure alone.

Properties

A vapor normally coexists with its condensed phase: water vapor in the air, for example, is in dynamic equilibrium with liquid water, and the balance is described by the substance's vapor pressure at that temperature. When a vapor is in equilibrium with its liquid or solid at a given temperature it is called saturated; vapor in excess of equilibrium, such as superheated steam, behaves increasingly like an ideal gas.

The familiar white "steam" seen above a kettle is not itself water vapor — true water vapor is an invisible gas — but a mist of tiny liquid droplets formed as the vapor condenses in cooler air. Aerosols, fog and clouds are similar mixtures of suspended condensed droplets in gas.

Usage of the term

The word covers phenomena from evaporation at a liquid's surface to vaporisation in industrial processes: vapor deposition in materials science, vapour-compression refrigeration in engineering, and electronic-cigarette "vaping", in which a heated propylene-glycol liquid forms an inhalable aerosol. In everyday speech "vapor" is also loosely used for any visible exhalation or fume.
