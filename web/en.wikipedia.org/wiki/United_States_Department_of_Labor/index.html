United States Department of Labor

The United States Department of Labor (DOL) is a Cabinet-level department of the US federal government responsible for occupational safety, wage and hour standards, unemployment insurance benefits, re-employment services and some economic statistics. Its stated purpose is to foster, promote and develop the welfare of wage earners, job seekers and retirees in the United States. It is headed by the Secretary of Labor and headquartered in the Frances Perkins Building in Washington, D.C., employing roughly 15,000–17,000 people.

History

A Bureau of Labor was created within the Department of the Interior in 1884. Labour functions joined commerce in the 1903 Department of Commerce and Labor, before President William Howard Taft signed the act creating a separate Department of Labor on 4 March 1913, his last day in office. Frances Perkins, Secretary from 1933 to 1945 under Franklin D. Roosevelt — the first woman to hold a US Cabinet post — oversaw the department's central role in the New Deal, including Social Security, the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (minimum wage, overtime and child-labour rules) and the National Labor Relations framework.

Agencies

The department's principal agencies include the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA, created 1970), the Wage and Hour Division, the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the Employment and Training Administration, the Employee Benefits Security Administration, the Office of Workers' Compensation Programs, the Mine Safety and Health Administration and the Veterans' Employment and Training Service. The Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs enforces equal-employment obligations of federal contractors.

Through these bodies the DOL enforces more than 180 federal labour laws covering some 150 million workers, publishes the monthly Employment Situation report, and administers federal-state unemployment insurance and workforce training programmes.
