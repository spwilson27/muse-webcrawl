Clever Hans

Clever Hans performing with his owner, Wilhelm von Osten.

Clever Hans (German: der Kluge Hans) was a horse that, in the early twentieth century, was claimed to be able to perform arithmetic and other intellectual tasks. After a formal investigation in 1907, psychologist Oskar Pfungst demonstrated that the horse was not actually calculating: it was responding to involuntary, unconscious cues given off by its questioners. The case gave rise to the term Clever Hans effect, a lasting lesson in experimental psychology about the need for blinded experimental design.

The performances

Hans belonged to Wilhelm von Osten, a retired schoolteacher in Berlin who believed he had taught the horse to count, add, subtract, tell time, read and spell in German, tapping out answers with his hoof. Von Osten exhibited Hans free of charge, and the horse's apparent abilities attracted wide public attention in 1904.

Investigation

A commission of experts, including the psychologist Carl Stumpf, found no evidence of fraud. Stumpf's student Oskar Pfungst then conducted systematic experiments: Hans answered correctly only when the questioner knew the answer and was visible to the horse. Pfungst showed that questioners unconsciously made tiny movements — a slight forward inclination as the tapping approached the right count, relaxing when it was reached — and that Hans had learned to read these cues. Pfungst could himself elicit correct "answers" from the horse by deliberately or unconsciously cueing him.

Legacy

The Clever Hans effect became a foundational cautionary example in the methodology of psychology and animal cognition research, motivating double-blind procedures and automated testing. It is frequently invoked whenever experimental results may be distorted by experimenter expectancy — in contexts from animal studies to evaluations of ostensibly intelligent machines. Later scientists such as Donald Griffin argued that the case, while debunking Hans, also chilled legitimate inquiry into animal minds for decades.
