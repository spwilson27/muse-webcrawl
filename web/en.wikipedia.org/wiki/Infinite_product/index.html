Infinite product

In mathematics, an infinite product is the product of infinitely many factors, defined as the limit of the sequence of partial products. Given a sequence a₁, a₂, a₃, …, the infinite product ∏ an is the limit of PN = a₁a₂···aN as N → ∞, provided this limit exists and is non-zero; if the limit is zero, the product is said to diverge to zero.

Convergence criteria

Writing the factors as 1 + bn, the product ∏(1 + bn) converges (to a non-zero limit) if and only if the series ∑ bn converges, under suitable conditions — in particular when ∑|bn| converges. Taking logarithms converts an infinite product into an infinite series: log ∏(1 + bn) = ∑ log(1 + bn), which is the basis of most convergence tests.

Famous examples

The Wallis product expresses π/2 as a product over all positive integers: (2/1)(2/3)(4/3)(4/5)(6/5)(6/7)⋯. Euler discovered product expansions for the sine function, sin(x) = x∏(1 − x²/(n²π²)), which he used to solve the Basel problem. The Riemann zeta function has an Euler product over the prime numbers, ζ(s) = ∏p (1 − p−s)−1, linking analysis with number theory.

Applications

Infinite products arise in complex analysis (Weierstrass factorization of entire functions), probability theory, and number theory. The q-Pochhammer symbol and related q-series are infinite products central to the theory of partitions.
