Brazing

Torch brazing a copper pipe joint with a filler rod.

Brazing is a metal-joining process in which a filler metal is melted and drawn into a capillary gap between two or more close-fitting parts. The filler metal, which has a lower melting point than the adjoining metal, is brought slightly above its melting (liquidus) temperature while protected by a suitable atmosphere, usually a flux. It then flows over the base metal (a process known as wetting) and is cooled to join the work pieces together. Brazing is similar to soldering but occurs at temperatures above 450 °C (840 °F).

Process

A successful brazed joint requires clean surfaces, close-fitting parts, and a filler alloy that wets the base metals. Common filler metals include silver alloys, copper-phosphorus, brass, nickel alloys and aluminium-silicon alloys. Heating methods include torch, furnace, induction, resistance and dip brazing.

Advantages and applications

Brazing can join dissimilar metals and thin sections without melting the workpieces, and produces neat, strong, leak-tight joints. It is widely used in plumbing and HVAC for copper pipe, in the manufacture of carbide-tipped tools and heat exchangers, and in jewellery making.
