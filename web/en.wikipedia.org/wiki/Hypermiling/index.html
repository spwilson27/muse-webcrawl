Hypermiling

Hypermiling is driving or flying a vehicle with techniques that maximise fuel efficiency. Those who practise these techniques are known as "hypermilers". In the United States, hypermiling was the New Oxford American Dictionary's word of the year for 2008.

Techniques

Common hypermiling techniques include:

  • Driving without breaks (DWB) — maintaining momentum by anticipating traffic flow, traffic lights, and stops so that braking and re-acceleration are minimised;
  • Pulse and glide — accelerating to a target speed, then coasting (in neutral or with the engine off in some vehicles) back down, exploiting the engine's most efficient operating range;
  • Drafting — following larger vehicles to reduce aerodynamic drag (controversial because of safety concerns);
  • Reducing speed on motorways, since aerodynamic drag rises with the square of speed;
  • Vehicle preparation: over-inflating tyres slightly, removing excess weight, regular maintenance, and using low-viscosity oil.

Safety and legality

Some techniques, such as close drafting, coasting with the engine off, and rolling through stop signs, are criticised as dangerous or illegal. Motoring organisations such as the AAA have cautioned against extreme hypermiling methods while endorsing moderate eco-driving practices such as smooth acceleration and observance of speed limits.

Popularity

Hypermiling communities formed online in the 2000s, with drivers of hybrid cars such as the Toyota Prius and Honda Insight competing to exceed official fuel-economy ratings; some Insight drivers reported averages above 100 miles per gallon over long distances. The practice gained attention during periods of high fuel prices and has influenced mainstream "eco-driving" advice.
