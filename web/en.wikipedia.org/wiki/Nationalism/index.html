Nationalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nationalism is an idea or social movement that holds that the nation should be congruent with the state. As a movement, it presupposes the existence of a nation and tends to promote the interests of a particular nation, especially with the aim of gaining and maintaining its sovereignty and self-governance. Nationalism holds that each nation should govern itself, free from outside interference (self-determination), that a nation is a natural and ideal basis for a polity, and that the nation is the only legitimate source of political power.

History

Nationalism's modern form emerged from the American and French Revolutions and the Napoleonic Wars, spread through nineteenth-century Europe (the unifications of Italy and Germany, independence movements in the Habsburg and Ottoman empires and in Latin America), and in the twentieth century powered both the world wars and anti-colonial liberation movements across Asia and Africa. The UN Charter's affirmation of self-determination made the nation-state the universal form of political organisation.

Types and theories

A common distinction contrasts civic nationalism (membership defined by citizenship and shared institutions) with ethnic nationalism (membership by descent, language or culture), though real movements mix both. Theorists divide into "modernists" — Ernest Gellner, Benedict Anderson (Imagined Communities, 1983), Eric Hobsbawm — who see nations as products of modernity, print capitalism and industrialisation; "primordialists", who stress deep ethnic roots; and ethnosymbolists such as Anthony D. Smith, who emphasise myths, memories and symbols. Michael Billig's "banal nationalism" describes the everyday flags and habits that reproduce nationhood unnoticed.

Debates

Nationalism is credited with enabling democratic solidarity, anti-colonial freedom and welfare-state cohesion, and blamed for chauvinism, minority oppression and war. Contemporary debates concern its resurgence in populist movements, tensions with globalisation and supranational bodies such as the European Union, and the nationalism of stateless peoples seeking recognition.
