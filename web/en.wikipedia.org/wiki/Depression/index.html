Depression

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Depression (major depressive disorder) is a mental disorder characterised by persistent low mood or loss of interest and pleasure (anhedonia) lasting at least two weeks, accompanied by symptoms such as fatigue, disturbed sleep and appetite, poor concentration, feelings of worthlessness, and in severe cases thoughts of death. It is among the leading causes of disability worldwide, affecting on the order of 5% of adults at any time, women more than men.

Depression arises from interacting genetic vulnerability, brain chemistry, life stress (especially loss and adversity), medical illness and personality. The older 'chemical imbalance' slogan oversimplifies: current models involve circuits, stress hormones, inflammation and synaptic plasticity, with psychological and social factors woven in.

Treatment

Mild depression may respond to exercise, sleep regularisation and psychological therapy alone; moderate-to-severe depression is treated with psychotherapy (especially CBT and interpersonal therapy) and/or antidepressant medicines (SSRIs and others), which outperform placebo modestly on average and more clearly in severe cases. Electroconvulsive therapy remains the most effective treatment for severe, resistant or psychotic depression; newer options include esketamine and, in trials, psychedelic-assisted therapy. Suicide prevention — treating depression, restricting means, crisis support — is integral to care.

History

Recognised since antiquity as melancholia, depression was reframed by Kraepelin within manic-depressive illness and split in modern classifications into unipolar depression and Bipolar disorder. Its rising recorded prevalence mixes real increase, awareness and diagnostic breadth — a live debate.
