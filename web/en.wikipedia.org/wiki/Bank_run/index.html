Bank run

Bank run
CauseSudden mass withdrawal of deposits
Root structureFractional-reserve banking
MitigationsDeposit insurance, lender of last resort

A bank run occurs when many clients withdraw their deposits from a bank simultaneously, fearing the bank will become insolvent. Because banks operate on fractional reserves — lending out most deposits while keeping only a fraction as cash — a run can force even a solvent bank to fail by making it illiquid. Runs are a classic self-fulfilling prophecy and a central mechanism of financial panics and systemic banking crises.

History

Bank runs are as old as banking: runs struck goldsmith-bankers in 17th-century London and accompanied the crises of 1797, 1857, 1873, and 1907 (the last prompting the creation of the Federal Reserve). During the Great Depression, waves of runs destroyed thousands of American banks between 1930 and 1933, until President Franklin D. Roosevelt declared a national bank holiday in March 1933. Later famous runs include the UK's Northern Rock in 2007 — the first British bank run in over 140 years, with queues televised nationwide — and the March 2023 collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, where depositors withdrew $42 billion in a single day, the fastest run in history, accelerated by social media and mobile banking.

Theory

The canonical model of Diamond and Dybvig (1983, later Nobel-recognised) shows that deposit contracts create two equilibria: a good one where only those who genuinely need cash withdraw, and a bad "run" equilibrium where everyone rushes to withdraw because they expect others to do so. A run on one bank can spread by contagion when depositors cannot distinguish sound from unsound institutions, turning a liquidity problem into a generalised panic.

Prevention

The main safeguards are deposit insurance (such as the FDIC, created in 1933, insuring deposits up to $250,000), central banks acting as lender of last resort in the doctrine of Walter Bagehot ("lend freely, at a high rate, against good collateral"), capital and liquidity regulation, and, in extremis, bank holidays and suspension of convertibility.
