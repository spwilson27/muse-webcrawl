NOTAR

NOTAR (from "no tail rotor") is a helicopter anti-torque system that eliminates the conventional tail rotor. Developed by McDonnell Douglas Helicopter Systems (through Hughes Helicopters), it replaces the spinning tail rotor with a fixed tail boom containing a fan-driven air circulation system and a direct jet thruster. NOTAR-equipped helicopters are among the quietest and safest helicopters, because the elimination of the tail rotor removes a major source of noise and of ground accidents.

An MD 520N, showing the slotted NOTAR tail boom.

How it works

A variable-pitch fan inside the fuselage, driven by the main gearbox, forces low-pressure air into the hollow tail boom. Along the right side of the boom run two longitudinal slots. Air escaping through these slots entrains the main rotor's downwash around the boom — the Coandă effect — generating a sideways aerodynamic force that counters most of the main rotor torque at hover and low speed. At the end of the boom, a rotating direct jet thruster, controlled by the pilot's pedals, provides the remaining anti-torque force and directional control in hover and forward flight. Rudders on the tail fins contribute at higher speeds.

History and aircraft

The concept grew out of Hughes experiments in the 1970s, including tests on an OH-6 Cayuse in 1975 and the dedicated NOTAR demonstrator that flew in December 1981. Production aircraft using NOTAR include the MD 520N, the MD 600N, and the twin-engine MD Explorer (MD 900/902). They are used in law enforcement, air ambulance and utility roles, where low noise and the absence of an exposed tail rotor are valued.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include greatly reduced external noise, no tail-rotor strikes on people or obstacles, reduced vibration and fewer moving parts in the tail. Disadvantages include some loss of engine power to the fan, slightly reduced efficiency in sideward flight, and less crisp yaw authority in extreme manoeuvres compared with a conventional tail rotor.
