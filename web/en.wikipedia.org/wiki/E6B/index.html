E6B

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The circular slide rule face of a metal E6B.

The E6B is a circular slide rule used in aviation, commonly known as a flight computer or "whiz wheel". It is used during flight planning (on the ground) and in flight to calculate ground speed, estimated time of arrival, wind correction angle, fuel consumption, true airspeed, density altitude, and conversions between units such as nautical and statute miles or gallons and litres. Despite the spread of electronic flight bags, the E6B remains standard equipment in flight training, and student pilots in many countries are required to demonstrate proficiency with it in written examinations.

Design

The classic E6B has two sides. The front is a circular slide rule: a rotating disc with logarithmic scales used for multiplication, division, and unit conversion, with index marks for common calculations such as time–speed–distance and fuel burn. The reverse carries a wind face: a rotating azimuth ring and a sliding grid or transparent plate on which the pilot plots the wind vector, allowing the graphical solution of the wind triangle to find heading and ground speed. Metal versions are durable and standard-sized; smaller "micro" versions and paperboard training versions also exist, as do electronic calculators that emulate the same functions.

History

The device was developed in the 1930s by US Navy Lieutenant Philip Dalton, who patented a series of dead-reckoning computers. The Dalton Model B became so widely used that its US Army Air Corps designation, "E-6B", became the generic name. Dalton's design condensed his earlier, bulkier computers into a pocket-sized instrument; versions were used in large numbers during the Second World War by American and Commonwealth aircrew, and post-war production by companies such as Jeppesen made it the standard civil flight computer.

Use in training

Because it needs no power and gives visual, approximate solutions, the E6B is still taught as a backup skill and as a check on electronic computations. Typical exam problems include computing wind correction angle and ground speed for a given route, converting indicated to true airspeed with altitude and temperature, and determining fuel required for a leg.
