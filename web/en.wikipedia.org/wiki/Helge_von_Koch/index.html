Helge von Koch

Niels Fabian Helge von Koch (25 January 1870 – 11 March 1924) was a Swedish mathematician who gave his name to the famous Koch snowflake, one of the earliest fractal curves to be described. He was born in Stockholm into a family of the Swedish nobility; his father, Richert Vogt von Koch, was a military officer and writer.

Work

Von Koch studied at Stockholm University under Gösta Mittag-Leffler and took his doctorate at Uppsala University in 1892 with a thesis on differential equations, influenced by the methods of Poincaré and Fuchs. His early research concerned linear differential equations, infinite determinants (a technique he pioneered for solving such equations) and number theory, including work connected with the prime number theorem and the Riemann hypothesis. He held posts at the Royal Institute of Technology (KTH) from 1901 and became professor of mathematics at Stockholm University College in 1911.

Von Koch is remembered today chiefly for his 1904 paper "Sur une courbe continue sans tangente, obtenue par une construction géométrique élémentaire" ("On a continuous curve without tangents, obtainable by elementary geometric construction"). Starting from an equilateral triangle and repeatedly replacing the middle third of each side by two sides of an outward equilateral triangle, he produced a continuous closed curve that is nowhere differentiable and has infinite length enclosing a finite area—the Koch snowflake. The construction, intended to give an elementary example for Weierstrass-type pathology, became a canonical example of a self-similar fractal once Mandelbrot popularised such objects in the 1970s and 1980s.
