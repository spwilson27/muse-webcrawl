Pothos


Labelled image placeholder: Epipremnum aureum, the plant commonly called pothos

Pothos is a genus of flowering plants in the family Araceae, native to China, the Indian Subcontinent, Australia, New Guinea, Southeast Asia, and various islands of the Pacific and Indian Oceans. The genus contains roughly 50 species of evergreen climbers.

In common usage, however, the name "pothos" most often refers to Epipremnum aureum — the golden pothos, devil's ivy, or money plant — which was formerly classified in the genus Pothos and remains one of the world's most popular houseplants. True Pothos species are rarely cultivated.

Description

Species of Pothos are slender climbers that root at the nodes and ascend trees by means of aerial roots. Their leaves are generally simple, entire, and lanceolate to ovate, borne on long petioles. Unlike many aroids, the inflorescence is comparatively modest, with a spathe and spadix followed by berries.

Taxonomy

The genus was established by Carl Linnaeus. Over time many species once placed in Pothos have been moved to other genera such as Epipremnum and Scindapsus, which is why the horticultural trade still applies the name pothos to plants no longer in the genus. The common houseplant Epipremnum aureum rarely flowers, even in the wild, a fact linked to a genetic impairment in gibberellin biosynthesis.

Cultivation

Golden pothos tolerates low light and irregular watering, making it a forgiving indoor plant. Popular cultivars include 'Marble Queen', 'Neon', and 'Jade'. It is toxic to pets if ingested because of insoluble calcium oxalate crystals.
