Annulment (Catholic Church)

Official nameDeclaration of nullity
Legal basis1983 Code of Canon Law, cc. 1055–1165
Decided byDiocesan tribunal
Distinguish fromDivorce; dissolution of marriage

In the Catholic Church, an annulment — formally a declaration of nullity — is a judgment by a Church tribunal that a marriage, though it appeared valid, was in fact not a valid sacramental marriage from the beginning because of some defect at the time consent was exchanged. It is distinct from divorce, which the Church does not recognize for a ratified and consummated sacramental marriage (Latin: ratum et consummatum).

Grounds

Under the 1983 Code of Canon Law, grounds of nullity include diriment impediments (such as a prior bond, consanguinity, or sacred orders), defects of canonical form, and defects of consent — including grave lack of discretion, inability to assume the essential obligations of marriage, force or grave fear, fraud, simulation of consent, or error about the person or a quality of the person.

Process

A case is introduced by a petition (libellus) to the tribunal of the diocese where the marriage was celebrated, where a party lives, or where most proofs are located. A collegiate tribunal of judges, assisted by a defender of the bond who argues for validity, examines documents and witness testimony. Pope Francis's 2015 motu proprio Mitis Iudex Dominus Iesus streamlined the process, abolishing the mandatory double conforming sentence and allowing an abbreviated process before the diocesan bishop in manifest cases.

Effects

A declaration of nullity leaves both parties free to marry in the Church. Children of a putative marriage remain legitimate under canon law. Annulments have no civil effect and do not alter the legal status of the union in secular law.
