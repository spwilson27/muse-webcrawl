Chrismation

Chrismation is the sacrament of anointing with holy oil (chrism) in Eastern Orthodoxy, Oriental Orthodoxy, and Eastern Catholicism, and the name sometimes given to the corresponding rite of Confirmation in the Latin Church. In the East it is the second of the three sacraments of initiation (baptism, chrismation, Eucharist) and is normally administered to infants immediately after baptism, whereas in the West confirmation is typically delayed until later childhood.

Theology

Chrismation is understood as the seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit, corresponding to the apostolic laying on of hands described in the New Testament (Acts 8:14–17; 19:6). It completes baptism, bestowing the Spirit for Christian witness, and is conferred once — it is never repeated, unlike ordinary anointings.

Rite

The priest anoints the newly baptised on the forehead, eyes, nostrils, mouth, ears, chest, hands and feet, each time saying "The seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit, Amen." The chrism (myron), blessed by the head of the church, is said to be fragrant oil mixed with numerous aromatics. Those received into Orthodoxy from certain other Christian bodies may be received by chrismation alone rather than re-baptism.
