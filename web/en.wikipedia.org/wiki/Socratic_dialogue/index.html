Socratic dialogue

The Socratic dialogue (Ancient Greek: Sōkratikos logos) is a genre of ancient Greek and Roman prose writing that imitates the conversations of the philosopher Socrates. In a typical dialogue, Socrates questions one or more interlocutors about ethical or philosophical concepts — courage, piety, justice, virtue — drawing out contradictions in their answers through the method of cross-examination known as the elenchus, often ending in aporia (puzzlement) rather than a settled doctrine. The surviving masterpieces of the form are the dialogues of Plato, in which most modern readers encounter Socrates.

Form and method

Socrates wrote nothing himself; after his execution in 399 BC, several of his companions composed dialogues presenting his conversations. Plato's early dialogues, such as Euthyphro, Laches and Charmides, are generally held closest to the historical Socrates's method: a confident expert claims to know what a virtue is; Socrates asks for a definition; each proposal is tested and refuted until the interlocutor acknowledges ignorance — presented as progress, since recognising ignorance is the first step to knowledge. In Plato's middle and late works (the Republic, Phaedo, Symposium, Laws) Socrates becomes increasingly the spokesman for Plato's own doctrines, and the question-and-answer form shades into exposition.

The Socratic problem

Because Xenophon's Memorabilia and Symposium portray a noticeably more conventional Socrates, scholars face the "Socratic problem": how much of Plato's figure is historical. Aristophanes' caricature in The Clouds and Aristotle's testimony provide further, conflicting evidence. Xenophon, Aeschines and other "Socratics" wrote dialogues too, though most survive only in fragments.

Legacy

The dialogue form was taken up by Cicero for Roman philosophy, by Lucian for satire, and revived in the Renaissance (Erasmus, Galileo's scientific dialogues, Berkeley's Three Dialogues). "Socratic method" teaching — questioning rather than lecturing — remains a standard of law schools and philosophy pedagogy.
