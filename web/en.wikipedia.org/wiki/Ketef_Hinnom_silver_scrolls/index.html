Ketef Hinnom scrolls

The Ketef Hinnom scrolls, also known as the Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls, are two small silver scrolls discovered in 1979 in a burial cave at Ketef Hinnom, southwest of the Old City of Jerusalem. Inscribed in paleo-Hebrew script with versions of the Priestly Blessing from the Book of Numbers, they are among the oldest surviving texts of the Hebrew Bible, dating to the late 7th or early 6th century BC, around the time of the First Temple period.

Discovery

The scrolls were found during excavations led by Gabriel Barkay of Tel Aviv University in a repository of grave goods in burial chamber 25 of Cave 24 at Ketef Hinnom, a site overlooking the Hinnom Valley. The tiny rolled-up silver sheets — one about 97 mm long, the other about 39 mm — had been worn or deposited as amulets. Because of their fragility, it took several years to develop a method of unrolling them without destroying the inscriptions; the work was assisted by the Israel Museum's laboratories and later enhanced by digital imaging in a West Semitic Research Project study published in 2004.

Text

Both scrolls contain formulations closely paralleling the Priestly Blessing of Numbers 6:24–26: "The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face shine upon you and be gracious to you; the LORD lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace." The larger scroll (KH1) preserves portions of the blessing together with other lines recalling phrases from Deuteronomy, while the smaller (KH2) carries a condensed form of the blessing. The texts also invoke YHWH as a warrior and rebucker of evil, suggesting an apotropaic, protective function.

Significance

Dating to roughly 600 BC — some four centuries before the Dead Sea Scrolls — the Ketef Hinnom amulets are the oldest known artefacts bearing a text from the Hebrew Bible. They demonstrate that the Priestly Blessing was in liturgical use before the Babylonian exile and provide important evidence for the history of the biblical text and of ancient Israelite religion. The scrolls are now housed in the Israel Museum in Jerusalem.
