Caspian tiger

Caspian tiger
Conservation statusExtinct
Scientific namePanthera tigris tigris (formerly P. t. virgata)
RangeCentral Asia, from Turkey to western China

The Caspian tiger was a tiger population of Central Asia, once found in riverine corridors (tugai forests) from eastern Turkey and the Caucasus through Iran, Iraq, Afghanistan, and the Central Asian republics to Mongolia and western China. It became extinct in the course of the 20th century, with the last reliable records dating to the 1970s.

Characteristics

It was a large tiger, comparable to the Siberian tiger, with a stocky build, relatively long legs, wide paws, and a bright, dense winter coat. Genetic studies have shown it to be extremely close to the Siberian tiger, differing by only a single nucleotide in mitochondrial DNA, suggesting the two were separated only recently and that Siberian tigers might be used for reintroduction in Central Asia.

Extinction

Its decline was driven by systematic eradication campaigns — the Russian army was ordered to exterminate tigers in areas being colonised — destruction of reed-bed and tugai habitat by irrigation agriculture, and depletion of prey such as wild boar and Bukhara deer. The last confirmed Caspian tigers were killed in Turkey around 1970. Reintroduction of Amur tigers to Kazakhstan's Ili delta has been proposed and begun as habitat restoration.
