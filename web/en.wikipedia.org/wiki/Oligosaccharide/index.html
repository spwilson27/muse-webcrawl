Oligosaccharide

Oligosaccharide
TypeCarbohydrate polymer
Composition3–10 monosaccharide units (definitions vary)
ExamplesRaffinose, stachyose, fructooligosaccharides, human milk oligosaccharides
RolesEnergy storage, cell recognition, prebiotics

An oligosaccharide is a carbohydrate polymer composed of a small number of monosaccharide (simple sugar) units — typically between three and ten — joined by glycosidic bonds. (Disaccharides, with two units, are sometimes included in the category.) Larger polymers are called polysaccharides. The name comes from the Greek oligos, "few", and sacchar, "sugar".

Examples and sources

  • Raffinose and stachyose — trisaccharide and tetrasaccharide found in beans, lentils, cabbage and other vegetables; humans lack the α-galactosidase enzyme needed to digest them, so they are fermented by gut bacteria, producing gas.
  • Fructooligosaccharides (FOS) and galactooligosaccharides (GOS) — used as prebiotic food ingredients that stimulate beneficial gut bacteria.
  • Human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) — more than 200 structurally diverse oligosaccharides in breast milk, the third most abundant solid component of human milk; they nourish the infant gut microbiome and act as decoys that block pathogen attachment.
  • Cyclodextrins — cyclic oligosaccharides of six to eight glucose units, used in pharmacy and food technology.

Biological functions

Oligosaccharides attached to proteins (glycoproteins) and lipids (glycolipids) on cell surfaces are central to cell–cell recognition. The ABO blood group antigens are oligosaccharides, and many viruses, bacteria and toxins initiate infection by binding to specific cell-surface oligosaccharides. In plants, oligosaccharides such as the raffinose family serve in carbon transport and storage and in stress protection.

Glycosylation

In cells, N-linked oligosaccharides are built on a dolichol phosphate carrier in the endoplasmic reticulum and transferred to asparagine residues of nascent proteins, then trimmed and elaborated in the Golgi apparatus; O-linked chains are added directly to serine or threonine residues. Defects in these pathways cause congenital disorders of glycosylation.
