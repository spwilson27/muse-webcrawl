Taser

A Taser is a conducted electrical weapon (CEW) — a less-lethal weapon that fires two small barbed probes attached by wires, delivering an electric current intended to cause temporary neuromuscular incapacitation. The name is a trademark of Axon Enterprise, Inc. (formerly Taser International), and derives from the 1974 invention of NASA researcher Jack Cover, who named his device the "Thomas A. Swift Electric Rifle" after the Tom Swift adventure novels.

How it works

On firing, compressed gas propels two probes up to several metres. When both probes strike the subject, the weapon delivers a series of short high-voltage pulses (peak voltages around 50,000 volts to arc through clothing, with much lower delivered energy) that interfere with voluntary motor control, causing muscles to contract and the subject to lose balance and control. Modern police models such as the TASER X2, X26P and TASER 7 record firing data, and civilian models (such as the Pulse) deliver a longer cycle intended to let the user escape.

Use and controversy

Tasers are used by thousands of police forces worldwide as an alternative to firearms and physical force. Manufacturers and many studies argue they reduce injuries to officers and suspects compared with other force options. However, their use has been controversial: human rights organisations have documented deaths following Taser use, particularly in people who were restrained, intoxicated or medically vulnerable, and police policies typically restrict repeated or prolonged discharges. Misuse — such as against passive resisters or children — has led to litigation, tighter rules and, in some jurisdictions, bans on civilian possession.

Legal status

Legality varies widely: Tasers are standard police equipment in the United States, United Kingdom (specially trained officers), Canada, Australia and elsewhere, while civilian ownership is restricted or prohibited in many countries and some US jurisdictions.
