Kurds

The Kurds (Kurdish: Kurd, کورد) are an Iranian ethnic group native to Kurdistan, a mountainous region spanning south-eastern Turkey, north-western Iran, northern Iraq and northern Syria, with a large diaspora in Europe. They are among the largest ethnic groups in the world without a state of their own.

Population and language

Estimates of the total Kurdish population range from 30 to 45 million. Kurds speak Kurdish, a group of related north-western Iranian languages whose main dialects — Kurmanji, Sorani and Southern Kurdish — are written in Latin, Arabic-based and other scripts depending on region.

History

Medieval Kurdish dynasties ruled parts of the region, and Kurdish emirates retained partial autonomy under the Ottoman and Persian empires. After the First World War, the 1920 Treaty of Sèvres envisaged a possible Kurdish state, but the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne made no such provision and Kurdistan was divided among Turkey, Iran, Iraq, Syria and the Soviet Union. The short-lived Republic of Mahabad was proclaimed in Iran in 1946. In Iraq, Kurds suffered the Anfal campaign and the Halabja chemical attack of 1988 under Saddam Hussein; since 1991 the Kurdistan Region has enjoyed constitutional autonomy within federal Iraq.

Culture

Kurdish culture centres on hospitality, oral literature — including the epic of Mem û Zîn by Ehmedê Xanî — and the spring festival of Newroz, the Kurdish New Year. Most Kurds are Sunni Muslims, with Alevi, Shia, Yarsani, Yazidi and other minorities.
