American and British English spelling differences

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Despite the various English dialects spoken from country to country and within different regions of the same country, there are only slight regional variations in English spelling; the two most notable variations are British and American spelling. Many of the differences between American and British English date back to a time when spelling standards had not yet developed, while others reflect deliberate reforms, notably those of Noah Webster in the early 19th century.

Historical origins

Early English orthography was unsettled. Samuel Johnson's dictionary of 1755 standardised British spelling largely according to etymology, retaining, for example, -our in words from Old French and -ise/-ize variants. Noah Webster's American dictionaries (notably 1828) promoted simpler, more phonetic spellings — honor, center, defense — as a conscious assertion of American cultural independence, and most of his reforms entered general American usage.

Principal patterns

  • -our / -or: British colour, favour, honour vs. American color, favor, honor.
  • -re / -er: British centre, theatre, metre vs. American center, theater, meter.
  • -ise / -ize: British often organise (though organize is also standard Oxford spelling) vs. American organize.
  • -yse / -yze: British analyse vs. American analyze.
  • -ogue / -og: British catalogue, dialogue vs. American catalog, dialog.
  • -ence / -ense: British defence, licence (noun) vs. American defense, license.
  • Doubled consonants: British travelled, modelling vs. American traveled, modeling.
  • -ae- / -oe- / e: British encyclopaedia, manoeuvre, oestrogen vs. American encyclopedia, maneuver, estrogen.
  • Other words: aluminium/aluminum, cheque/check, grey/gray, kerb/curb, pyjamas/pajamas, sulphur/sulfur, tyre/tire, whisky/whiskey.

Use in other varieties

Canadian spelling mixes British and American conventions (e.g., colour but often organize), while Australian and New Zealand English generally follow British norms. International organisations and publishers adopt their own house styles.
