Aphids are small sap-sucking insects in the superfamily Aphidoidea, commonly known as greenflies or blackflies (though not true flies). About 5,000 species have been described, of which some 450 feed on crop and garden plants, making aphids among the most destructive insect pests worldwide. They feed by piercing plant tissues with needle-like mouthparts and sucking phloem sap, weakening the plant, excreting sticky honeydew that fosters sooty moulds, and transmitting many plant viruses.

Biology and life cycle

Aphid life cycles are famously complex: many species alternate between a primary host plant, on which sexual reproduction and egg-laying occur, and a secondary host, where colonies multiply explosively by parthenogenesis — females giving birth to live young that are already pregnant ("telescoping generations"). Most have a pair of abdominal tubes called cornicles that secrete defensive fluids. Ants often tend aphid colonies, protecting them from predators in exchange for honeydew. Natural enemies include ladybirds, lacewings, hoverfly larvae, and parasitoid wasps, and aphid populations are also devastated by fungal pathogens.

Control

Because of their reproductive rate and evolved resistance, aphids are difficult to control; integrated pest management combines insecticides, resistant plant varieties, removal of weeds, and encouragement of predators. Notorious species include the green peach aphid (Myzus persicae), the soybean aphid, and the grape phylloxera, which devastated European vineyards in the 19th century.
