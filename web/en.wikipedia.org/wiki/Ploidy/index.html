Ploidy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ploidy (/ˈplɔɪdi/) is the number of complete sets of chromosomes in a cell, and hence the number of possible alleles for autosomal and pseudoautosomal genes. Sets of chromosomes refer to the number of maternal and paternal chromosome copies in each homologous set, which exist in a cell's nucleus as chromosomes. Somatic cells, tissues, and individual eukaryotic organisms can be described according to the number of sets present (the ploidy level): monoploid (1 set), diploid (2 sets), triploid (3 sets), tetraploid (4 sets), pentaploid (5 sets), hexaploid (6 sets), heptaploid (7 sets), and so on.

The generic term polyploid is often used to describe cells with three or more chromosome sets. Practically all sexually reproducing organisms are made of somatic cells that are diploid or greater, but ploidy level may vary widely between different organisms, between different tissues within the same organism, and at different stages of an organism's life cycle.

Terminology

The number of chromosomes in a single (non-homologous) set is called the monoploid number (x). The chromosome number found in normal gametes is called the haploid number (n): for humans, x = n = 23, but for bread wheat, x = 7 while n = 21, since wheat is hexaploid (6x = 42). A euploid cell carries an exact multiple of the monoploid number, while an aneuploid cell has a chromosome number that is not an exact multiple — for example, a human cell with 45 or 47 chromosomes.

Examples in organisms

Animals

Most animals are diploid in their somatic cells, with haploid gametes produced by meiosis. In Hymenoptera (ants, bees, and wasps) sex is determined by ploidy: fertilized (diploid) eggs become females, while unfertilized (haploid) eggs become males — a system called haplodiploidy. Some animals, such as certain amphibians (e.g., Xenopus laevis, an allotetraploid) and fish, are naturally polyploid.

Plants

Polyploidy is especially common in plants and is a major mechanism of speciation. Cultivated examples include hexaploid bread wheat (Triticum aestivum, 6x), tetraploid durum wheat and potatoes (4x), and octoploid strawberries (8x). Seedless fruits are often produced from triploid plants, such as seedless watermelons and bananas, whose odd ploidy prevents successful meiosis. Plants also exhibit alternation of generations, alternating between a diploid sporophyte and a haploid gametophyte generation; in mosses the gametophyte is dominant, while in flowering plants it is greatly reduced.

Fungi and other organisms

Many fungi are haploid for most of their life cycle, with a brief diploid phase. Some organisms have unusual arrangements: cells of the endosperm in flowering plant seeds are typically triploid, receiving two maternal sets and one paternal set.

Variation and medical relevance

In humans, abnormal ploidy at conception is usually lethal; triploidy occurs in roughly 1–3% of conceptions and rarely survives to term. Aneuploidies of single chromosomes, such as trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), trisomy 18 and trisomy 13, are better tolerated. Many cancer cells are aneuploid, with chromosome numbers far from diploid.
