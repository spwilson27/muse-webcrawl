Ringworm, medically known as dermatophytosis or tinea, is a fungal infection of the skin. Despite its name, it is not caused by a worm; the name derives from the characteristic red, ring-shaped rash that typically appears on the affected area. The infection is caused by a group of fungi known as dermatophytes, which feed on keratin found in the skin, hair, and nails.

Signs and symptoms

Ringworm typically presents as a circular, red, scaly patch with clearer skin in the centre, giving it a ring-like appearance. The affected area may be itchy. When it occurs on the scalp (tinea capitis) it can cause hair loss; on the feet it is known as athlete's foot (tinea pedis), and in the groin as jock itch (tinea cruris). Infection of the nails leads to thickened, discoloured nails.

Cause

Dermatophytosis is caused by fungi in the genera Trichophyton, Microsporum, and Epidermophyton. The infection spreads through direct skin-to-skin contact with an infected person or animal, or indirectly through contaminated objects such as towels, clothing, and shower floors. Warm, moist environments favour fungal growth.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is often based on the appearance of the rash. It can be confirmed by examining skin scrapings under a microscope after treatment with potassium hydroxide, by fungal culture, or by examining the skin under a Wood's lamp.

Treatment

Most cases are treated with topical antifungal creams such as clotrimazole, miconazole, or terbinafine, applied for one to three weeks. Infections of the scalp or nails, or widespread infections, usually require oral antifungal medication such as terbinafine, itraconazole, or griseofulvin. Keeping the skin clean and dry helps prevent recurrence.

Epidemiology

Ringworm is very common worldwide; it is estimated that up to 20–25% of the world's population may be affected by a superficial fungal infection at any given time. It occurs in both humans and animals, including cats and dogs.
