Sejong the Great

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Statue of King Sejong on Gwanghwamun Square in Seoul, seated before the Hangul letters]

Sejong the Great (1397–1450; r. 1418–1450) was the fourth king of Joseon Korea and its most revered monarch, creator of Hangul, the Korean alphabet. Inheriting the throne from his father Taejong (who continued to wield military power until 1422), Sejong gathered scholars in the Hall of Worthies (Jiphyeonjeon) and presided over a golden age of Korean science, agriculture, medicine, music and historiography — astronomical instruments and rain gauges, the agricultural manual Nongsa jikseol, advances in printing and gunpowder weapons, and the northern campaigns that fixed Korea's borders at the Yalu and Tumen rivers. His portrait is on the South Korean 10,000-won note.

Hangul

Sejong's supreme achievement addressed a real injustice: Classical Chinese characters, mastered only by the yangban elite, could not write Korean speech, leaving commoners and women voiceless in writing. In 1443/1444 he and his scholars completed a featural alphabet of 28 letters (now 24) of brilliant design — consonant shapes modelling the speech organs, vowels built from philosophical principles — promulgated in 1446 as the Hunminjeongeum ('Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People') over the protests of officials who saw it as a threat to civilisation itself. Banned by a later king (1504) and long despised as 'women's letters', Hangul triumphed in the 20th century as the emblem of Korean identity.

Reign and legacy

Sejong's reign also systematised Joseon's Confucian order: law codes, rituals, the five military commands, diplomacy balancing Ming China (tributary) and the Jurchen and Japanese frontiers (the Ōei invasion of Tsushima, 1419). Personal costs shadow the achievement — diabetes and blindness in his last years, the deaths of his queen and sons, and his reliance on Crown Prince Munjong, who died two years after succeeding. Korean memory places Sejong beside the handful of rulers any nation calls simply 'the Great'.
