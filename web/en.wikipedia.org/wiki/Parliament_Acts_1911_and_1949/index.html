Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Palace of Westminster, home of the UK Parliament
The Palace of Westminster, home of the UK Parliament

The Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 are two Acts of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that define the powers of the House of Lords in relation to the House of Commons. They removed the Lords' power to veto most public bills, replacing it with a power to delay — reduced by the 1949 Act from two years to one — and abolished any veto over money bills. Together they underpin the modern primacy of the elected Commons.

Background and the 1911 Act

The crisis began when the Lords rejected David Lloyd George's 'People's Budget' of 1909, breaking a centuries-old convention. After two general elections in 1910 and a threat by George V (secured by Prime Minister H. H. Asquith) to create enough new peers to swamp the opposition, the Lords passed the Parliament Act 1911: money bills could be delayed one month; other bills passed by the Commons in three successive sessions over two years could receive royal assent without the Lords' consent.

The 1949 Act and later use

The Attlee government used the 1911 procedure to pass the Parliament Act 1949 itself, cutting the delay to two sessions over one year — a use later upheld in Jackson v Attorney General (2005). The Acts have been used sparingly: for the Welsh Church Act 1914, Irish Home Rule (1914), the 1949 Act, and since 1990 for the War Crimes Act 1991, the European Parliamentary Elections Act 1999, the Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act 2000 and the Hunting Act 2004.

Significance

The Acts are a cornerstone of the uncodified British constitution: they settle legislative supremacy in the Commons, yet preserve the Lords as a revising chamber whose delay can force reconsideration. Their 'manner and form' implications — whether Parliament can bind itself — remain debated by constitutional theorists.
