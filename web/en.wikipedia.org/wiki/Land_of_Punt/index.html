The Land of Punt (Egyptian: pwnt) was an ancient kingdom or trading region known from ancient Egyptian records, famed as a source of gold, aromatic resins such as frankincense and myrrh, blackwood (ebony), ivory, and wild animals. Egyptian inscriptions describe trading expeditions to Punt from as early as the Old Kingdom (c. 2500 BC) down to the New Kingdom, after which direct contact appears to have faded.

Punt was sometimes poetically called Ta netjer, "the Land of the God", a term also applied to regions associated with the rising sun and the incense used in temple ritual. Because no inscription from Punt itself has been found, its exact location remains debated; most scholars place it on the Horn of Africa, in present-day Eritrea, Ethiopia, Djibouti, or Somalia, though a minority argue for a location on the Arabian Peninsula or on both shores of the Red Sea.

History

Old and Middle Kingdom

The earliest known expedition to Punt is recorded on the Palermo Stone, which notes that a fleet sent by Pharaoh Sahure of the Fifth Dynasty (c. 25th century BC) returned with large quantities of myrrh, electrum, and timber. During the Eleventh Dynasty, the steward Henenu, serving Mentuhotep III, led an expedition of some 3,000 men from Coptos through Wadi Hammamat to the Red Sea coast, where ships were built for the voyage to Punt.

Expedition of Hatshepsut

The most famous expedition was sent by Queen Hatshepsut around 1470 BC and is documented in extraordinary detail in reliefs at her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari. The Egyptian fleet of five ships, led by the chancellor Nehsi, returned laden with myrrh trees (thirty-one of which were transplanted to Egypt), frankincense, ebony, ivory, gold, cinnamon wood, eye cosmetic, apes, monkeys, dogs, leopard skins, and Puntites themselves. The reliefs depict the chief of Punt, Parahu, and his wife Ati, who is shown with a distinctive physique that has generated much scholarly discussion.

Later history

Later New Kingdom pharaohs, including Thutmose III and Ramesses III, also received or sent missions to Punt. After the collapse of the New Kingdom around the late 2nd millennium BC, direct Egyptian expeditions ceased, and Punt passed into legend.

Location

The location of Punt is one of the most debated questions in Egyptology. Egyptian sources place it reachable both by land and by sea via the Red Sea, southeast of Egypt. Analysis of the flora and fauna depicted in the Deir el-Bahari reliefs, and isotopic studies of mummified baboons imported from Punt, point toward the Horn of Africa, particularly the Eritrea–Ethiopia region. Other scholars note that Puntite products could also have been gathered from southwestern Arabia, and it is possible "Punt" covered territories on both sides of the southern Red Sea.

Trade goods

Punt's chief exports were incense resins (myrrh and frankincense), prized for temple ritual and mummification; gold; ebony and other hardwoods; ivory; cinnamon; throw sticks; live exotic animals including monkeys, baboons, leopards, and giraffes; and animal skins. In return, the Egyptians offered manufactured goods such as weapons, tools, jewellery, and foodstuffs.
