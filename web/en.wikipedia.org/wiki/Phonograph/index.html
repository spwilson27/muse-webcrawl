Phonograph

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


An Edison Standard phonograph, c. 1905.

A phonograph, in its later forms also called a gramophone (as a trademark since 1887, as a generic name in the UK since 1910) or a record player (since the 1940s), is a device for the mechanical and analogue recording and reproduction of sound. The sound vibration waveforms are recorded as corresponding physical deviations of a helical groove engraved, etched, incised, or impressed into the surface of a rotating cylinder or disc, called a "record".

The phonograph was invented in 1877 by Thomas Edison. Its first recordings were spoken words and music captured on tinfoil wrapped around a grooved cylinder. Alexander Graham Bell and colleagues improved the design with wax-coated cardboard cylinders (the graphophone), and Emile Berliner developed the flat disc gramophone in the late 1880s, whose shellac records could be mass-produced from a single master.

History

Early sound recording

The earliest known device for recording sound was the phonautograph, patented in 1857 by Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville, which traced sound waves onto soot-blackened paper but could not play them back. Edison's phonograph, patented in 1878, was the first device that could both record and reproduce sound.

Cylinder vs. disc

Through the 1890s and 1900s cylinder and disc formats competed. Discs won out by the 1910s: they were cheaper to stamp in quantity, easier to store, and championed by the Victor Talking Machine Company, whose Victrola became a household fixture. Electrical recording in the mid-1920s greatly improved fidelity, and the introduction of the long-playing (LP) microgroove record by Columbia Records in 1948, together with RCA Victor's 45 rpm single, established the formats that dominated the twentieth century.

How it works

On playback, a stylus (needle) rides in the groove of the rotating record. Vibrations transmitted from the stylus are converted into sound: in acoustic-era machines by a diaphragm and horn, and in later machines by a phono cartridge whose piezoelectric or magnetic elements convert motion into an electrical signal for amplification. Standard turntable speeds were 78, 33⅓, and 45 revolutions per minute.

Legacy

Although largely displaced by cassettes, compact discs, and digital streaming, the phonograph experienced a sustained revival in the twenty-first century, with vinyl record sales growing year on year from the mid-2000s onward. The turntable also became a musical instrument in its own right through turntablism in hip hop culture.
