Wolbachia is a genus of intracellular bacteria that infects mainly arthropod species, including a high proportion of insects. It is one of the most common parasitic microbes in the world, and is possibly the most common reproductive parasite in the biosphere. Its interactions with its hosts are often complex. Some host species cannot reproduce, or even survive, without Wolbachia colonisation; others can survive, but with severe competitive disadvantages, while yet others are completely unaffected.

Wolbachia was first identified in 1924 by Marshall Hertig and Simeon Burt Wolbach in the mosquito Culex pipiens; the genus was named after Wolbach. The species Wolbachia pipientis was described in 1936.

Effects on hosts

Because Wolbachia is transmitted vertically through eggs, it manipulates host reproduction to favour infected females. Known strategies include cytoplasmic incompatibility — in which the sperm of infected males cannot fertilise eggs of uninfected females, while infected females can mate successfully with any male, giving them a reproductive advantage; feminisation of genetic males; induction of parthenogenesis in some wasps; and male killing, in which infected male embryos die. These manipulations spread the bacterium rapidly through host populations.

In filarial nematodes, by contrast, Wolbachia is an obligate mutualist essential to worm fertility and survival, and it is a major contributor to the inflammatory pathology of diseases such as river blindness and lymphatic filariasis. Antibiotic treatment with doxycycline that kills the bacteria sterilises and eventually kills the worms, making Wolbachia a target for antifilarial therapy.

Disease control

Wolbachia has been harnessed for vector control. Certain strains (notably wMel, originally from Drosophila melanogaster) block the replication of dengue and other arboviruses in Aedes aegypti mosquitoes. The World Mosquito Program has released Wolbachia-carrying mosquitoes in many countries; a randomized controlled trial in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, published in 2021 showed that deployments of wMel-infected mosquitoes reduced virologically confirmed dengue cases by about 77% and dengue hospitalisations by about 86%. A separate approach uses cytoplasmic incompatibility: releasing large numbers of Wolbachia-infected males suppresses wild mosquito populations, a method deployed against Aedes albopictus and Aedes aegypti in pilot projects.

Genomics

The Wolbachia genome is small (roughly 1–1.7 million base pairs), reflecting its intracellular lifestyle. Notably, fragments of Wolbachia DNA have been found integrated into the genomes of several host species — lateral gene transfer between the bacterium and its hosts. The bacterium cannot be cultured outside host cells, which constrains experimental study.
