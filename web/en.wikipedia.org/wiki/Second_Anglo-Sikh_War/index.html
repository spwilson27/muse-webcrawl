The Second Anglo-Sikh War was a military conflict between the Sikh Empire and the British East India Company that took place from 1848 to 1849. It resulted in the fall of the Sikh Empire and the annexation of the Punjab by the East India Company — the last major region of the Indian subcontinent to come under British rule — bringing the Company's territories up to the Khyber Pass and the frontier with Afghanistan.

Background

After the First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46), the treaties of Lahore and Bhairowal left the Sikh Empire formally independent but under a British resident's control, with its army reduced and the child Maharaja Duleep Singh on the throne under a council of regency dominated by the British. Resentment at this protectorate, and at interference in local government, simmered. In April 1848 the Dewan (governor) of Multan, Mulraj Chopra, resisted a British-imposed replacement; two British officers sent to take over, Vans Agnew and Anderson, were murdered by his troops, and Multan revolted. Sardar Sher Singh Attariwalla, sent to suppress the rising, instead joined it, and his father Chattar Singh raised the northwest in revolt.

Campaign

General Lord Gough advanced into the Punjab in November 1848. The campaign's main battles were Ramnagar (22 November 1848), the bloody and indecisive Chillianwala (13 January 1849) — one of the hardest-fought battles in the Company's history, where British losses were so heavy the result shocked London — the fall of Multan (January 1849), and finally Gujrat (21 February 1849), the "battle of the guns", where overwhelming British artillery broke the Sikh army. The Sikhs surrendered at Rawalpindi in March, and Afghan allies who had joined them under Dost Mohammad's son withdrew across the Indus.

Aftermath

On 29 March 1849 the Governor-General, Lord Dalhousie, proclaimed the annexation of the Punjab. Duleep Singh was pensioned off and formally surrendered the Koh-i-Noor diamond to Queen Victoria. Punjab was administered by a Board of Administration headed by Henry Lawrence; within a decade, Punjabi recruits had become a mainstay of the British Indian Army, and the province remained largely loyal during the Indian Rebellion of 1857.
