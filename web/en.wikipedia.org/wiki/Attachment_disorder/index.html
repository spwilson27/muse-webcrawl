Attachment disorder

Attachment disorder
SpecialtyChild psychiatry, psychology
Recognised formsReactive attachment disorder; disinhibited social engagement disorder
OnsetEarly childhood

Attachment disorder is a broad term describing disorders of mood, behaviour and social relationships arising from failure to form normal healthy attachments to primary caregivers in early childhood. Such failure can result from severe early experiences of neglect, abuse, abrupt separation, or frequent changes of caregivers. In mainstream psychiatry the recognised diagnoses are reactive attachment disorder (RAD) and disinhibited social engagement disorder (DSED), both defined in the DSM-5 and ICD-11.

Recognised disorders

  • Reactive attachment disorder – a pattern of inhibited, emotionally withdrawn behaviour toward caregivers; the child rarely seeks or responds to comfort when distressed.
  • Disinhibited social engagement disorder – a pattern of overly familiar, indiscriminately friendly behaviour with unfamiliar adults, formerly a subtype of RAD.

Both require a history of seriously insufficient care ("pathogenic care") and typically begin before age five. Research on children raised in depriving institutions, notably Romanian orphanages, has been central to understanding them.

Criticism and fringe usage

A broader, loosely defined "attachment disorder" is promoted by some practitioners of so-called attachment therapy, with symptom lists and coercive treatments (such as forced holding and "rebirthing") that are not evidence-based and have been condemned by professional bodies after cases of child deaths. Mainstream clinicians distinguish the DSM diagnoses sharply from this fringe usage.

Treatment

Evidence-based treatment centres on providing the child with a stable, sensitive caregiver and interventions that build attachment security; assessment and treatment of comorbid conditions are also important.
