Wild rice

Genus: Zizania

Family: Poaceae (grasses)

Native range: North America and East Asia

Wild rice, also called Canada rice, Indian rice, or water oats, is any of the four species of grasses forming the genus Zizania. Despite the name, the plants are not directly related to domesticated rice (Oryza sativa). They are aquatic grasses that grow in shallow lakes and slow-flowing streams, and their grains have been harvested as a food for centuries.

Species and distribution

Three species are native to North America: northern wild rice (Zizania palustris), wild rice (Z. aquatica), and Texas wild rice (Z. texana), the last endangered. A fourth species, Manchurian wild rice (Z. latifolia), is native to Asia. Z. palustris is the species most commonly cultivated and sold commercially.

Cultural significance

Wild rice, known as manoomin in the Ojibwe language, is central to the culture and diet of the Anishinaabe peoples of the Great Lakes region, who traditionally harvest it by knocking the ripe grains into canoes. It is also the official state grain of Minnesota. Traditional harvesting remains important both nutritionally and spiritually, and wild rice features in Anishinaabe migration prophecies.

Cultivation and cuisine

Commercial production takes place largely in Minnesota and California, where plants are grown in paddies and harvested mechanically. The dark, slender grains have a chewy texture and nutty flavour and are used in pilafs, soups, and stuffings. Because of its long cooking time and relatively high price, wild rice is often blended with other rices.
