Ars nova ("new art") is a musical style and a period in the history of French and International music in the Late Middle Ages, flourishing in the 14th century before the early Renaissance. The term comes from the title of a treatise attributed to the composer and theorist Philippe de Vitry (c. 1322), and it is contrasted with the older style, the ars antiqua.

Characteristics

Ars nova notation gave composers far greater rhythmic freedom: duple ("imperfect") division of note values was admitted alongside triple; smaller note values such as the minim became available; and mensuration signs — ancestors of modern time signatures — indicated how beats were grouped. Pope John XXII's bull Docta sanctorum patrum (1324–25) attacked the new style's use in church for its rhythmic complexity and secular tunes in the tenor.

Isorhythm and the motet

The motet was the prestige genre of the ars nova. In the isorhythmic motet, the tenor is organised by repeating patterns: a melodic pattern (color) and an independent rhythmic pattern (talea), whose interplay structures the whole composition. Guillaume de Machaut, the era's most famous composer, also brought the secular formes fixes — ballade, rondeau and virelai — to a high level of sophistication, and composed the first complete polyphonic setting of the Mass Ordinary attributable to one composer, the Messe de Nostre Dame.

Composers

Principal figures include Philippe de Vitry, Jehan des Murs (a theorist rather than composer), Machaut, and, late in the century, the composers of the mannered ars subtilior such as Baude Cordier and Jacob Senleches.

Legacy

Ars nova freed rhythm from modal patterns and laid the foundation for the notational and compositional practices of the Renaissance. The Italian Trecento, with composers such as Francesco Landini, developed in parallel with a somewhat different notational system.
