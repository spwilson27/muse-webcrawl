United States v. Morrison

United States v. Morrison, 529 U.S. 598 (2000), is a U.S. Supreme Court decision that held that parts of the Violence Against Women Act of 1994 were unconstitutional because they exceeded the powers granted to the United States Congress under the Commerce Clause and the Fourteenth Amendment of the United States Constitution.

Background

In 1995, Christy Brzonkala alleged that she was assaulted and raped by two Virginia Tech football players, Antonio Morrison and James Crawford. After university proceedings she regarded as inadequate, she sued under 42 U.S.C. § 13981, the civil remedy provision of the Violence Against Women Act (VAWA), which allowed victims of gender-motivated violence to sue their attackers in federal court. The district court dismissed the claim, and the Fourth Circuit ultimately held the provision unconstitutional.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision authored by Chief Justice William Rehnquist, the Court struck down § 13981. The Court held that gender-motivated violence is not economic activity and therefore cannot be regulated under the Commerce Clause merely because its aggregate effects might affect interstate commerce — extending the reasoning of United States v. Lopez (1995). The Court also held that the provision could not be justified under Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment because it regulated private conduct rather than state action, and was not congruent and proportional to any pattern of state discrimination.

Legacy

Morrison is a cornerstone of the Rehnquist Court's federalism jurisprudence, limiting Congress's commerce power over non-economic intrastate activity. Critics argued the decision disregarded extensive congressional findings on the economic effects of violence against women; supporters praised it as a necessary check on federal power. Other VAWA programs — funding, grants, and criminal provisions that survived — were repeatedly reauthorized by Congress.
