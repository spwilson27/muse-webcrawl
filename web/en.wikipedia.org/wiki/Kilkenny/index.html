Kilkenny (Irish: Cill Chainnigh) is a city in County Kilkenny in the south-east of Ireland, built on both banks of the River Nore. Though modest in population, it held city status for over 400 years (from a royal charter of 1609) and is famed for its remarkably preserved medieval core, earning it a reputation as Ireland's best-preserved medieval city and the nickname "the Marble City" after its black polished limestone.

History

The settlement grew around a 6th-century church founded by St Canice (Cainnech), whose cathedral still stands on the site. After the Norman invasion, William Marshal built Kilkenny Castle (from 1195) and the town became one of the chief towns of Norman Ireland and seat of the Butler earls of Ormond. In 1366 the parliament meeting at Kilkenny passed the Statutes of Kilkenny, attempting to halt the Gaelicisation of the Anglo-Norman colony by forbidding intermarriage and the use of Irish law and language. During the Irish Confederate Wars, Kilkenny served as the capital of Confederate Ireland (1642–1649) before falling to Cromwell's forces in 1650. The city is also remembered for the 1324 witchcraft trial of Alice Kyteler, whose servant Petronilla de Meath became the first person in Ireland burned at the stake for heresy.

Landmarks and culture

Highlights of the "Medieval Mile" include Kilkenny Castle, St Canice's Cathedral with its climbable round tower, the Black Abbey, Rothe House (a Tudor merchant's townhouse), Kyteler's Inn, and the Tholsel. Modern Kilkenny is known for its arts festivals — including the Kilkenny Arts Festival and the Cat Laughs comedy festival — its craft and design scene centred on the National Design & Craft Gallery, and for Smithwick's ale, brewed at St Francis Abbey Brewery from 1710 until production moved in 2013. In sport, Kilkenny is synonymous with hurling: the county team, the Cats, is the most successful in the history of the All-Ireland Senior Hurling Championship.
