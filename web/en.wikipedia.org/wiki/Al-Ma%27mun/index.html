Al-Ma'mun

Al-Ma'mun
Caliph of the Abbasid Caliphate
Reign: 813 – 833 CE
Predecessor: al-Amin
Successor: al-Mu'tasim
Born: 14 September 786, Baghdad
Died: 9 August 833 (aged 46), near Tarsus

Abū al-ʿAbbās ʿAbd Allāh ibn Hārūn al-Rashīd (14 September 786 – 9 August 833), better known by his regnal name al-Maʾmūn, was the seventh Abbasid caliph, who reigned from 813 until his death in 833. He is celebrated for his patronage of scholarship and the translation movement centred on Baghdad, and notorious among Sunni traditionalists for the mihna, the inquisition he instituted to enforce Mu'tazilite doctrine.

Rise to power

Son of the caliph Harun al-Rashid and a Persian concubine, al-Ma'mun was appointed governor of Khurasan and designated second in the succession after his half-brother al-Amin. Disputes over their inheritance led to civil war (the Fourth Fitna, 809–813): al-Ma'mun's general Tahir ibn Husayn defeated al-Amin's armies and besieged Baghdad, which fell in 813 with al-Amin's death. Al-Ma'mun remained in Merv for some years before entering Baghdad in 819, relying on Persian administrators such as al-Fadl ibn Sahl and on the Tahirid family he had raised to prominence.

Patronage of learning

Al-Ma'mun intensified Baghdad's role as a centre of learning, supporting the translation of Greek, Persian, and Indian works into Arabic associated with the Bayt al-Hikma (House of Wisdom) and translators such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq. He sponsored astronomers who measured the Earth's meridian degree, maintained observatories at Baghdad and Damascus, and corresponded with scholars; the geographers and mathematicians of his court, including al-Khwarizmi, dedicated works to him. He also adopted Mu'tazilite theology, designating in 817 the Alid Ali al-Rida as his heir — a controversial move reversed after al-Rida's death in 818.

The mihna and later reign

In 833 al-Ma'mun proclaimed the doctrine that the Qur'an was created and instituted the mihna, requiring judges and scholars to affirm it; traditionist Ahmad ibn Hanbal's refusal and punishment became its most famous episode. Al-Ma'mun campaigned in person against the Byzantine Empire in Anatolia in his last years and died suddenly near Tarsus in 833, reportedly after eating river dates and drinking cold water. He was succeeded by his brother al-Mu'tasim, who continued the mihna and the reliance on Turkish military slaves that reshaped the caliphate.
