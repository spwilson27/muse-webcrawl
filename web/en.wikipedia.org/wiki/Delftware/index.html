Delftware

[Image: Blue-and-white Delftware tiles and vases]
Delft blue pottery in the Rijksmuseum

Delftware or Delft pottery, also known as Delft Blue (Dutch: Delfts blauw), is a type of tin-glazed earthenware produced in and around Delft in the Netherlands from about 1620 to the late eighteenth century. It arose in response to the popularity of Chinese blue-and-white porcelain imported by the Dutch East India Company; when imports faltered during the Ming–Qing transition, Delft's potteries — many converted from breweries — filled the market with a local faience imitating porcelain.

Production and style

Delftware is made from a light earthenware body coated with an opaque white tin glaze, painted in cobalt blue (and later also manganese purple and polychrome enamels) and covered with a transparent lead glaze. The industry peaked between about 1650 and 1700, with more than thirty potteries active in Delft. Products ranged from tiles — produced by the million for fireplaces and walls — to plates, vases, tulip vases, jugs and ornamental plaques, decorated with Chinese kraak motifs, Dutch landscapes, biblical scenes and peasant figures. After 1700 the industry declined under competition from true porcelain (Meissen) and cheap English creamware; only a few factories survived into the 1800s.

Legacy

The last historic factory, De Porceleyne Fles (founded 1653, today Royal Delft), still produces hand-painted Delftware and is a major visitor attraction. Delft tiles and vases are held by museums worldwide and remain an emblem of Dutch Golden Age craft.
