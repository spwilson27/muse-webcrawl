The Electronic Delay Storage Automatic Calculator (EDSAC) was an early British computer built at the University of Cambridge Mathematical Laboratory under the direction of Maurice Wilkes. Inspired by John von Neumann's draft report on the EDVAC, it was one of the first practical stored-program computers and the first to provide a regular computing service to researchers.

EDSAC ran its first programs on 6 May 1949, calculating a table of squares and a list of prime numbers. It used mercury delay lines for its main memory, vacuum tubes (thermionic valves) for logic, and paper tape for input and output. Its initial order code — a set of single-letter instructions loaded from rotary switches — was a very early form of assembly language.

Design and operation

The machine stored 512 words of 18 bits, later doubled, and executed a few hundred instructions per second. Programs were punched on paper tape and results printed by teleprinter. Wilkes and his colleagues David Wheeler and Stanley Gill developed a library of subroutines and described them in The Preparation of Programs for an Electronic Digital Computer (1951), the first textbook on programming. Wheeler invented the subroutine "jump" (the Wheeler jump) for EDSAC.

Use and legacy

EDSAC supported research across Cambridge, including work in astronomy, crystallography and economics; Martin Ryle's radio astronomy group and others were early users. In 1952 Alexander S. Douglas wrote OXO, a tic-tac-toe program for EDSAC that is among the earliest video games. The machine also inspired the LEO computer, the first business computer. EDSAC was shut down in 1958 after being replaced by EDSAC 2. A working replica project was undertaken at The National Museum of Computing at Bletchley Park.
