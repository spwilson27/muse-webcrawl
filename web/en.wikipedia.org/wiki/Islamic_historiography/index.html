Islamic historiography

The historiography of early Islam concerns the writing of history by Muslim scholars from the 7th century CE onward. It encompasses the biography of the Prophet Muhammad (sīra), collections of his sayings and actions (hadith), tribal genealogies and battle narratives (ayyām al-ʿArab), and universal chronicles that expanded into one of the richest historical traditions of the pre-modern world.

Origins

Early Muslim historical writing grew out of hadith scholarship, with its characteristic chains of transmission (isnād) used to authenticate reports (akhbār). Historians such as Ibn Ishaq (d. 767), whose Sīrat Rasūl Allāh survives in the recension of Ibn Hisham, and al-Waqidi (d. 823), who chronicled Muhammad's campaigns (maghāzī), laid the foundations.

Classical period

By the 9th and 10th centuries, historians produced vast annals: al-Baladhuri's Futūḥ al-Buldān (Book of the Conquests) and genealogy works; al-Tabari's monumental History of the Prophets and Kings, a year-by-year chronicle from Creation to 915 CE; and al-Mas'udi's wide-ranging Meadows of Gold. Biographical dictionaries (ṭabaqāt) of scholars, judges, and transmitters became a distinctive genre.

Later developments

Court historians served the dynasties of Persia, Egypt, and the Ottoman world, writing in Arabic, Persian, and Turkish. Ibn Kathir, al-Maqrizi, and Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti are prominent later chroniclers. In the 14th century Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah (1377) offered a general theory of history and society, treating historiography critically and analyzing the rise and decline of dynasties — a work often considered a founding text of the philosophy of history and sociology.

Modern scholarship

Modern historians debate the reliability of early Islamic sources, most written down 150–250 years after the events. "Revisionist" scholars of the 1970s (e.g. John Wansbrough, Patricia Crone, Michael Cook) questioned traditional accounts, spurring new methods — including source criticism of isnāds, archaeology, coins, and non-Muslim sources — that continue to shape the field.
