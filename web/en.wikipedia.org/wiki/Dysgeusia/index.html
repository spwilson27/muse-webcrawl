Dysgeusia

Dysgeusia
SpecialtyNeurology; otorhinolaryngology
SymptomsDistorted sense of taste — metallic, salty, sour, foul
CausesMedications, infections, chemotherapy, zinc deficiency, nerve injury
Related termsAgeusia (loss), hypogeusia (reduction), parageusia

Dysgeusia, also known as parageusia, is a distortion of the sense of taste in which foods and drinks taste abnormal — commonly metallic, salty, rancid or foul — or a taste is perceived when nothing is in the mouth. It differs from ageusia, the complete loss of taste, and hypogeusia, a diminished taste sensitivity.

Causes

Dysgeusia has many causes. It is a common side effect of medications, including some antibiotics, blood-pressure drugs such as ACE inhibitors, and metformin, and is frequently reported during chemotherapy and radiation therapy to the head and neck. Infections of the upper respiratory tract — notably COVID-19, in which taste distortion often accompanies loss of smell — oral infections, poor oral hygiene, gastroesophageal reflux, zinc deficiency, pregnancy and hormonal changes, and injury to the taste nerves (facial, glossopharyngeal) during ear or dental surgery can all produce it.

Diagnosis and treatment

Evaluation includes a review of medications, oral and neurological examination and, where needed, formal taste testing with solutions of sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami stimuli. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause: symptoms often resolve when an offending drug is stopped or an infection clears; zinc supplementation is used when deficiency is present, and chemotherapy-related dysgeusia typically recovers over months after treatment ends. Persistent cases can impair appetite and nutrition, and are managed with dietary adjustments such as using plastic utensils to reduce metallic tastes and emphasising marinades and strong seasonings.
