Oak apple

An oak apple is a large, round gall commonly found on oak trees. Oak apples range from about 2 to 5 centimetres (1 to 2 inches) in diameter and are caused by chemicals injected by the larvae of certain gall wasps of the family Cynipidae — in Britain and much of Europe principally Biorhiza pallida, and in North America species such as Andricus californicus and Amphibolips confluenta.

Formation

In spring, the female wasp lays eggs in a developing oak leaf bud. The larva's secretions trigger the tree to grow a spongy, apple-like swelling around it, which supplies the larva with food and shelter. The gall is soft and green at first, becoming brown and woody as it matures; the adult wasp eventually chews its way out in summer. Unlike the hard, spherical marble galls, oak apples are spongy inside with a fibrous structure.

Oak Apple Day

Oak apples gave their name to Oak Apple Day (29 May), a former English public holiday celebrating the restoration of Charles II in 1660; according to tradition the king escaped capture after the Battle of Worcester by hiding in an oak tree. In some villages, including Castleton in Derbyshire, related garlanding ceremonies continue, and celebrants who failed to wear a sprig of oak on the day were traditionally liable to be pelted or pinched — in some places "nettled".
