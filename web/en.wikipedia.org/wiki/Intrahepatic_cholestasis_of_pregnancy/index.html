Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy

SpecialtyObstetrics, hepatology
SymptomsItching, especially of palms and soles, usually in the third trimester
RisksPreterm birth, meconium staining, stillbirth
DiagnosisElevated serum bile acids and liver transaminases

Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), also known as obstetric cholestasis, is a liver disorder of pregnancy characterized by intense itching (pruritus) without a rash and impaired flow of bile, reflected in raised serum bile acid levels. It usually develops in the late second or third trimester and resolves rapidly after delivery.

Causes

ICP results from a combination of genetic susceptibility — variants in hepatobiliary transport genes such as ABCB4 and ABCB11 have been implicated — and the cholestatic effects of pregnancy hormones, particularly estrogen and progesterone metabolites. It occurs in roughly 0.5–2% of pregnancies in many populations, with notably higher rates historically reported in Chile and Scandinavia, and it tends to recur in subsequent pregnancies.

Management

Treatment centres on ursodeoxycholic acid, which improves itching and biochemical markers, antihistamines for symptomatic relief, monitoring of bile acid levels and fetal surveillance, and planned early delivery — commonly recommended around 36–39 weeks depending on bile acid concentration — because the principal danger is to the fetus, including an increased risk of stillbirth at high bile acid levels. Maternal prognosis is generally good, though ICP is associated with a later increased risk of gallstones and hepatobiliary disease.
