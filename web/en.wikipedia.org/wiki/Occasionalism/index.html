Occasionalism

Occasionalism is a philosophical theory of causation according to which created substances cannot be efficient causes of events. Instead, all events are taken to be caused directly by God: finite things provide the "occasions" on which God acts. The doctrine is most closely associated with the followers of René Descartes, especially Nicolas Malebranche, and with the Ash'arite school of Islamic theology.

Origins

Occasionalist ideas appear in medieval Islamic theology, notably in the work of Abu Hamid al-Ghazali, whose Tahafut al-Falasifa (The Incoherence of the Philosophers, c. 1095) argued that observed connections between causes and effects are not necessary: when fire burns cotton, it is God, not the fire, that produces the burning. Jewish philosopher Maimonides discussed a similar position among the Mutakallimun.

Cartesian occasionalism

In seventeenth-century Europe, occasionalism arose as a response to the mind–body problem generated by Descartes's dualism: if mind and matter are entirely distinct substances, it is unclear how they could interact. Thinkers such as Louis de la Forge, Géraud de Cordemoy, Johannes Clauberg, and Arnold Geulincx proposed that God is the true cause of both mental and physical events, coordinating the two series on the "occasion" of changes in either. Malebranche gave the doctrine its most systematic form in The Search After Truth (1674–75), arguing that there is no necessary connection between any finite cause and its supposed effect, so all true causation belongs to God alone.

Criticism and influence

Critics objected that occasionalism makes God the direct author of sin, renders secondary causes superfluous, and turns nature into perpetual miracle. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz responded with his doctrine of pre-established harmony, and Baruch Spinoza's monism offered an alternative resolution. Occasionalism nonetheless influenced later philosophy, including the scepticism of David Hume about necessary connection, and remains a live topic in philosophy of causation and philosophical theology.
