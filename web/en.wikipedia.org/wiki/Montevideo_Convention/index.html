The Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States is a treaty signed at Montevideo, Uruguay, on 26 December 1933, during the Seventh International Conference of American States. It is famous for its Article 1, which sets out the most widely cited criteria for statehood under international law.

Criteria for statehood

Article 1 provides that the state as a person of international law should possess the following qualifications: (a) a permanent population; (b) a defined territory; (c) a government; and (d) a capacity to enter into relations with other states. Article 3 declares that the political existence of a state is independent of recognition by other states — the so-called declaratory theory of statehood, as opposed to the constitutive theory, under which recognition by others creates statehood.

Other provisions

The Convention also affirms that states are juridically equal, that no state has the right to intervene in the internal or external affairs of another, and that territory may not be acquired by force. The United States signed under President Franklin D. Roosevelt, consistent with the Good Neighbor policy toward Latin America.

Legacy

Although formally an inter-American regional treaty with a modest number of parties, the Montevideo criteria are generally accepted as reflecting customary international law and are routinely applied when the statehood of contested entities is debated.
