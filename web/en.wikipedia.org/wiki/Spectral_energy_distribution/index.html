Spectral energy distribution

A spectral energy distribution (SED) is a plot of energy versus frequency or wavelength of light, commonly used in astronomy to characterise astronomical sources such as stars, galaxies and quasars. The SED assembles photometric and spectroscopic measurements from across the electromagnetic spectrum — radio, infrared, optical, ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma-ray — into a single curve describing how the source's luminosity is distributed.

A multi-wavelength SED of a quasar, showing the "big blue bump" of the accretion disc and infrared re-emission by dust.

Construction

SEDs are assembled from archival data served by observatory archives and tools such as the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database and virtual-observatory SED builders. Axes are usually plotted as λFλ or νFν (energy per logarithmic interval) versus wavelength or frequency on logarithmic scales, so that equal areas under the curve correspond to equal energy output.

Applications

  • Stars: the SED approximates a blackbody; its peak gives effective temperature (by Wien's law) and its integral gives luminosity, hence radius when combined with distance (parallax).
  • Galaxies: fitting stellar-population and dust models to broadband SEDs yields photometric redshifts, star-formation rates and stellar masses — the basis of "SED fitting" in extragalactic surveys.
  • Active galactic nuclei: characteristic features (big blue bump, infrared torus bump, synchrotron and inverse-Compton components in blazars) diagnose the accretion flow and jets.
  • Young stellar objects: infrared excess over the stellar photosphere traces circumstellar discs and envelopes, defining the Class I/II/III evolutionary sequence.
