The Enūma Eliš (Akkadian: 𒂖𒉌𒈬𒂖𒂖), also spelled Enuma Elish, is the Babylonian creation epic, named for its incipit, "When on high". Written on seven clay tablets in Akkadian verse, it narrates the origin of the world from primordial waters, the war of the younger gods against the salt-water goddess Tiamat, and the exaltation of Marduk, the god of Babylon, as king of the gods. It was recited annually during Babylon's New Year (Akitu) festival.

Summary

In the beginning, the sweet waters Apsu and the salt waters Tiamat mingle and beget generations of gods, whose noise provokes Apsu to plot their destruction; the god Ea slays Apsu instead. Tiamat, enraged and goaded by her new consort Kingu, raises an army of monsters. The gods, terrified, accept the young Marduk as their champion after he demands supreme authority as his price. Marduk defeats Tiamat in single combat, splits her body "like a shellfish" to form heaven and earth, fixes the stars and the calendar, and creates humankind from the blood of Kingu to relieve the gods of labour. The grateful gods build Babylon and Marduk's temple Esagila, and proclaim his fifty names.

Discovery and significance

The text was recovered in fragmentary copies from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh and other sites after excavations beginning in 1849; George Smith published the first translation in 1876, drawing wide attention for its parallels — a primordial watery chaos, the splitting of a body to make the cosmos, a sequence of creation — with the Book of Genesis. Scholars read the epic as theological politics: the myth charter for Babylon's supremacy, with Marduk's rise mirroring the city's own, and in Assyrian copies the god's name was simply replaced by Ashur.
