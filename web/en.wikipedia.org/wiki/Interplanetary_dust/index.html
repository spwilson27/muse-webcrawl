Interplanetary dust


The zodiacal light is sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust

Interplanetary dust consists of tiny solid particles — typically micrometres to millimetres in size — distributed throughout the space between the planets of the Solar System. Collectively it forms the interplanetary dust cloud, whose scattered sunlight produces the zodiacal light, a faint cone of light visible along the ecliptic after sunset or before sunrise. Estimates of the total mass of dust falling onto Earth range from about 5 to 50 thousand tonnes per year.

Sources

The dust is continually replenished, because individual grains are short-lived: they spiral into the Sun under Poynting–Robertson drag, are ejected from the Solar System by radiation pressure, or are destroyed in collisions. The main sources are collisions in the asteroid belt and the disintegration and outgassing of comets; the dust bands discovered by the IRAS satellite are associated with particular asteroid families, while the smooth background cloud is attributed largely to Jupiter-family comets. Interstellar dust grains passing through the Solar System have also been detected directly by spacecraft.

Properties

Particles collected in Earth's stratosphere by high-flying aircraft — interplanetary dust particles (IDPs) — are typically porous aggregates of silicates, carbonaceous material and sulfides, often unlike any terrestrial rock. They are among the most primitive materials available for laboratory study and may have delivered water and organic compounds to the early Earth. Radar observations of meteors and in-situ detectors on spacecraft such as Pioneer, Ulysses, Galileo and Parker Solar Probe measure the dust's spatial distribution, which is concentrated towards the ecliptic plane and enhanced in rings and trails associated with Earth, Venus and cometary orbits.

Effects

Interplanetary dust affects spacecraft (impact erosion and a hazard to optics and solar panels), produces the meteor showers and sporadic meteors seen when larger grains burn up in planetary atmospheres, and seeds the upper atmosphere with meteoric smoke particles that influence noctilucent clouds. Its scattering and thermal emission are also a foreground that must be removed from observations of the cosmic microwave background and faint distant galaxies.
