Andrey Andreyevich Vlasov (14 September 1901 – 1 August 1946) was a Soviet Red Army general who, after being captured by Nazi Germany during the Second World War, defected and led the Russian Liberation Army (ROA), a formation of Soviet prisoners of war and émigrés that fought alongside Germany against the Soviet Union. His name became synonymous with collaboration in Soviet and post-Soviet Russia, where "Vlasovite" remains a term for a traitor.

Early career

Vlasov joined the Red Army in 1919 and fought in the Russian Civil War on the southern front. He rose steadily through the ranks, served as a military adviser in China in 1938–39, and by 1941 commanded the 4th Mechanised Corps in Ukraine. After Operation Barbarossa he distinguished himself in the defence of Kiev and in the counteroffensive before Moscow, where his 20th Army helped recapture Solnechnogorsk. Stalin decorated him and he was promoted to lieutenant general.

Capture and defection

In March 1942 Vlasov took command of the 2nd Shock Army on the Volkhov Front, tasked with breaking the siege of Leningrad. The army was encircled and destroyed in the Lyuban swamp in June 1942; Vlasov was captured on 12 July 1942. In captivity he agreed to cooperate with the Germans, presenting himself as an opponent of Stalinism rather than of Russia. He toured occupied territory and POW camps recruiting for a Russian army under German auspices.

Russian Liberation Army

Only in November 1944, with Germany losing the war, was Vlasov allowed to form the Committee for the Liberation of the Peoples of Russia (KONR) in Prague and nominally command its armed forces. Two ROA divisions were organised; the 1st Division briefly fought on the Oder front in April 1945 before turning against the Germans and aiding the Prague uprising in May.

Capture, trial and execution

Vlasov was captured by Soviet forces on 12 May 1945. He and eleven associates were tried in camera in Moscow and hanged on 1 August 1946. Debate continues over whether he was primarily an opportunist, a traitor, or a would-be national liberator; in Russia he remains a strongly negative figure, while some émigré and revisionist accounts have presented a more sympathetic view.
