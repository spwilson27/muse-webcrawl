Long-term support (LTS) is a product lifecycle management policy in which a stable release of computer software is maintained for a longer period than the standard edition. The term is typically reserved for special editions of software that are otherwise published under shorter support cycles. LTS editions promise extended periods of security updates and critical bug fixes, while generally avoiding potentially destabilising feature changes, making them attractive for enterprise and other conservative deployments.

Characteristics

An LTS release is maintained in parallel with faster-moving "standard" or "current" releases. During its support window it receives backported security patches and reliability fixes but few or no new features. Users trade access to the newest functionality for predictability, longer upgrade intervals and reduced regression risk. When support ends ("end of life"), users are expected to migrate to a newer release.

Examples

Well-known LTS schemes include Ubuntu Linux, whose LTS releases appear every two years with five years of standard support (extendable further with Ubuntu Pro); the Linux kernel, which designates certain versions for multi-year maintenance; Node.js, where even-numbered release lines enter LTS; and software such as Blender, Mozilla Firefox ESR and LibreOffice "Still" releases. Microsoft's Long-Term Servicing Channel (LTSC) editions of Windows apply the same idea commercially.

Rationale and criticism

LTS policies reduce total cost of ownership for large fleets and embedded systems. Critics note that long-lived releases can accumulate technical debt, that backporting fixes becomes harder over time, and that users may be stranded when support finally lapses.
