The Open Archives Initiative (OAI) was an organisation and effort that developed and promoted interoperability standards intended to facilitate the efficient dissemination of content, particularly scholarly literature held in e-print archives and institutional repositories. It is best known for the OAI Protocol for Metadata Harvesting (OAI-PMH), released in 2001 and revised as version 2.0 in 2002.

OAI-PMH

The protocol defines a simple HTTP-based mechanism by which data providers (repositories) expose metadata about their records, and service providers harvest that metadata to build union catalogues and search services. It has six request types ("verbs") — Identify, ListMetadataFormats, ListSets, ListIdentifiers, ListRecords and GetRecord — and requires repositories to be able to supply unqualified Dublin Core metadata in XML. Harvesting may be selective, by date range or by set membership, and supports resumption tokens for large result lists.

Origins and later work

The initiative grew out of a 1999 meeting in Santa Fe, New Mexico, convened around the Universal Preprint Service proposal, with early stewardship from figures including Herbert Van de Sompel and Carl Lagoze and funding from the Digital Library Federation, the Coalition for Networked Information and the US National Science Foundation. A later OAI project, Object Reuse and Exchange (OAI-ORE, 2008), defined a model for describing aggregations of web resources in Resource Maps, serialised in Atom and RDF.

Impact

OAI-PMH became a foundational plumbing standard of the open access movement: repository platforms such as EPrints, DSpace and Fedora included OAI endpoints by default, and directories such as OpenDOAR and services like BASE and CORE aggregated millions of records harvested with the protocol. (It is unrelated to the Open Source Initiative, a separate organisation concerned with software licences.)
