Bézout's identity (also called Bézout's lemma) is a theorem in elementary number theory: let a and b be integers with greatest common divisor d. Then there exist integers x and y such that ax + by = d. Moreover, the integers of the form az + bt are exactly the multiples of d.

History

The identity is named after the French mathematician Étienne Bézout, who proved it for polynomials in 1779; the integer case had earlier been stated by Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac in 1624. The coefficients x and y are called Bézout coefficients and can be computed with the extended Euclidean algorithm.

Applications

Bézout's identity is a cornerstone of elementary number theory. It implies that two integers are coprime if and only if 1 is a linear combination of them, underlies the proof of Euclid's lemma and hence the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, and is used in solving linear Diophantine equations and computing modular inverses. The identity generalises to any principal ideal domain; rings in which it holds are called Bézout domains (see Bézout domain).
