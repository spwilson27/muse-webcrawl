Constitutional Court of Turkey

The Constitutional Court of Turkey (Turkish: Anayasa Mahkemesi, abbreviated AYM) is the highest legal body for constitutional review in Turkey. It examines the constitutionality, in both form and substance, of laws, decrees having the force of law, and the Rules of Procedure of the Grand National Assembly of Turkey. It was established on 25 April 1962 under the Constitution of 1961, making Turkey one of the first countries in the region to adopt a dedicated constitutional court, modelled broadly on the post-war European examples.

Jurisdiction

The court reviews the constitutionality of statutes and decrees, tries senior officials — including the President, ministers, and high judges — in its capacity as the Supreme Criminal Tribunal (Yüce Divan), and decides on the dissolution of political parties and the deprivation of their state aid. It also audits the finances of political parties.

Since a constitutional amendment adopted by referendum in 2010, individuals may apply directly to the court claiming that a fundamental right guaranteed by the constitution and falling within the scope of the European Convention on Human Rights has been violated by public authorities. This "individual application" (bireysel başvuru) mechanism entered into force on 23 September 2012 and has since produced a large caseload concerning freedom of expression, fair trial and property rights.

Composition

The court consists of fifteen members. Twelve are appointed by the President of the Republic from among candidates nominated by the high courts, the Council of Higher Education and senior public administrators; three are elected by the Grand National Assembly. Members serve a single, non-renewable term of twelve years and retire at the age of 65. The members elect a president and two vice-presidents from among themselves for four-year terms.

Notable decisions

The court has played a central and sometimes controversial role in Turkish politics, dissolving numerous political parties, ruling on headscarf restrictions in universities, and adjudicating disputes between state institutions. In 2007 its decision requiring a quorum of 367 deputies for presidential elections — the so-called "367 decision" — triggered a political crisis and early elections. In 2008 it narrowly rejected a closure case against the governing party while imposing financial sanctions.
