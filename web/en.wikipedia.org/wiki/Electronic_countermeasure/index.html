Electronic countermeasure


An ECM pod fitted to a combat aircraft

An electronic countermeasure (ECM) is an electrical or electronic device designed to trick or deceive radar, sonar, or other detection systems, like infrared (IR) or lasers. It may be used both offensively and defensively to deny targeting information to an enemy. The system may make many separate targets appear to the enemy, or make the real target appear to disappear or move about randomly. It is used effectively to protect aircraft against guided missiles. Most air forces use ECM to protect their aircraft from attack. It has also been deployed by military ships and recently on some advanced tanks to fool laser/IR guided missiles.

History

Electronic countermeasures grew out of the radar battles of the Second World War. British night bombers dropped strips of aluminium foil, codenamed Window (known as chaff in the United States), to swamp German air-defence radar during the Hamburg raids of July 1943. Dedicated jamming aircraft and radar warning receivers soon followed, and ECM expanded throughout the Cold War as radar-guided surface-to-air missiles proliferated.

Types of countermeasures

Radar ECM

Radar ECM techniques include noise jamming, which floods a receiver with energy so the real echo is hidden; deception jamming, which manipulates return signals to produce false range, bearing, or velocity information; and expendables such as chaff and decoys.

Infrared countermeasures

Against heat-seeking missiles, aircraft use flares that burn hotter than the engine exhaust, and directional infrared countermeasures (DIRCM) that dazzle the missile seeker with a modulated laser beam.

Self-protection suites

Modern combat aircraft integrate radar warning receivers, missile approach warners, jammers, and expendable dispensers into a single electronic warfare suite that reacts automatically to threats within milliseconds.

Counter-countermeasures

Methods designed to defeat ECM are known as electronic counter-countermeasures (ECCM), including frequency agility, monopulse tracking, and home-on-jam modes in missiles.
