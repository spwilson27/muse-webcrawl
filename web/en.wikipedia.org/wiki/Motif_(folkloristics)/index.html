In folkloristics, a motif is a recurring, recognizable element within folktales, myths, legends, and other traditional narratives — such as a character, object, incident, or place — that appears across different stories and cultures. The American folklorist Stith Thompson defined the motif as "the smallest element in a tale having a power to persist in tradition," and devoted his monumental Motif-Index of Folk-Literature to cataloguing thousands of them.

Definition and nature

Motifs are the building blocks from which traditional tales are assembled. Thompson grouped them into three broad classes: actors (for example, a wicked stepmother, a helpful animal, or a youngest son), items (such as a magic ring, a forbidden chamber, or a glass mountain), and incidents (a single event, such as a hero's three tasks, a transformation, or a deception discovered). Most motifs are incidents. Because motifs recur independently in many traditions — the flood hero, the stolen bride, the magic object that grants wishes — they allow scholars to compare narratives across languages and continents and to trace the diffusion and transformation of stories through history.

The Motif-Index

Thompson's Motif-Index of Folk-Literature: A Classification of Narrative Elements in Folktales, Ballads, Myths, Fables, Mediaeval Romances, Exempla, Fabliaux, Jest-books, and Local Legends was published in six volumes between 1932 and 1936 and revised in 1955–1958. It organizes motifs into 23 lettered categories, from A (mythological motifs) through Z (groups of motifs), each subdivided by decimal numbers, so that a motif such as "magic sleep" or "rescue by a sibling" can be cited by a precise code — for instance, D1960 (magic sleep) or R111 (rescue of a captive). The Index works alongside the Aarne–Thompson–Uther Index, which classifies whole tale types: a tale type is typically a combination of many motifs.

Uses and criticism

Motif analysis underpins the historic-geographic method of folklore study and remains a standard reference for comparing tales, epics, and myths. Critics have noted that extracting motifs from context can flatten meaning, that the Index reflects the European and literary sources available to its compilers, and that structuralist approaches (such as Vladimir Propp's functions in Morphology of the Folktale) offer a complementary way of analyzing narrative. In literary studies, the word motif is also used more loosely for any recurring image, theme, or pattern within a work — a usage related to, but distinct from, the folkloristic technical sense.
