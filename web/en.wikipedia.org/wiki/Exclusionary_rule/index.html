Exclusionary rule

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The exclusionary rule is a legal principle in the United States under which evidence collected in violation of a defendant's constitutional rights — most often the Fourth Amendment's ban on unreasonable searches and seizures — is inadmissible in a criminal trial. Derived rather than stated in the Constitution, it was applied to federal cases in Weeks v. United States (1914) and imposed on the states in Mapp v. Ohio (1961), with the derivative "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine excluding evidence obtained through the tainted evidence.

Rationale and exceptions

The rule's stated purpose is deterrence of police misconduct (an earlier rationale invoked judicial integrity); its cost — reliable evidence lost, sometimes freeing the guilty ("the criminal is to go free because the constable has blundered", in Cardozo's phrase) — drives continuing reform proposals and judicially carved exceptions: good-faith reliance on a defective warrant (United States v. Leon, 1984), inevitable discovery, independent source, attenuation, and limits on who may invoke it (standing, impeachment use, grand jury and civil contexts). Most other democracies admit unlawfully obtained evidence subject to balancing or discretion, making the US rule comparatively distinctive — a standing case study in how remedies shape rights.
