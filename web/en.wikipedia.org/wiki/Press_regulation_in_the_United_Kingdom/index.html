Press regulation in the United Kingdom

Press regulation in the United Kingdom concerns the systems by which British newspapers and magazines are held to standards of accuracy, privacy and conduct. Unlike broadcasting, which is statutorily regulated by Ofcom, the printed press has historically been self-regulated: publishers voluntarily join a regulator that enforces a code of practice and handles complaints. This arrangement has been repeatedly challenged by scandals and public inquiries, and its reform remains politically contentious.

Early history

Pre-publication censorship ended when the Licensing Act lapsed in 1695, but governments long controlled newspapers through stamp duties, libel law and contempt rules. The modern era of self-regulation began with the voluntary General Council of the Press, created in 1953 after a royal commission, followed by the Press Council in 1963. Two further royal commissions (1947–49, 1962 and 1974–77) criticised standards but stopped short of statutory control.

The Press Complaints Commission

The Calcutt Committee of 1990, dismayed by intrusive reporting, led to the replacement of the Press Council by the Press Complaints Commission (PCC) in 1991, enforcing an Editors' Code of Practice covering accuracy, privacy, harassment and the treatment of children and victims. The PCC was widely criticised as ineffective — it could not fine or compel apologies of equal prominence — and a second Calcutt review in 1993 recommended a statutory tribunal, which the government rejected.

Phone hacking and the Leveson Inquiry

Revelations that journalists at the News of the World had hacked the voicemail of murdered schoolgirl Milly Dowler triggered the paper's closure in 2011 and the Leveson Inquiry into press culture, practices and ethics. Lord Justice Leveson's 2012 report proposed a new regulator, independent of both industry and government but underpinned by statute. The PCC closed in 2014.

The current system

Most major publishers instead joined the Independent Press Standards Organisation (IPSO), a self-regulator that can investigate, fine up to £1 million and order corrections. A smaller regulator, IMPRESS, became the first to be recognised by the Press Recognition Panel under the 2013 Royal Charter framework; some national titles, including the Guardian and the Financial Times, regulate themselves through internal ombudsmen and readers' editors. The government announced in 2018 that the second part of the Leveson Inquiry would not proceed and that the costs-shifting incentives in the Crime and Courts Act 2013 would be repealed, leaving the post-Leveson settlement only partially implemented.
