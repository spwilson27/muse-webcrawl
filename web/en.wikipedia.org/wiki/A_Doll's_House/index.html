A Doll's House

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Helmers' drawing room at Christmas: Nora in a fancy-dress costume by the decorated tree, in a still from the play's domestic world]

A Doll's House (Norwegian: Et dukkehjem) is a three-act play by Henrik Ibsen, premiered at the Royal Theatre in Copenhagen on 21 December 1879. It is the most famous of Ibsen's plays and one of the most performed plays in the world: the story of Nora Helmer, a bank manager's seemingly frivolous wife, who forged her father's signature to borrow money that saved her husband's life — and who, when the debt is exposed and her husband's gratitude turns to rage and shame, recognises the doll's house of her marriage and walks out of it, slamming the door.

The play grew from the case of Ibsen's acquaintance Laura Kieler, who had taken a secret loan and been committed to an asylum by her husband. Ibsen denied writing a feminist tract — he claimed to describe humanity — but Nora's exit, her insistence that she is a human being before a wife and mother (in the usual summaries of her final speech), made the play a founding text of modern feminism and of modern drama alike.

The German ending

For the 1880 German production, whose leading actress refused to play a mother leaving her children, Ibsen was forced to supply an alternative ending in which Nora, shown the children, collapses and stays — a 'barbaric outrage' he performed on his own work under protest, as he called it, and which theatres soon abandoned. The episode remains the classic case study in an author's rights against the commercial stage.

Legacy

Translated into every theatrical language, endlessly revived and rewritten (including contemporary sequels and re-imaginings), the play's door-slam — Bernard Shaw heard in it the end of an era — still anchors arguments about marriage, debt, respectability and selfhood.
