Strangling

Strangling is compression of the neck that may lead to unconsciousness or death by causing an increasingly hypoxic state in the brain. Strangling can be broadly divided into three types: hanging, ligature strangulation, and manual strangulation. Fatal strangling typically occurs in cases of violence, accidents, and is one of the principal methods of suicide and execution.

Structures of the neck vulnerable to compression.

Mechanism

Strangling obstructs blood flow to and from the brain and/or airflow through the trachea. Compression of the carotid arteries interrupts the supply of oxygenated blood to the brain; compression of the jugular veins prevents drainage; and compression of the airway obstructs breathing. Relatively little pressure is required on the carotid arteries — around 5 kg (11 lb) — compared with the much greater force needed to occlude the trachea. Unconsciousness can follow within seconds, and death within minutes if compression is maintained.

Classification

  • Hanging — suspension of the body by a ligature around the neck, where the body's weight provides the compressing force.
  • Ligature strangulation — a constricting band tightened by a force other than body weight.
  • Manual strangulation — compression by hands, forearms, or other limbs; in martial arts contexts, chokeholds and strangleholds are grappling techniques that apply similar pressure, usually to subdue rather than kill.

Medical effects

Survivors can suffer injuries including damage to the larynx, fractures of the hyoid bone or thyroid cartilage, neurological deficits, and delayed complications such as airway swelling. Petechial haemorrhages in the eyes and face are characteristic forensic findings. Strangulation in the context of domestic violence is recognised as a significant risk factor for later homicide, and many jurisdictions have made non-fatal strangulation a specific criminal offence.
