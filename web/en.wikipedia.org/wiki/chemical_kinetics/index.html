Chemical kinetics

Chemical kinetics, also known as reaction kinetics, is the branch of physical chemistry that studies the rates of chemical reactions and the factors that affect them, as well as the molecular mechanisms by which reactions proceed. It contrasts with chemical thermodynamics, which concerns equilibrium but not speed.

Rate laws

The rate of a reaction is commonly expressed through a rate law relating the rate to the concentrations of reactants raised to powers called reaction orders. For a reaction aA + bB → products, the rate often takes the form rate = k[A]m[B]n, where k is the rate constant. Orders are determined experimentally and need not equal the stoichiometric coefficients. Integrated rate laws describe how concentrations change with time for zero-, first-, and second-order reactions; the half-life is the time for a reactant's concentration to fall by half.

Temperature dependence

The temperature dependence of reaction rates is described by the Arrhenius equation, k = A·e−Ea/RT, involving an activation energy Ea and a pre-exponential factor A. Collision theory and transition state theory give microscopic interpretations: molecules must collide with sufficient energy and proper orientation, passing through an activated complex at the top of the energy barrier.

Other factors

Rates are also affected by catalysts, which provide an alternative pathway of lower activation energy; by concentration and pressure; by the surface area of solids; and by light in photochemical reactions. Enzyme kinetics, described by the Michaelis–Menten equation, is an important special case.
