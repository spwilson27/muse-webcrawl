Cuban cuisine

Ropa vieja served with rice, black beans and plátanos maduros.

Cuban cuisine is the cooking tradition of Cuba, a fusion of Spanish, African, Taíno and Caribbean influences, later shaped by Chinese, French and American elements. Because of trade restrictions and rationing on the island, Cuban cooking at home tends to rely on a modest pantry of staples, while the cuisine of the Cuban diaspora — especially in Miami — is more elaborate.

Staples and techniques

Almost every savoury dish begins with a sofrito of onion, bell pepper, garlic, cumin and oregano sautéed in oil. Black beans and white rice are the foundation of the table; moros y cristianos ("Moors and Christians") cooks the two together. Root vegetables such as yuca, malanga and boniato, and plantains in every stage of ripeness — green tostones, sweet maduros, and chips — are ubiquitous.

Signature dishes

Ropa vieja, shredded beef stewed in a tomato-based sauce, is often called Cuba's national dish. Other standards include lechón asado (mojo-marinated roast pork), picadillo (seasoned ground beef with olives and raisins), arroz con pollo, vaca frita (crisped flank steak with onions), and the pressed Cuban sandwich of ham, roast pork, Swiss cheese, pickles and mustard.

Drinks and desserts

Strong, sweet espresso-style cafecito anchors the day. Cuba gave the world the mojito, the Cuba libre and the daiquirí. Desserts include flan, arroz con leche, guava pastries and tres leches cake.
