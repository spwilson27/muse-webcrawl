Ion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A diagram of a sodium atom losing an electron to become a positively charged sodium ion]
A diagram of a sodium atom losing an electron to become a positively charged sodium ion

An ion is an atom or molecule with a net electrical charge, having gained or lost one or more electrons. Positively charged ions are cations (attracted to the cathode in electrolysis), negatively charged ones anions. Ions are fundamental to chemistry and biology: they conduct electricity in solutions and plasmas, form ionic compounds such as table salt, and carry signals in nerves, muscles and batteries.

Formation and properties

Atoms ionise by losing electrons (typically metals, forming cations like Na⁺ and Ca²⁺) or gaining them (non-metals, forming anions like Cl⁻ and O²⁻), driven by the stability of complete electron shells. Ions in gas phase are produced by radiation, electrical discharge or heat — the upper atmosphere's Ionosphere — and in solution by the dissociation of salts, acids and bases.

Roles

Electrolytes (sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride) regulate fluid balance, nerve impulses (Action potential) and pH in living bodies. Ion channels and pumps in cell membranes manage this traffic. In technology, ion exchange purifies water, Mass spectrometry weighs ions to identify substances, ion thrusters propel spacecraft, and lithium-ion batteries shuttle Li⁺ between electrodes.

Naming and notation

The terms were coined by Michael Faraday (from Greek 'going') in his electrolysis research of the 1830s. Charges are written as superscripts (Fe³⁺, SO₄²⁻); polyatomic ions such as ammonium (NH₄⁺) and sulfate behave as single units in reactions.
