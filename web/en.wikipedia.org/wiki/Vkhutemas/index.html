Vkhutemas

Vkhutemas (Russian: Вхутемас, an acronym for Высшие художественно-технические мастерские, "Higher Art and Technical Studios") was a state art and technical school in Moscow, founded in 1920 by decree of the Soviet government. Together with the German Bauhaus, it was one of the two most important schools of modernist art, architecture and design in the interwar period, and a centre of the Russian avant-garde movements of Constructivism, Rationalism and Suprematism.

History

Vkhutemas was created by merging the Stroganov School of Applied Arts with the Moscow School of Painting, Sculpture and Architecture, following the earlier establishment of the Free State Art Studios (Svomas). Its first rector was Yefim Ravdel. The school enrolled thousands of students across faculties of painting, sculpture, architecture, textiles, ceramics, woodwork, metalwork and printing.

A compulsory preliminary course, the Basic Division, introduced all students to the analysis of form, colour, space and volume — a system comparable to the Bauhaus Vorkurs, developed by teachers such as Lyubov Popova, Alexander Rodchenko, Varvara Stepanova and Gustav Klutsis. The architecture faculty, led by figures including Nikolai Ladovsky and the Vesnin brothers, became the cradle of Rationalist and Constructivist architecture.

Workshops and pedagogy

The school's psychotechnical laboratory and Ladovsky's "space" exercises trained students' perception of architectural form through abstract compositional tasks. Student designs for workers' clubs, communal housing, textiles, theatre sets and photomontage fed directly into Soviet cultural production. The school exhibited internationally, including at the 1925 Paris Exposition, where Rodchenko's Workers' Club installation won acclaim.

Dissolution and legacy

In 1927 the school was reorganised as Vkhutein (Higher Art and Technical Institute), and in 1930, as Stalinist cultural policy turned against the avant-garde, it was dissolved and its faculties dispersed into other institutes. Its methods profoundly influenced modern design education and, through exchanges with teachers and exhibitions, the Bauhaus itself, with which Vkhutemas is frequently paired.
