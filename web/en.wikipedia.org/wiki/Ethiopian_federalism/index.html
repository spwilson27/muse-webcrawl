Ethiopian federalism

Ethiopian federalism is the system of government of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, established by the Constitution of 1995. It divides the country into regional states drawn largely along ethnic and linguistic lines, making Ethiopia a distinctive example of ethnic federalism: a federation in which the constituent units are intended to correspond to "nations, nationalities and peoples."

Constitutional framework

Article 8 of the 1995 constitution vests sovereignty in the "Nations, Nationalities and Peoples of Ethiopia" rather than in citizens as individuals. Article 39 grants every nation, nationality and people an unconditional right to self-determination, including secession — a clause unique among modern constitutions. The federation consists of the federal government and the regional states, each with its own constitution, council (legislature), executive and judiciary. The federal parliament has two chambers: the House of Peoples' Representatives, elected by population, and the House of the Federation, composed of representatives of the nations and nationalities, which decides constitutional disputes, self-determination questions and revenue sharing.

Regional states

At its adoption the federation had nine regional states (kililoch): Tigray, Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Somali, Benishangul-Gumuz, the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples' Region (SNNPR), Gambela and Harari, plus the chartered cities of Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa. Internal referendums later created new states: Sidama (2020), South West Ethiopia Peoples' Region (2021), and in 2023 the SNNPR was replaced by the South Ethiopia and Central Ethiopia regions, bringing the total to twelve regions.

Origins and debate

The system emerged from the 1991 overthrow of the Derg by the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF), a coalition of ethnically organised movements led by the Tigray People's Liberation Front. Its architects argued that ethnic federalism answered the "national question" that had fuelled decades of insurgency under the empire and the Derg. Critics contend it institutionalised ethnic identity, hardened internal boundaries, and contributed to inter-regional conflicts and displacement, while defenders credit it with recognising Ethiopia's diversity and enabling mother-tongue education and local self-rule. Debate intensified after 2018 under Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, whose Prosperity Party merged the EPRDF's ethnic parties, and during and after the Tigray War (2020–2022).
