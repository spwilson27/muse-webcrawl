Goon (ice hockey)

A goon is a player in ice hockey whose primary role is that of an enforcer: to fight opposing players and intimidate opponents in order to protect teammates, particularly star players, and to respond to violent play by the other team. The role is most associated with the National Hockey League (NHL) of the 1970s and 1980s, when fighting was a prominent part of the game.

Role

Enforcers are typically forwards or defencemen with limited scoring ability but considerable size and fighting skill. They may be deployed for only a few minutes per game, often in response to a hard hit on a teammate or a fight by an opposing enforcer. The unwritten "code" of hockey fighting governs when fights occur and is generally understood by players and officials.

History

The enforcer role became prominent with teams such as the Philadelphia Flyers of the mid-1970s, nicknamed the "Broad Street Bullies", who won two Stanley Cups with an intimidating style. Players such as Dave "The Hammer" Schultz, Bob Probert, Tie Domi and Marty McSorley became famous in the role. The 1977 film Slap Shot satirised goon hockey.

Decline

From the 1990s onward, rule changes (notably the instigator rule), the introduction of the salary cap, and a shift toward speed and skill reduced the role; concussion concerns further diminished it. By the 2010s, most NHL teams no longer dressed a dedicated enforcer, though players who combine toughness with playing ability remain valued.
