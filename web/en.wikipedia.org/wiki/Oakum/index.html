Oakum

Oakum is a preparation of tarred fibre traditionally used to seal the seams of wooden ships and to pack the joints of cast-iron pipes. It was made primarily from old tarred ropes and cordage, picked apart into loose fibres — work historically assigned to prisoners and workhouse inmates, a punishment and labour known as "picking oakum".

Use in shipbuilding

In wooden ship construction, oakum was driven into the seams between hull planks with a caulking iron and mallet, then sealed with hot pitch or marine glue — a process called caulking — to make the hull watertight. Untarred ("white") oakum was used where tar might stain, and in plumbing, oakum packed into bell-and-spigot joints of cast-iron soil pipes before molten lead was poured in to seal them.

Oakum picking

Picking oakum was notorious as monotonous, painful labour in Victorian prisons and workhouses: inmates were given set weights of old rope to unpick each day, and the task appears in accounts by writers such as Charles Dickens and in Oscar Wilde's accounts of prison life. The practice was gradually abolished in the early 20th century.
