Managed care

Managed care, or managed healthcare, is a system of health care delivery in the United States intended to reduce the cost of providing health benefits and improve the quality of care. Organizations that provide managed care are called managed care organizations (MCOs). The term generally describes techniques used by insurers and provider networks to control utilization and cost, including provider networks, prior authorization, utilization review, and financial incentives.

Forms

  • Health maintenance organization (HMO) — patients choose a primary care physician who coordinates care and provides referrals; care is generally covered only within a network, often paid by capitation.
  • Preferred provider organization (PPO) — a network of providers at negotiated rates; patients may see out-of-network providers at higher cost without referrals.
  • Point of service (POS) — a hybrid of HMO and PPO features.
  • Accountable care organization (ACO) — provider groups that share savings if they meet quality and cost targets, notably under Medicare.

History

Prepaid group practices such as Kaiser Permanente pioneered the model in the mid-20th century. The federal Health Maintenance Organization Act of 1973 promoted HMOs, and during the 1980s and 1990s managed care became the dominant form of employer-sponsored insurance in the United States, largely replacing traditional indemnity ("fee-for-service") plans. A consumer and provider backlash in the late 1990s led to looser restrictions, external review rights, and "patients' bill of rights" legislation in many states.

Criticism

Critics argue that utilization management can delay or deny needed care and burdens clinicians with administrative work; supporters argue managed care reduces unnecessary services and controls premiums. Managed care techniques have also been adopted within public programs, such as Medicaid managed care and Medicare Advantage.
