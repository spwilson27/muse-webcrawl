Glossolalia

Glossolalia, commonly called speaking in tongues, is the phenomenon of speaking in an utterance perceived as a language unknown to the speaker. It is practised in Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, where it is understood as a gift of the Holy Spirit, and occurs in some other religious traditions as well.

Biblical accounts

In the Acts of the Apostles, the disciples at Pentecost are described as speaking in languages understood by foreign listeners — a phenomenon scholars distinguish as xenoglossy. In his First Epistle to the Corinthians, Paul discusses glossolalia as a spiritual gift, encouraging order in worship and pairing it with the gift of interpretation.

Modern practice

Modern Pentecostalism traces its emphasis on tongues to revivals such as the Azusa Street Revival (1906) in Los Angeles. Some denominations teach that speaking in tongues is the initial evidence of baptism in the Holy Spirit; others regard it as one gift among many.

Linguistic and psychological study

Linguists who have analysed recordings generally find that glossolalic speech has the phonetic patterns of the speaker's native language but lacks the consistent semantic structure of a real language. Psychologists have studied it in connection with trance, learned behaviour, and religious experience.
