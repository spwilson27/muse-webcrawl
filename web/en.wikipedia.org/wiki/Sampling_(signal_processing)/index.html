Sampling (signal processing)

In signal processing, sampling is the reduction of a continuous-time signal to a discrete-time signal. A common example is the conversion of a sound wave to a sequence of "samples". A sample is a value of the signal at a point in time and/or space; this definition differs from the term's usage in statistics, which refers to a set of such values.

A sampler is a subsystem or operation that extracts samples from a continuous signal. A theoretical ideal sampler produces samples equivalent to the instantaneous value of the continuous signal at the desired points. The original signal can be reconstructed from a sequence of samples, up to the limit set by the Nyquist rate, by passing the sequence of samples through a type of low-pass filter called a reconstruction filter.

Theory

Sampling can be done for functions varying in space, time, or any other dimension, and similar results are obtained in two or more dimensions. For functions that vary with time, let s(t) be a continuous function (or "signal") to be sampled, and let sampling be performed by measuring the value of the continuous function every T seconds, which is called the sampling interval or sampling period. Then the sampled function is given by the sequence s(nT), for integer values of n. The sampling frequency or sampling rate, fs, is the average number of samples obtained in one second, thus fs = 1/T, with units of samples per second, sometimes referred to as hertz.

Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem

The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that a band-limited signal can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples if the sampling rate is more than twice the highest frequency present in the signal. Frequencies above half the sampling rate — the Nyquist frequency — cannot be distinguished and fold back into lower frequencies, an effect known as aliasing. Practical systems use an anti-aliasing filter before sampling to limit the signal's bandwidth.

Types of sampling

Periodic sampling

Periodic sampling is the regular, uniform sampling used in most digital audio and image systems. The compact disc, for example, samples audio at 44,100 samples per second.

Oversampling and undersampling

Oversampling samples a signal at a rate significantly higher than the Nyquist rate, easing the requirements on filters and improving resolution when combined with quantization noise shaping. Undersampling samples a bandpass signal at a rate below its Nyquist rate yet still allows reconstruction, exploiting the signal's limited bandwidth.

Applications

Sampling is fundamental to analog-to-digital conversion, digital audio, digital video, digital photography, and software-defined radio. In audio, typical sample rates are 44.1 kHz for compact discs and 48 kHz for video production.
