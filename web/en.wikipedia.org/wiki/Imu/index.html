Imu

An imu is a traditional Hawaiian underground oven used for cooking large quantities of food, most famously the whole pig served at a lūʻau as kālua pork. The word also refers to the pit and cooking method itself.

Method

A pit is dug and a fire built inside it to heat volcanic rocks. When the rocks are glowing, they are levelled, lined with vegetation such as banana stalks and ti leaves, and the seasoned food — often a whole pig — is placed on top. More leaves cover the food, followed by wet burlap and a layer of earth to trap the steam. The food slow-cooks for many hours, emerging tender and smoky.

Cultural role

Preparing an imu is communal work central to celebrations and gatherings in Hawaiʻi, and the method resembles earth ovens used across Polynesia, such as the Māori hāngī.
