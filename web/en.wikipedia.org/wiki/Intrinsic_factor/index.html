Intrinsic factor (IF), also known as cobalamin binding intrinsic factor, is a glycoprotein produced by the parietal cells of the stomach lining. It is essential for the absorption of vitamin B12 (cobalamin) in the terminal ileum of the small intestine. In the stomach, B12 released from food is first bound by haptocorrin (R-protein); in the duodenum, pancreatic proteases degrade haptocorrin and B12 transfers to intrinsic factor. The IF–B12 complex travels to the ileum, where it binds the cubilin–amnionless (cubam) receptor on enterocytes and is taken up by endocytosis.

Clinical significance

Lack of intrinsic factor causes vitamin B12 malabsorption and pernicious anaemia — a megaloblastic anaemia with potential neurological damage. Most often this results from autoimmune atrophic gastritis, in which autoantibodies destroy parietal cells or target intrinsic factor itself; it can also follow gastrectomy or bariatric surgery, or arise from rare congenital mutations in the CBLIF gene. Before the 1920s pernicious anaemia was fatal; Minot and Murphy's liver diet (1926) and the later isolation of B12 transformed treatment, and modern management uses intramuscular or high-dose oral B12, bypassing IF-dependent absorption. Anti–intrinsic factor antibodies are a specific diagnostic test, and the historical Schilling test once localised the defect directly.

History

William Bosworth Castle coined the terms "extrinsic factor" (in food, later identified as vitamin B12) and "intrinsic factor" (in gastric juice) in the late 1920s, after showing that normal gastric juice plus beef muscle could cure pernicious anaemia whereas neither alone could.
