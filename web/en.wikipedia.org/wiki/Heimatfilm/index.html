Heimatfilm

The Heimatfilm (German for "homeland film") is a popular German film genre that developed in the years after the Second World War and was especially successful during the 1950s. Heimatfilme were characterised by rural settings, sentimental plots centred on love, family and friendship, and an emphasis on traditional morality and the beauty of the German and Austrian countryside.

Characteristics

The typical Heimatfilm is set in an idyllic mountain or forest region — the Alps, the Black Forest, or the Lüneburg Heath. Plots often revolve around a conflict between tradition and modernity, the return of a prodigal son or daughter to the village, or a romance threatened by class difference or an unscrupulous outsider, and are resolved in favour of the traditional rural community. Nature is filmed in lush colour, and folk music and regional dress feature prominently.

History

Precursors existed in the mountain films of the Weimar era and in films of the Nazi period, but the genre flowered after 1945, when escapist entertainment appealed to audiences including millions of expellees from the eastern territories. Landmark successes included Schwarzwaldmädel (1950) and Grün ist die Heide (1951). Hundreds of Heimatfilme were produced in West Germany and Austria during the 1950s.

By the late 1960s the genre appeared dated, and younger filmmakers of the New German Cinema parodied or subverted it; the "anti-Heimatfilm" used the rural setting to expose provincial bigotry. Edgar Reitz's television epic Heimat (1984) offered a complex, critically acclaimed reworking of the theme. A softened revival of Heimat themes has appeared in German television films since the 1990s.
