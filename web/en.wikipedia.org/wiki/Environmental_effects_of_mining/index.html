Environmental effects of mining

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The environmental effects of mining include erosion, formation of sinkholes, loss of biodiversity, and contamination of soil, groundwater, and surface water by chemicals from mining processes. In some cases, additional forest logging is done near mines to increase available room for storing the created debris and soil.

Water pollution

Acid mine drainage occurs when sulfide minerals exposed by mining react with air and water to produce sulfuric acid, which can leach heavy metals such as lead, cadmium, and arsenic into streams, harming aquatic life for decades or centuries after a mine closes. Tailings dam failures, such as the 2015 Mariana and 2019 Brumadinho disasters in Brazil, can release vast quantities of toxic slurry.

Land and air impacts

Surface mining, including mountaintop removal, removes vegetation and topsoil and reshapes landscapes, while underground mines can cause subsidence. Mining operations also emit dust and greenhouse gases, and gold mining with mercury amalgamation contaminates air and water and exposes workers and nearby communities to mercury poisoning.

Mitigation

Mitigation measures include environmental impact assessments before permitting, water treatment, safe tailings management, and mine reclamation — restoring mined land once operations end. Regulations such as the U.S. Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977 require reclamation bonding and restoration of mined lands.
