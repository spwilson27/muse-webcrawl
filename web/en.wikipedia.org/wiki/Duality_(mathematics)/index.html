Duality (mathematics)

The cube and its dual, the octahedron

In mathematics, a duality translates concepts, theorems or mathematical structures into other concepts, theorems or structures in a one-to-one fashion, often by means of an involution: if the dual of A is B, then the dual of B is A. Such involutions sometimes have fixed points, so that the dual of A is A itself. Dualities are pervasive across mathematics and often allow a difficult problem to be restated in a form in which it becomes easier, or a theorem proved in one setting to be carried over automatically to a "mirror" setting.

Examples

Geometry

In projective geometry, points and lines (or, in three dimensions, points and planes) can be interchanged in any true statement to obtain another true statement: two points determine a line, and dually two lines determine a point. For convex polyhedra, dual polyhedra pair vertices with faces — the cube is dual to the octahedron, and the dodecahedron to the icosahedron.

Algebra and order theory

In order theory, reversing all inequalities exchanges meets and joins, maxima and minima. In linear algebra, vectors and linear functionals are related through the dual space. In logic and set theory, De Morgan's laws express the duality between conjunction and disjunction, and between union and intersection under complementation.

Analysis and optimisation

Fourier analysis exhibits a duality between a function and its frequency representation. In convex optimisation, every minimisation problem has a dual maximisation problem; under suitable conditions (strong duality), the two have equal optimal values, a principle exploited throughout linear programming. Pontryagin duality generalises the Fourier transform to locally compact abelian groups, and Poincaré duality relates the homology and cohomology of a closed orientable manifold.

Category-theoretic view

In category theory, duality is systematised by the notion of the opposite category, in which all arrows are reversed: a statement about a category yields, by reversing arrows, a dual statement that holds automatically. Limits and colimits, monomorphisms and epimorphisms, and products and coproducts are dual pairs in this sense.
