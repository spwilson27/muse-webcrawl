Jacob Ludwig Carl Grimm (4 January 1785 – 20 September 1863), usually known as Jacob Grimm, was a German philologist, linguist, lawyer, and folklorist. He formulated Grimm's law, the description of the regular sound changes separating the Germanic languages from other Indo-European languages, and together with his younger brother Wilhelm Grimm collected and published the famous Kinder- und Hausmärchen ("Children's and Household Tales"), known in English as Grimm's Fairy Tales.

Life

Grimm was born in Hanau and studied law at the University of Marburg, where the lectures of Friedrich Carl von Savigny sparked his interest in historical scholarship. He worked in Paris for Savigny, served as a librarian in Kassel, and in 1829 became professor at the University of Göttingen. In 1837 he was dismissed as one of the "Göttingen Seven" who protested King Ernest Augustus I's abrogation of the Hanoverian constitution. From 1841 he worked in Berlin at the invitation of Frederick William IV of Prussia.

Work

Jacob's Deutsche Grammatik (1819–1837) laid the foundations of Germanic philology; its second edition (1822) contained the first statement of the consonant shifts now known as Grimm's law. He also authored works on German mythology (Deutsche Mythologie, 1835) and legal antiquities. With Wilhelm, he began the monumental Deutsches Wörterbuch, the comprehensive dictionary of the German language, begun in 1838 and completed only in 1961, more than a century after their deaths.

Fairy tales

The brothers' collection of folk tales, first published in 1812 and expanded through seven editions by 1857, was intended as a scholarly record of German folk tradition but became one of the most beloved children's books in the world, including tales such as "Cinderella," "Hansel and Gretel," "Rapunzel," and "Snow White."
