Convergent evolution

Convergent evolution is the independent evolution of similar features in species of different periods or epochs in time. Convergent evolution creates analogous structures that have similar form or function but were not present in the last common ancestor of those groups. The cladistic term for the same phenomenon is homoplasy. The recurrence of similar structures arises because similar environments exert similar selective pressures, so natural selection favors similar adaptations.

Examples

Flight has evolved independently in insects, pterosaurs, birds, and bats, producing wings that are analogous rather than homologous in their detailed structure. Streamlined, fusiform bodies with fins and flippers evolved separately in sharks (fish), ichthyosaurs (reptiles), and dolphins (mammals). Camera-type eyes evolved independently in vertebrates and cephalopods. On islands, marsupial mammals in Australia evolved counterparts of placental mammals elsewhere, such as the marsupial mole and the placental mole, and the thylacine and the wolf. The succulent, water-storing forms of cacti (Americas) and euphorbias (Africa) are a well-known plant example.

Distinguishing convergence

Convergent evolution is distinguished from parallel evolution, in which similar changes occur in closely related lineages, and from divergent evolution, in which related lineages accumulate differences. Convergence must also be distinguished from evolutionary relay and mimicry. Because analogous structures can mislead classification, systematists seek characters whose similarity reflects shared ancestry (homology) rather than shared function.

Significance

Convergence is taken as evidence for the power and predictability of natural selection: similar challenges tend to produce similar solutions. At the molecular level, convergent substitutions — for example in the echolocation gene Prestin in bats and toothed whales — show that convergence can extend to the level of DNA and protein sequences.
