Colonel Sanders

Harland David Sanders (September 9, 1890 – December 16, 1980), commonly known as Colonel Sanders, was an American entrepreneur who founded the fast-food chicken restaurant chain Kentucky Fried Chicken (KFC). His image — white suit, black string tie, glasses, goatee, and walking cane — remains the company's principal symbol.

Early life

Sanders was born in Henryville, Indiana. After his father's death when he was five, he learned to cook for his younger siblings. He left home as a teenager and worked a succession of jobs, including farmhand, streetcar conductor, railroad fireman, insurance salesman, and service station operator, and briefly studied law by correspondence.

Career

In 1930 Sanders began selling fried chicken from his service station in Corbin, Kentucky. His popularity grew, and in 1935 Kentucky governor Ruby Laffoon made him an honorary Kentucky colonel. At his Sanders Court & Café he perfected his secret recipe of 11 herbs and spices and the pressure-frying method that defined his product.

When a new interstate highway bypassed Corbin in the 1950s, Sanders, then in his sixties, took to the road franchising his chicken concept. The first franchise opened in South Salt Lake, Utah, in 1952. By 1964 there were more than 600 outlets, and Sanders sold the company for $2 million while remaining its salaried brand ambassador.

Later life and death

Sanders remained the public face of KFC until his death from pneumonia, complicated by leukemia, in Louisville, Kentucky, in 1980, aged 90. He was buried in his trademark white suit in Cave Hill Cemetery, Louisville.
