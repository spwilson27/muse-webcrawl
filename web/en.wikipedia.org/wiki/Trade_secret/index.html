Trade secret

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trade secret is information — a formula, process, design, customer list or business method — that has economic value because it is not generally known, and which its holder takes reasonable measures to keep secret. Unlike patents, trade secrets require no registration and can last indefinitely (the Coca-Cola formula is the textbook case), but protection is lost by independent discovery or lawful reverse engineering, and remedies lie only against misappropriation (theft, bribery, breach of confidence).

The legal framework was harmonised internationally by TRIPS Agreement Article 39 (“undisclosed information”). In the United States, the Uniform Trade Secrets Act (adopted by the large majority of states) and the federal Defend Trade Secrets Act (2016, adding a federal civil action and whistle-blower immunity) govern; the EU adopted a Trade Secrets Directive in 2016. The Economic Espionage Act (1996) criminalised theft benefiting foreign governments — the basis of high-profile US–China cases. Policy debate pits secrecy's incentive and low administrative cost against patent law's disclosure bargain and the risk of suppressing employee mobility (non-compete and inevitable-disclosure doctrines sit at the fault line).
