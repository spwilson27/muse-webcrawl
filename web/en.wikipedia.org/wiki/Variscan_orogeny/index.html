Variscan orogeny


Extent of Variscan structures in Europe

The Variscan orogeny, or Hercynian orogeny, was a geologic mountain-building event caused by Late Paleozoic continental collision between Euramerica (Laurussia) and Gondwana to form the supercontinent of Pangaea. It unfolded mainly during the Devonian and Carboniferous periods, roughly 419 to 299 million years ago, and produced a vast mountain belt across what is now Europe and North Africa, with the Appalachian orogeny of North America forming part of the same collisional system.

Development

The orogeny resulted from the closure of the Rheic Ocean and smaller oceanic basins as Gondwana moved northward against Laurussia. Successive terranes—including Armorica and other microcontinents—were accreted, sediments were folded and thrust, and large volumes of granite were intruded. The belt is typically divided along strike into zones (from northwest to southeast in Europe: the Rhenohercynian, Saxothuringian and Moldanubian zones).

Present-day remnants

Erosion and later tectonics have reduced the Variscan mountains to a series of basement massifs, including the Massif Central of France, the Armorican Massif of Brittany, the Rhenish Massif and Harz of Germany, the Bohemian Massif, the Iberian Meseta, and the uplands of Cornwall and southwest England. Variscan deformation also folded the coal-bearing Carboniferous strata exploited by the coalfields of South Wales, northern France and Belgium, the Ruhr, and Silesia, underpinning much of Europe's early industrialization.
