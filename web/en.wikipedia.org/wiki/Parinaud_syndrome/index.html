Parinaud syndrome

Parinaud syndrome
Also known as: Dorsal midbrain syndrome, Sylvian aqueduct syndrome
Specialty: Neurology, neuro-ophthalmology
Symptoms: Paralysis of upward gaze, convergence-retraction nystagmus, light-near dissociation
Causes: Pineal gland tumour, midbrain lesion, hydrocephalus

Parinaud syndrome, also known as dorsal midbrain syndrome, is a group of abnormalities of eye movement and pupil dysfunction. It is caused by lesions of the dorsal (upper) midbrain, particularly compression of the vertical gaze centre at the rostral interstitial nucleus of the medial longitudinal fasciculus, and is named after the French ophthalmologist Henri Parinaud (1844–1905), who first described it.

Signs and symptoms

The classic features of Parinaud syndrome include:

  • Paralysis of conjugate upward gaze (vertical gaze palsy), often with relative sparing of downward gaze in early stages.
  • Convergence-retraction nystagmus, in which attempted upward saccades produce rhythmic convergence and retraction of the globes.
  • Light-near dissociation of the pupils: the pupils constrict poorly to light but constrict normally during accommodation and convergence.
  • Eyelid retraction (Collier's sign), giving a staring appearance.
  • Skew deviation and convergence insufficiency may also occur.

Causes

The most common causes are tumours of the pineal gland region (such as pineocytomas and germinomas) in younger patients and midbrain infarction in older patients. Other causes include hydrocephalus with dilation of the suprapineal recess, multiple sclerosis, trauma, and infection. In children, a non-communicating hydrocephalus caused by aqueductal stenosis is a frequent mechanism.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by neuroimaging (MRI) to identify the dorsal midbrain lesion. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause, for example shunting of hydrocephalus, surgery or radiotherapy for pineal tumours. Eye movement abnormalities may persist despite treatment of the lesion.
