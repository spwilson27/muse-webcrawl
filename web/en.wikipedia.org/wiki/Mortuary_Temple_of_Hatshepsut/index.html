The Mortuary Temple of Hatshepsut is a mortuary temple built during the reign of Pharaoh Hatshepsut of the Eighteenth Dynasty of Egypt (reigned c. 1479–1458 BC). Located opposite the city of Luxor, it is considered one of the "incomparable monuments of ancient Egypt". The temple was built into the cliff face of Deir el-Bahari on the west bank of the Nile, rising in three terraced levels.

Architecture

The temple, known in ancient Egyptian as Djeser-Djeseru ("Holy of Holies"), is attributed to Senenmut, Hatshepsut's steward and architect. It was constructed over about fifteen years beginning around the seventh year of her reign. A causeway and ramps connect three colonnaded terraces that climb against the limestone cliffs. The design was partly inspired by the earlier mortuary temple of Mentuhotep II beside it, but executed on a far grander scale with long pillared terraces that harmonise with the cliff face.

Reliefs and decoration

The temple's reliefs are famous: the middle terrace depicts the divine birth of Hatshepsut, legitimising her rule, and the celebrated expedition to the Land of Punt, showing ships laden with myrrh trees, gold, ebony and exotic animals. Chapels were dedicated to Hathor and Anubis, and a sanctuary of Amun lay at the temple's axis. After Hatshepsut's death, her successor Thutmose III had many of her images and names defaced in a campaign of damnatio memoriae.

Later history

In the Coptic period a monastery of Saint Phoibammon occupied the temple, giving Deir el-Bahari ("Monastery of the North") its name. The site was excavated by Auguste Mariette, Édouard Naville and others in the 19th and early 20th centuries; Polish teams have conducted major restoration since 1961. In November 1997 the site was the scene of the Luxor massacre, in which 62 people, mostly tourists, were killed by Islamist militants at the temple.
