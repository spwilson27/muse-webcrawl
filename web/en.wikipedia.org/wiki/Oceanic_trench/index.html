Oceanic trench

Type: Seafloor topographic depression

Formed by: Subduction at convergent plate boundaries

Deepest: Mariana Trench, about 10,900 m below sea level

An oceanic trench is a long, narrow, and very deep topographic depression of the ocean floor. Trenches are the deepest parts of the ocean and are found along convergent plate boundaries, where one tectonic plate is forced beneath another in the process of subduction. They typically run parallel to a volcanic island arc or a continental margin, offset a few hundred kilometres from it.

Characteristics

Trenches are usually 50 to 100 kilometres wide and thousands of kilometres long, with V-shaped cross-sections, and they descend 3 to 4 kilometres below the surrounding ocean floor, reaching depths of 6,000 to nearly 11,000 metres below sea level. The deepest known point on Earth, the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench of the western Pacific, lies roughly 10.9 kilometres below the surface. Other major trenches include the Tonga, Japan, Kuril–Kamchatka, Aleutian, Puerto Rico, and Peru–Chile (Atacama) trenches.

Formation and associated features

Where dense oceanic lithosphere sinks into the mantle, the plate bends downward, forming the trench. The subduction zone generates powerful megathrust earthquakes and, as the descending slab heats and releases water into the overlying mantle, partial melting that feeds chains of volcanoes — island arcs such as Japan and the Marianas, or continental arcs such as the Andes. Material scraped off the descending plate can accumulate as an accretionary wedge on the trench's landward slope.

Life in trenches

Despite pressures exceeding 1,000 atmospheres and near-freezing temperatures, hadalpelagic ecosystems thrive in trenches, including snailfish, amphipods, and xenophyophores, sustained largely by organic matter sinking from surface waters.
