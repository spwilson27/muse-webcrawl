Cardenolide
ClassSteroid / cardiac glycoside aglycone
Core structureSteroid with a five-membered butenolide ring at C-17
ExamplesDigoxigenin, ouabain (strophanthin), oleandrin

A cardenolide is a type of steroid compound characterised by a five-membered unsaturated lactone (butenolide) ring attached at carbon 17 of the steroid nucleus. Many cardenolides occur as glycosides — cardiac glycosides — in plants of families such as Apocynaceae (including foxgloves, oleander and milkweeds) and are potent inhibitors of the sodium–potassium ATPase pump.

Occurrence and ecology

Cardenolide glycosides function as chemical defences for the plants that produce them. Some insects, most famously the monarch butterfly, sequester cardenolides from milkweed host plants for their own defence against predators. Oleander and lily of the valley are common ornamental sources of poisoning in humans and livestock.

Pharmacology

By inhibiting Na+/K+-ATPase in cardiac muscle, cardenolides increase intracellular calcium and thereby the force of contraction. Digoxin, the glycoside of the cardenolide digoxigenin from Digitalis, has long been used in the treatment of heart failure and atrial fibrillation, though its narrow therapeutic index demands careful monitoring.
