Habibullāh Kalakāni (Dari: حبیب‌الله کلکانی‎, c. 1890 – 1 November 1929), also known by his supporters as Habibullah Khan and by his detractors as Bacha-e Saqao (بچه سقاو, "son of the water carrier"), was the King of Afghanistan from January to October 1929. A Tajik from the village of Kalakan north of Kabul, he seized power during the Afghan Civil War of 1928–29, capitalizing on widespread discontent with the rapid modernizing reforms of King Amanullah Khan. He was the only Tajik monarch of Afghanistan in the modern era and one of the very few Afghan rulers not of Pashtun origin.

Early life

Kalakani was born around 1890 in Kalakan, in the Koh Daman valley north of Kabul, the son of a water carrier (saqao). As a young man he left home, served in the royal army, fought in the Third Anglo-Afghan War of 1919, and afterwards took to brigandage in the northern hills, where he built a reputation as a charismatic bandit leader.

Rise to power

King Amanullah's reforms — taxation changes, conscription, and social measures affecting dress and education — alienated conservative tribes and clergy. In late 1928 a Shinwari revolt broke out in the east, and Kalakani's forces advanced from the north. In January 1929 he attacked Kabul; Amanullah abdicated in favour of his brother Inayatullah, who held the throne for only three days before Kalakani occupied the capital and proclaimed himself king on 17 January 1929.

Reign

Kalakani reversed Amanullah's reforms: he closed girls' schools, reinstated traditional taxes in kind, and relied on religious and tribal support. His administration, however, controlled little territory beyond Kabul and the north, and the treasury was empty. The Pashtun tribes of the south rallied around Mohammed Nadir Khan, a former war minister, whose forces advanced on the capital. Kalakani fled Kabul on 13 October 1929; Nadir Shah was proclaimed king.

Death and legacy

Despite a written guarantee of safe conduct, Kalakani and seventeen of his associates were executed by firing squad in Kabul on 1 November 1929. He remains a controversial figure: remembered in official historiography as an illiterate bandit-king, but commemorated by some, particularly among Tajiks, as a pious defender of tradition. His remains were reburied in a public ceremony in Kabul in 2016 after long-running controversy over his grave's location.
