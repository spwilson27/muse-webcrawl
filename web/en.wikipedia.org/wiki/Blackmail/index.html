Blackmail

Blackmail is a criminal act of coercion using a threat to reveal or publicise substantially true or false information about a person or people unless certain demands are met. It is often damaging information, and it may be revealed to family members or associates rather than to the general public. It involves using threats of physical harm, criminal prosecution, or exposure of secret or embarrassing information to obtain money, property or services.

Etymology and history

The word derives from Scots: "black mail" — rent or tribute paid in goods or labour (rather than silver, "white mail") to freebooters and clan chiefs in the border country between England and Scotland for protection against raids. Modern statutes generally treat blackmail as a form of extortion or coercion.

Legal treatment

In England and Wales, blackmail under section 21 of the Theft Act 1968 is making an unwarranted demand with menaces, with a view to gain or loss to another, punishable by up to 14 years. In the United States, federal law (18 U.S.C. § 873 and the Hobbs Act tradition) and state extortion statutes criminalise similar demands; threats to report a crime unless paid are a classic example. The "blackmail paradox" — why it is illegal to threaten to do something lawful (reveal truth) unless paid, when either act alone is lawful — is a famous puzzle in legal philosophy.

Related forms

Digital variants include sextortion and ransomware, where data or access is held hostage. See also extortion as a predicate act under RICO.
