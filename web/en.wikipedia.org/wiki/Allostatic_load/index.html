Allostatic load

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Allostatic load is the "wear and tear on the body" which accumulates as an individual is exposed to repeated or chronic stress. The term was coined by Bruce McEwen and Eliot Stellar in 1993, building on the concept of allostasis — achieving stability through physiological or behavioural change — introduced by Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer.

Concept

The body responds to challenge through mediators such as cortisol, adrenaline, cytokines and metabolic hormones. These responses are protective in the short term, but when they are repeatedly switched on, fail to switch off, or fail to mount adequately, the mediators themselves damage tissues. McEwen distinguished four patterns that produce load: repeated stressors, failure to habituate, a prolonged response after the stressor ends, and an inadequate response that triggers compensatory over-activity in other systems.

Measurement

In research, allostatic load is usually operationalised as a composite index counting how many biomarkers fall in a high-risk range — commonly systolic and diastolic blood pressure, waist–hip ratio, cholesterol fractions, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), cortisol, C-reactive protein and others. Higher scores predict increased risk of cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, frailty and mortality.

Health disparities

Allostatic load scores are consistently higher in lower socioeconomic groups and in populations facing chronic social stressors, offering a biological mechanism linking social inequality to poorer health outcomes. Interventions proposed to reduce load include sleep, exercise, social support and treatment of the component conditions.
