Rayleigh–Bénard convection

Rayleigh–Bénard convection is a form of natural convection in a horizontal layer of fluid heated from below and cooled from above. When the temperature gradient exceeds a critical threshold, the fluid organizes into regular patterns of convection rolls or cells; at higher driving the pattern transitions to turbulence. It is a canonical problem in the study of pattern formation and nonlinear dynamics.

Physics

Heating from below makes the lower fluid expand and become buoyant. If the imposed temperature difference is small, heat crosses the layer by conduction alone. Beyond a critical value of the dimensionless Rayleigh number — about 1708 for a layer between rigid plates — conduction becomes unstable and convection begins. The pattern near onset is described by the Rayleigh number and the Prandtl number; Henri Bénard's 1900 experiments on thin films revealed the famous hexagonal cells, in which surface-tension (Marangoni) effects also play a role, while Lord Rayleigh's 1916 theory treated the buoyancy-driven bulk instability.

Significance

Rayleigh–Bénard convection provides a model system for the route to chaos and turbulent convection and is relevant to atmospheric and oceanic circulation, mantle convection, stellar interiors, and industrial processes such as crystal growth and cooling.
