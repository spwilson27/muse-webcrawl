Peculiar velocity

Peculiar velocity refers to the velocity of an object — typically a galaxy — relative to the overall expansion of the universe described by Hubble's law. The observed recession velocity of a galaxy is the sum of the velocity due to cosmic expansion (the Hubble flow) and the galaxy's own peculiar velocity, which results from the gravitational pull of nearby mass concentrations.

Definition

In cosmology, the total observed velocity v of a distant object can be written as v = H0d + vpec, where H0 is the Hubble constant, d is the object's proper distance, and vpec is the peculiar velocity. Typical peculiar velocities of galaxies are a few hundred kilometres per second, so they matter most for nearby objects, where the Hubble term is small.

Examples

The Milky Way and the Andromeda Galaxy are approaching each other at about 110 km/s despite cosmic expansion, because of their mutual gravitational attraction. The Local Group as a whole moves at roughly 600 km/s relative to the cosmic microwave background, in the direction of the constellation Hydra–Centaurus, a motion historically attributed to the "Great Attractor" — a concentration of mass in the Zone of Avoidance — and to more distant structures such as the Shapley Supercluster.

Measurement and use

Peculiar velocities are estimated by comparing a galaxy's redshift with an independent distance measurement, for example from the Tully–Fisher relation for spirals or the fundamental plane for ellipticals. Surveys such as Cosmicflows use these measurements to map the distribution of matter, including dark matter, in the local universe, and to study bulk flows and structures such as the Laniakea Supercluster. In stellar astronomy the same term is used for a star's motion relative to the local standard of rest.
