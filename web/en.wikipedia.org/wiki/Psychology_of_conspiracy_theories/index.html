Psychology of conspiracy theories

The psychology of conspiracy theories is the study of the psychological factors that make people more or less likely to believe conspiracy theories — explanations for events that invoke secret plots by powerful groups. Research in this area, which grew substantially from the 2010s, examines cognitive, motivational and social influences on conspiracy belief, as well as its consequences for health behaviour, politics and intergroup relations.

Cognitive factors

Belief in conspiracy theories has been linked to general cognitive tendencies, including pattern perception (seeing meaning in coincidence), agency detection (attributing events to intentional agents), and a preference for intuitive over analytic thinking. People who endorse one conspiracy theory tend to endorse others, even mutually contradictory ones, suggesting an underlying general "conspiracy mentality" or monological belief system.

Motivational factors

Researchers commonly distinguish three broad motives:

  • Epistemic motives — the desire to understand one's environment and reduce uncertainty, especially during crises.
  • Existential motives — the desire for security and control; feelings of powerlessness, anxiety and loss of control are associated with stronger conspiracy belief.
  • Social motives — the desire to belong to and positively evaluate one's own group, and to blame out-groups for misfortune.

Individual differences

Conspiracy belief correlates modestly with certain personality traits and attitudes, including lower trust, higher paranoia and schizotypy, narcissism, and a need for uniqueness. Political extremism at both ends of the spectrum, and feelings of disadvantage or marginalisation, also predict endorsement.

Consequences

Conspiracy beliefs have been associated with vaccine hesitancy and reduced uptake of preventive health behaviours, rejection of scientific consensus on climate change, political disengagement or, in some cases, support for political violence. At the same time, scholars note that some conspiracy beliefs have historically been vindicated, and that suspicion of authorities is not inherently irrational; the field therefore distinguishes between belief in specific claims and the general tendency toward conspiracist thinking.

Interventions

Approaches to reducing harmful conspiracy belief include corrections and fact-checking, "prebunking" or psychological inoculation against manipulation techniques, and analytic-thinking prompts. Effects are generally modest, and researchers debate how best to intervene without stigmatising believers.
