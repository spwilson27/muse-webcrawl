The Crown Court is the criminal court of first instance in England and Wales responsible for hearing all trial by jury cases — trials on indictment for serious criminal offences, committals for sentence, and appeals from magistrates' courts. It replaced the ancient courts of assizes and quarter sessions when it was created by the Courts Act 1971, coming into being on 1 January 1972.

The Crown Court is a single national court that sits at centres across England and Wales. Its most famous venue is the Central Criminal Court in London, universally known as the Old Bailey. Alongside the High Court and the Court of Appeal, it forms part of the Senior Courts of England and Wales, and it is administered by HM Courts & Tribunals Service.

History

Before 1972, serious crime was tried at assize courts (held periodically by visiting High Court judges) and quarter sessions (county and borough courts). The Royal Commission on Assizes and Quarter Sessions (the Beeching Commission, 1966–1969) recommended a unified Crown Court; the Courts Act 1971 enacted the reform. The new court unified criminal jurisdiction while keeping trial by jury for indictable offences.

Jurisdiction and procedure

The Crown Court has exclusive jurisdiction over trials on indictment. It also hears appeals against conviction and sentence from magistrates' courts (reheard by a judge sitting with lay magistrates), committals for sentence from those courts, and certain confiscation and enforcement matters. Either-way offences may be sent up from the magistrates if they decline jurisdiction or the defendant elects jury trial.

Trials are presided over by a judge, who rules on law and sentences after verdict, while guilt is determined by a jury of twelve. Preliminary hearings, plea and trial preparation hearings, and arraignment manage the case before trial.

Judges

Cases are heard by High Court judges, resident and other circuit judges, and recorders (part-time judges, usually practising barristers or solicitors). The most serious offences — murder, rape, and certain terrorism and Official Secrets cases — must be tried by a High Court judge (or a circuit judge specially authorized), allocated by the Presiding Judges of each circuit under rules established after the Beeching reforms.

Classification of cases

Indictable offences are divided into classes for allocation: Class 1 (the gravest: treason, murder), Class 2 (including rape and other offences reserved to High Court judges unless released to circuit judges), Class 3 (other serious offences triable by circuit judges or recorders), and Class 4 (general indictable crime). Appeals from the Crown Court in criminal matters go to the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) and potentially onward to the UK Supreme Court on points of law of general public importance.

Appeals

Defendants convicted on indictment may appeal against conviction (with leave, on the ground that the conviction is unsafe) and against sentence. The prosecution has limited rights, notably to refer unduly lenient sentences to the Court of Appeal and to appeal against terminating rulings.
