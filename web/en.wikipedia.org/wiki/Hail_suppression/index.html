Hail suppression

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anti-hail rockets were widely deployed in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe.

Hail suppression is a form of weather modification that attempts to prevent hailstones from growing large enough to damage crops, property, and vehicles. Most methods rely on cloud seeding — dispersing substances such as silver iodide into thunderstorms — on the theory that introducing many extra ice nuclei will make the available supercooled water form many small hailstones instead of a few large, destructive ones (the "beneficial competition" hypothesis).

Methods

  • Aircraft seeding — flares of silver iodide burned or dropped into storm updrafts.
  • Rockets and artillery — ground-launched rockets deliver seeding agent or explode in the hail-formation zone; widely used in the former USSR, China, Bulgaria, and parts of France and Argentina.
  • Ground generators and hail cannons — networks of silver-iodide burners; "hail cannons" producing shock waves are sold to farmers and car plants but have no demonstrated physical basis.
  • Hail nets and insurance — non-modification alternatives: netting over orchards and vineyards, and crop hail insurance, are proven mitigation measures.

History and programmes

Large state programmes began in the Soviet Union in the 1960s, where scientists such as G. K. Sulakvelidze claimed 70–90% reductions in hail damage using radar-guided rockets. In the United States, the National Hail Research Experiment (NHRE, 1972–76) in Colorado tested the Soviet hypothesis and failed to confirm it. China operates the world's largest weather modification apparatus, including extensive anti-hail rocket forces, and several European wine regions fund seeding by aircraft or ground generators.

Scientific assessment

The World Meteorological Organization and the American Meteorological Society regard the efficacy of hail suppression as unproven: hailstorms are highly variable, controlled experiments are difficult, and statistical claims are contested. The physical hypothesis is plausible, but rigorous randomised trials have not demonstrated a reliable reduction in hail damage, so many scientists remain sceptical while operational programmes continue on the strength of cost–benefit arguments by agricultural insurers.
