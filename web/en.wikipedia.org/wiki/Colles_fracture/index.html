Colles fracture

A Colles fracture (also spelled Colles' fracture) is a type of fracture of the distal forearm in which the broken end of the radius is displaced dorsally (toward the back of the hand). It is one of the most common fractures in adults, classically sustained when a person falls onto an outstretched hand (FOOSH). The injury is named after the Irish surgeon and anatomist Abraham Colles, who first described it in 1814.

Dorsal displacement of the distal fragment gives the wrist its characteristic "silver fork" deformity.

Signs and symptoms

The classic clinical presentation includes pain, swelling, and deformity of the wrist. When viewed from the side, the hand appears displaced backwards relative to the forearm, producing the so-called silver fork (or dinner fork) deformity. Bruising and tenderness over the distal radius are common, and median nerve symptoms may occur if swelling compresses the carpal tunnel.

Causes and risk factors

Colles fractures most often result from a fall on an outstretched hand with the wrist in extension. They are particularly common in older adults, especially postmenopausal women, because of osteoporosis, which weakens the bone and makes it more susceptible to fracture from low-energy trauma. In younger patients, higher-energy trauma such as road traffic accidents or sports injuries is usually responsible.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is confirmed by plain radiography of the wrist in posteroanterior and lateral views. The lateral view demonstrates the dorsal displacement and dorsal angulation of the distal fragment, which distinguishes a Colles fracture from the reverse pattern, a Smith's fracture, in which the fragment is displaced volarly (toward the palm).

Treatment

Many Colles fractures can be treated non-operatively with closed reduction under anaesthesia followed by immobilisation in a plaster cast for about six weeks. Fractures that are unstable, intra-articular, or significantly displaced may require surgical fixation, most commonly with a volar locking plate, percutaneous K-wires, or an external fixator. Rehabilitation with physiotherapy helps restore wrist motion and grip strength.

Complications

Possible complications include malunion (often with residual dorsal tilt), stiffness, complex regional pain syndrome, carpal tunnel syndrome, rupture of the extensor pollicis longus tendon, and post-traumatic arthritis of the wrist.
