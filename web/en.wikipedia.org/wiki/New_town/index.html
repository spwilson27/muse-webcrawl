New town

A new town is a settlement that was consciously planned from the beginning and built in a new location, rather than growing gradually. New towns have been created for political, economic, colonial, and urban-planning reasons throughout history.

History

Planned towns date to antiquity — Roman colonies, medieval bastides, and Renaissance ideal cities — and include capitals such as Washington, D.C., Canberra, Brasília, and Islamabad. In the modern era Ebenezer Howard's garden city movement inspired Letchworth and Welwyn Garden City in England.

British new towns

After the Second World War, the United Kingdom's New Towns Act 1946 designated a series of new towns to relieve overcrowding in London and other cities, including Stevenage, Harlow, Basildon, and later Milton Keynes, administered by development corporations.

Worldwide

Other countries built new towns extensively: France's villes nouvelles, Sweden's Vällingby, the Netherlands' Almere, Hong Kong's new towns, Singapore's housing estates, and China's planned cities. Critics have noted both their orderly infrastructure and, at times, blandness or social dislocation.
