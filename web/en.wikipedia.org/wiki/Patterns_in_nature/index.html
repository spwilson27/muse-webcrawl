Patterns in nature

Patterns in nature are visible regularities of form found in the natural world. These patterns recur in different contexts and can sometimes be modelled mathematically. Natural patterns include symmetries, trees and branching structures, spirals, meanders, waves, foams, tessellations, cracks, and stripes. Early Greek philosophers such as Plato and Pythagoras studied pattern in attempts to explain order in nature, and the modern scientific explanation of these patterns brings together mathematics, physics, chemistry, and natural selection.

The spirals of a sunflower's seeds follow Fibonacci-numbered phyllotaxis.

Types of pattern

  • Symmetry — bilateral symmetry dominates animal body plans; radial symmetry appears in starfish and many flowers; spherical symmetry in some microorganisms.
  • Spirals — logarithmic spirals in shells and galaxies; Archimedean spirals in spider webs; the golden-angle spirals of phyllotaxis in leaves and seeds.
  • Tessellations and foams — the hexagonal cells of honeycomb, columnar basalt, and soap-film foams that minimize surface area.
  • Stripes and spots — animal coat patterns explained by Alan Turing's reaction–diffusion model of morphogenesis.
  • Waves and dunes — wind and water shape sand into repeating ripples, dunes, and meanders.
  • Fractals and branching — trees, river networks, blood vessels, and lightning repeat similar structures at multiple scales.

Explanations

Different patterns have different causes. Physical laws — energy minimization, fluid dynamics, wave mechanics — generate foams, dunes, and cracks without any biology at all. In living things, genes encode growth rules whose outcomes are patterns, and natural selection favours patterns that work: camouflage stripes, warning spots, efficient packing of seeds. Mathematicians from D'Arcy Thompson, whose On Growth and Form (1917) treated biological form physically, to Turing and later theorists of chaos and self-organization, have shown how simple local rules can generate global order.
