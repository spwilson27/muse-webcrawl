Mirror symmetry

Mirror symmetry
TypeReflection symmetry
GeometryInvariant under reflection across a plane or axis
Physics usageParity (P-symmetry); mirror symmetry in string theory

Mirror symmetry is symmetry with respect to reflection. In everyday language it describes the property of an object that is unchanged when reflected in a mirror: the left and right halves are interchanged but the object looks the same. Formally, an object or figure has mirror symmetry if there is a plane (in three dimensions) or a line (in two dimensions), called the plane or axis of symmetry, such that reflection through it maps the figure onto itself. It is the most familiar of the symmetries and underlies the approximate bilateral symmetry of most animals, including humans.

In geometry

In two dimensions, mirror symmetry is reflection across a line; a figure may have one or several axes of symmetry. A square has four, a circle infinitely many, while a scalene triangle has none. In three dimensions the reflection takes place across a plane. Shapes lacking any mirror symmetry are called chiral — their mirror image cannot be superimposed on the original — a property of great importance in chemistry, where many molecules exist as left- and right-handed enantiomers.

In physics

Reflection symmetry of physical laws is called parity, or P-symmetry. For most of the twentieth century parity was assumed to be an exact symmetry of nature, until experiments proposed by Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang and carried out by Chien-Shiung Wu in 1956–57 showed that the weak interaction violates parity: the universe distinguishes, at a fundamental level, between left and right. In string theory, "mirror symmetry" has a second, unrelated technical meaning: a duality between pairs of Calabi–Yau manifolds under which complex and symplectic geometry are exchanged, a discovery that transformed enumerative geometry by allowing hard curve-counting problems on one manifold to be solved on its mirror.

In art and architecture

Mirror symmetry is a pervasive device in ornament, from Islamic geometric patterns to the facades of classical architecture such as the Taj Mahal, whose plan and elevation are organised around a central axis. It also appears in music, in some forms of palindrome-like structure, and in literature through chiastic patterns.
