Fine-structure constant

The coupling α 'runs': it grows at higher energies (shorter distances).

In physics, the fine-structure constant, also known as the Sommerfeld constant, commonly denoted by α (the Greek letter alpha), is a fundamental physical constant that quantifies the strength of the electromagnetic interaction between elementary charged particles. It is a dimensionless quantity, independent of any system of units: α = e²/(4πε₀ħc) ≈ 1/137.036.

Introduced by Arnold Sommerfeld in 1916 as a relativistic correction in the old quantum theory (it sets the ratio of an electron's speed in the hydrogen ground state to the speed of light), α became the coupling constant of Quantum electrodynamics: the probability amplitude for photon emission or absorption is proportional to √α, so QED's perturbation series is an expansion in powers of α — the reason the theory is so accurate. Its origin is unexplained — why this dimensionless number has this value is one of physics' famous mysteries, ruminated on by Richard Feynman and Wolfgang Pauli alike.

Running and measurement

α is not strictly constant: vacuum polarisation screens charges, so the effective α grows at short distances (reaching ~1/128 at the Z-boson mass). It is among the most precisely measured constants (to better than a part per billion, via the electron's anomalous magnetic moment and atom-recoil experiments), and astronomers test whether it has varied over cosmic time by examining quasar spectra and the Oklo natural reactor — so far, no significant variation has been established.
