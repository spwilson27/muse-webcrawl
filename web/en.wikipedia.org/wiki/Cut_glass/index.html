Cut glass


American Brilliant Period cut glass bowl (placeholder image)

Cut glass is glass that has been decorated entirely by hand by grinding or cutting intricate patterns into its surface with rotating wheels. The technique exploits the high refractive index of lead glass (lead crystal) to produce sparkling, prismatic effects.

History

Wheel-cut decoration dates to ancient Rome (as in cage cups) but was revived in Bohemia and England in the 17th and 18th centuries. Steam-powered cutting wheels in the early 19th century made deep, precise cuts practical, and firms such as Baccarat, Waterford and Stevens & Williams built international reputations. The American Brilliant Period (roughly 1876–1917) produced deeply cut, highly polished wares with motifs such as hobstars, fans and strawberry diamonds, from firms including Libbey and T. G. Hawkes.

Technique

A rough design is marked on the blank, then cut with iron or stone wheels fed with abrasive slurry, smoothed with wooden wheels, and finally polished — historically with acid or by hand. "Cut glass" should be distinguished from cheaper pressed glass molded to imitate it, and from crystal engraving, in which a copper wheel cuts fine surface detail.
