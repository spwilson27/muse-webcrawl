Foam

Foam
TypeColloid (gas dispersed in liquid or solid)
Stabilized bySurfactants, polymers, particles
ExamplesSea foam, soap foam, polyurethane foam, metal foam

A foam is a substance formed by trapping pockets of gas in a liquid or solid. In most foams the volume of gas is large, with thin films of liquid or solid separating regions of gas; such foams are often classed as colloidal systems. Examples range from the foam on beer and soap suds to sea foam, firefighting foam, and manufactured foamed plastics such as polystyrene and polyurethane.

Structure and stability

A foam consists of gas cells (bubbles) separated by thin films called lamellae, which meet in channels known as Plateau borders. Foams are inherently unstable: liquid drains from the films under gravity, small bubbles shrink as gas diffuses into larger ones (Ostwald ripening), and films eventually rupture, causing coalescence. Surfactants, proteins, polymers, or fine particles stabilize foams by reducing surface tension and slowing drainage. A solid foam—such as a foamed plastic or metal foam—retains the cellular structure after the matrix solidifies.

Formation and destruction

Foams form when air or another gas is introduced into a liquid by shaking, sparging, or chemical gas generation in the presence of surface-active agents. Antifoaming agents (defoamers), such as silicone oils, are used in industry to suppress unwanted foam in paints, fermentation, wastewater treatment, and food processing.

Applications

Foams are used in flotation separation in mining, fire suppression (aqueous film-forming foams), food products from whipped cream to bread dough, personal care products, insulation and packaging (expanded polystyrene), cushioning (memory foam), and biomedical scaffolds. Solid foams are valued for combining low density with stiffness, energy absorption, and thermal insulation.
