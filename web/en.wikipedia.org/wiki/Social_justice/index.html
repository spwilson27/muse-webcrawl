Social justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Social justice is the concept of fair and just relations between the individual and society, measured by the distribution of wealth, opportunities for personal activity and social privileges. The term was coined in the 1840s (the Jesuit Luigi Taparelli is credited) within Catholic social teaching, developed through papal encyclicals on labour and society, and adopted by labour movements, the International Labour Organization (whose 1919 constitution declares universal peace possible only on the basis of social justice) and modern egalitarian politics.

Content and controversy

As a banner it covers distributive claims (fair shares of income, wealth and opportunity), recognitional claims (equal status and respect for groups), and procedural claims (voice in decisions). John Rawls's A Theory of Justice gave the concept its most rigorous liberal form. Uses vary sharply: for proponents it names the goal of equal citizenship in social form; for critics (Hayek called it a mirage when applied to market outcomes) it risks conflating justice — a virtue of conduct and rules — with states of affairs no one designed. The phrase's 21st-century ubiquity in activism, institutional mission statements and backlash politics ("anti-social-justice" polemics) keeps its definition itself a political contest.
