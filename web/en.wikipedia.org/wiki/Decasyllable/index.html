Decasyllable (Italian: decasillabo; Serbian: deseterac) is a poetic metre of ten syllables per line, used in several European poetic traditions. It is frequent in Italian, Spanish, and French poetry and is the characteristic line of South Slavic epic song.

In French and English poetry

The decasyllabic line was the standard metre of the Old French chansons de geste, typically divided by a caesura after the fourth syllable (4+6). In Middle English, Geoffrey Chaucer adapted the French and Italian ten-syllable line, combining it with the native accentual tradition to produce the iambic pentameter that became the dominant metre of English verse, as in the heroic couplet.

In Italian and Spanish poetry

Italian poets developed the related endecasillabo (hendecasyllable), in which the line counts eleven syllables under Italian prosodic conventions; the Spanish decasílabo has been used since the medieval period and appears in both learned and popular verse.

South Slavic epic

The deseterac, a ten-syllable line with a caesura after the fourth syllable, is the metre of Serbo-Croatian and Bulgarian oral epic poetry, traditionally sung to the accompaniment of the single-stringed gusle. The epic songs collected by Vuk Karadžić and studied by Milman Parry and Albert Lord—whose analysis of the metre and formulaic composition founded oral-formulaic theory—are in this line.
