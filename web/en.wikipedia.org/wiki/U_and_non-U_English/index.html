U and non-U English

U and non-U English refers to a set of English words that (according to defenders of the distinction) indicate class: U (upper class) words are those used by the upper class and the upper middle class seeking to follow their example, while non-U words are those thought to be "smart" by the lower middle classes but which the upper classes regard as inferior, affected, or pretentious. The distinction was introduced by the linguist Alan S. C. Ross in 1954 and popularised by Nancy Mitford in her 1955 essay "The English Aristocracy".

The Ross article and Mitford essay

Professor Alan Ross, a Finnish-trained linguist at the University of Birmingham, published "Linguistic class-indicators in present-day English" in the Finnish journal Neuphilologische Mitteilungen in 1954. Nancy Mitford drew on it for her humorous essay in Encounter (September 1955), later reprinted in the book Noblesse Oblige (1956) alongside contributions by Evelyn Waugh, John Betjeman and others. Betjeman's poem "How to Get On in Society" played on the same shibboleths.

Counter-intuitively, many non-U forms are euphemisms or French-derived words ("serviette", "toilet") chosen to sound refined, while the U forms are plain English (sometimes older French of the Norman period, like "napkin"). Ross emphasized that he was describing indicators current in the early 1950s, and usage has shifted considerably since.

Influence

The essay became a cultural sensation and a touchstone in British discussions of class and language. Later writers, including Kate Fox in Watching the English (2004), revisited the categories, noting that some non-U words had become standard while class signalling moved to other markers.
