The Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, also known as the Paris Manuscripts, are a series of unfinished notes written by Karl Marx between April and August 1844 during his exile in Paris. Marx did not publish them in his lifetime; they first appeared in print in 1932 in the Soviet Union and in Germany, and their publication transformed twentieth-century interpretations of his thought.

Contents

The manuscripts combine a critique of classical political economy (Smith, Ricardo, Say, Mill) with philosophical analysis influenced by Hegel and Feuerbach. Their most famous section develops the theory of alienated labour (entfremdete Arbeit): under capitalism, the worker is alienated in four ways — from the product of labour, which confronts him as an alien object owned by another; from the activity of production itself, experienced as forced torment rather than self-realisation; from his "species-being" (Gattungswesen), humanity's free, conscious, universal productive life; and from other human beings, who stand in relations of competition and domination. Private property is analysed as both the product and the means of alienated labour. Other sections discuss rent, capital, needs, money, and a critique of Hegel's dialectic, praising Feuerbach while anticipating historical materialism.

Reception and influence

On publication the manuscripts fuelled debate over the relationship between the "young Marx" and the author of Capital: some readers (including Marxist humanists and existentialists such as Erich Fromm and the Yugoslav Praxis School) saw alienation as the unifying core of his work, while others, notably Louis Althusser, posited an "epistemological break" between the humanist early writings and the scientific mature theory. The manuscripts remain central to discussions of alienation, work, and human flourishing in Marxist and social theory.
