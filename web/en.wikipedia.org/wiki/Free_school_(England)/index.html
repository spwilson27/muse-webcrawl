Free school (England)

A free school in England is a type of academy established since 2010 under the Government's free schools initiative. From May 2015, usage of the term was formally extended to include new academies set up via a local authority competition. Like other academies, free schools are non-profit-making, state-funded schools which are free to attend but which are mostly independent of the local authority's control.

Free schools are run by academy trusts and funded directly by the Department for Education. They do not have to follow the National Curriculum, although they must provide a broad and balanced curriculum, and they have greater freedom over teachers' pay and conditions, the length of the school day and term dates. New free schools may be established by groups including parents, teachers, charities, universities, independent schools and faith groups.

History

The policy was announced by the Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition government following the 2010 general election, with legislation in the Academies Act 2010 permitting all existing state schools to become academies and enabling the creation of new schools as academies. The first free schools opened in September 2011. The concept drew on precedents including charter schools in the United States and friskolor in Sweden.

Process of establishment

Prospective proposers must submit an application to the Department for Education demonstrating demand from parents, a suitable site and a viable educational plan. Approved groups work with the Department through a pre-opening phase before signing a funding agreement. Since 2015, local authorities seeking to establish a new school have been required to seek proposals for a free school academy under the "free school presumption".

Criticism and support

Supporters argue that free schools raise standards through innovation and parental choice. Critics have raised concerns about the cost of establishing schools in areas with surplus places, the absence of a requirement to employ qualified teachers, and effects on neighbouring schools. The National Audit Office and parliamentary committees have periodically reviewed the programme's value for money.
