Vanilla planifolia is a species of vanilla orchid native to Mexico and Central America. It is one of the primary sources for vanilla flavouring, due to its high vanillin content, and is the vanilla species most widely cultivated commercially.

Description

It is a thick, fleshy climbing vine with long internodes that roots in trees, clinging by aerial roots and reaching lengths of 30 metres or more. The alternate, oblong-elliptic leaves are large and glossy. The short-lived, waxy, greenish-yellow flowers, about 10 cm across, open in the morning and close by afternoon on the same day; in the wild they are pollinated only by specific meliponine bees and possibly hummingbirds, with a natural fruit set of roughly one percent. The fruit is a long capsule ("vanilla bean" or pod), 15–23 cm long, which ripens over about eight to nine months.

Cultivation

Outside its native range the flower must be pollinated by hand; the technique, devised in 1841 by Edmond Albius, a 12-year-old enslaved boy on Réunion, made cultivation possible worldwide and is still used. Major producers include Madagascar (the source of "Bourbon vanilla"), Indonesia, Mexico and Uganda. After harvest the pods undergo a laborious curing process of blanching, sweating, sun-drying and conditioning, during which glucovanillin is hydrolysed to vanillin and the characteristic aroma develops.

Uses

Cured pods of V. planifolia are the principal commercial source of natural vanilla extract used in food, beverages, perfumery and pharmaceuticals. Because of its cost—the second most expensive spice after saffron—most vanilla flavouring in commerce is synthetic vanillin derived from lignin or petrochemical precursors.
