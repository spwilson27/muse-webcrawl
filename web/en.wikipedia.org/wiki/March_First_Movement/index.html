The March First Movement (Korean: 3·1 운동; Samil Undong, lit. "Three-One Movement") was a series of protests against Japanese colonial rule of Korea that began on 1 March 1919 in Seoul and spread throughout the country. It was one of the earliest public displays of Korean resistance during the Japanese occupation, and remains one of the most significant events in modern Korean history. The date, Sam-il-jeol (March 1st Day), is a national holiday in South Korea.

Background

Korea had been annexed by the Empire of Japan in 1910. Japanese rule, particularly in its first decade, was harsh and militaristic, administered by a Governor-General with broad powers. The death of the former Korean emperor Gojong in January 1919, amid rumors that he had been poisoned, heightened tensions. Abroad, U.S. President Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points, proclaiming the right of national self-determination, inspired Korean students and exiles. Korean students in Tokyo issued a declaration of independence on 8 February 1919, encouraging organizers at home.

The movement

On 1 March 1919, thirty-three Korean cultural and religious leaders — including representatives of Cheondoism, Christianity, and Buddhism — signed a Korean Declaration of Independence drafted by the historian Choe Nam-seon. The signatories gathered at the Taehwagwan restaurant in Seoul and read the declaration, then informed the authorities and submitted to arrest. Simultaneously, a public reading took place in Pagoda Park before a large crowd, after which demonstrators marched through the streets shouting "Dongnip manse!" ("Long live Korean independence!").

The protests spread rapidly across the peninsula and to Korean communities abroad. Participants numbered an estimated two million people in over 1,500 demonstrations. Although the movement's leaders emphasized nonviolence, Japanese police and military forces suppressed the protests with considerable brutality, including mass arrests, beatings, and massacres such as the Jeam-ri massacre, in which residents were locked in a church that was then set on fire.

Aftermath and legacy

The movement failed to achieve independence immediately, and thousands were killed, wounded, or imprisoned — Korean figures cite some 7,500 deaths, while Japanese official figures were lower. However, the movement galvanized Korean nationalism, led to the formation of the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea in Shanghai in April 1919, and pressured Japan to moderate its colonial administration, replacing military rule with a nominally more conciliatory "cultural rule" in the 1920s. The March First Movement is commemorated annually in both Koreas and is regarded as a foundational moment of the modern Korean nation.
