José Oswald de Souza Andrade (11 January 1890 – 22 October 1954) was a Brazilian poet, playwright and cultural agitator, one of the founders of Brazilian modernism. He was a leading figure of the São Paulo Week of Modern Art of 1922 and husband, for a time, of the painter Tarsila do Amaral.

Oswald de Andrade, founder of the anthropophagic movement

Work

In the Manifesto Pau-Brasil (1924) Oswald called for a poetry for export, like Brazilwood, Brazil's first export product. His most famous text, the Manifesto Antropófago (Cannibalist Manifesto, 1928), proposed that Brazil should "cannibalise" European and other foreign cultures — absorbing and transforming them rather than imitating or rejecting them — famously opening with the Shakespearean pun "Tupi, or not Tupi: that is the question". His novels Memórias Sentimentais de João Miramar (1924) and Serafim Ponte Grande (1933) used fragmented, colloquial language influential on later Brazilian writing.

Legacy

The anthropophagic idea was revived in the 1960s by the Tropicalia movement in music and the visual arts, and Oswald's reputation grew steadily after his death, making him a central reference for Brazilian cultural identity debates.
