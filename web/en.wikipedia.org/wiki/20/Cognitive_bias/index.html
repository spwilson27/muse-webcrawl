Cognitive bias

Cognitive bias
FieldCognitive psychology, behavioral science
Key figuresAmos Tversky, Daniel Kahneman
ExamplesConfirmation bias, anchoring, availability heuristic

A cognitive bias is a systematic pattern of deviation from norm or rationality in judgment, whereby inferences about other people and situations may be drawn in an illogical fashion. Individuals create their own "subjective reality" from their perception of the input, and this construction — rather than the objective input — may dictate their behavior in the world.

History

The notion of cognitive biases was introduced by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in 1972, growing out of their experience of people's innumeracy and the heuristics-and-biases research program. They proposed that people rely on a small number of mental shortcuts (heuristics) that usually work but can produce predictable errors.

Types

Well-documented biases include confirmation bias (favoring information that confirms prior beliefs), anchoring (over-relying on an initial piece of information), the availability heuristic (judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind), hindsight bias, the Dunning–Kruger effect, framing effects, and the fundamental attribution error. Lists of cognitive biases now enumerate well over a hundred distinct effects.

Explanations

Biases are variously explained as byproducts of information-processing shortcuts, limited mental capacity, social influence, and evolved adaptations for speed over accuracy. Some researchers caution that "bias" implies an irrational departure from a norm that may itself be contested, and replication failures have tempered claims about some effects.

Practical implications

Cognitive biases matter in medicine, law, finance, intelligence analysis and everyday decision-making. Debiasing strategies include considering alternatives, structured analytic techniques, checklists, and pre-registration of hypotheses.
