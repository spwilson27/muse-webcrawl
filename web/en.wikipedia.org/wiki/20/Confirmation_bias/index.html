Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias (also called confirmatory bias or myside bias) is the tendency to search for, interpret, favor, and recall information in a way that confirms or supports one's prior beliefs or values. People display this bias when they gather or remember information selectively, or when they interpret ambiguous evidence as supporting their existing position.

Experimental evidence

Peter Wason's 2-4-6 task demonstrated the effect: participants asked to discover a rule governing number triples overwhelmingly tested only sequences consistent with their guessed rule and rarely tried falsifying cases. In later studies, people judged arguments supporting their views as stronger than opposing arguments, and ambiguous mixed evidence often polarized attitudes — as in a famous study of capital-punishment attitudes by Lord, Ross and Lepper (1979).

Forms

Psychologists distinguish biased search (seeking confirmatory evidence), biased interpretation (reading ambiguity in one's favor), and biased memory (recalling supporting instances more readily). Related phenomena include belief perseverance, the backfire effect debated in political misinformation research, and motivated skepticism.

Consequences

Confirmation bias contributes to overconfidence, persistence of superstitions and discredited theories, polarization of public debate, and errors in science, investing, medicine and criminal investigation. Considering the opposite and actively seeking disconfirming evidence are standard countermeasures.
