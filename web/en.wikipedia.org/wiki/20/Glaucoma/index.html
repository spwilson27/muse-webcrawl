Glaucoma

SpecialtyOphthalmology
SymptomsGradual loss of peripheral vision; in acute angle closure: pain, redness, blurred vision
CausesDamage to the optic nerve, often associated with raised intraocular pressure
Risk factorsRaised eye pressure, family history, age, certain ethnic backgrounds
TreatmentEye drops, laser treatment, surgery
PrognosisVision loss is irreversible but progression can be slowed

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases that damage the optic nerve, the bundle of fibres carrying visual information from the eye to the brain. It is one of the leading causes of irreversible blindness worldwide. Damage is often associated with abnormally high pressure inside the eye (intraocular pressure), although glaucoma can also occur with normal pressures.

Types

The most common form is open-angle glaucoma, which develops slowly and painlessly as the eye's drainage canals become less efficient; peripheral vision is lost gradually, and the disease may go unnoticed until it is advanced. Angle-closure glaucoma occurs when the drainage angle between the iris and cornea is blocked; its acute form is a medical emergency causing severe eye pain, headache, nausea and blurred vision. Other forms include normal-tension glaucoma, congenital glaucoma in infants, and secondary glaucoma resulting from injury, inflammation or medication.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines measurement of intraocular pressure (tonometry), examination of the optic disc, visual field testing and imaging of the retinal nerve fibre layer. Treatment cannot restore lost vision but can slow progression: first-line therapy is pressure-lowering eye drops such as prostaglandin analogues; laser procedures (for example, selective laser trabeculoplasty) and surgery (such as trabeculectomy or drainage implants) are used when drops are insufficient. Regular eye examinations, especially after age 40 and for those with a family history, are key to early detection.
