Ophthalmology is the branch of medicine and surgery concerned with the diagnosis and treatment of disorders of the eye and the visual system. Ophthalmologists are medical doctors who provide the full spectrum of eye care, from prescribing glasses and contact lenses to performing delicate microsurgery; the field is distinct from optometry, which focuses primarily on vision testing and correction.

Scope

The discipline covers diseases of the eyeball, orbit, eyelids, tear system and visual pathways. Major areas include cataract surgery (one of the most common surgical procedures in the world), glaucoma, retinal diseases such as age-related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy, corneal disease and transplantation, paediatric ophthalmology and strabismus, neuro-ophthalmology, uveitis, ocular oncology and oculoplastic surgery. Refractive surgery, including LASIK, corrects focusing errors by reshaping the cornea.

History

Eye medicine is among the oldest medical specialities: the Egyptian Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BC) describes eye remedies, and the Indian surgeon Sushruta described cataract couching in antiquity. The ophthalmoscope, invented by Hermann von Helmholtz in 1851, revolutionised diagnosis by allowing direct inspection of the retina. In the 20th century, Charles Kelman's phacoemulsification technique transformed cataract surgery, and intraocular lens implantation — pioneered by Harold Ridley — became routine.

Training

Ophthalmologists complete medical school followed by a residency in ophthalmology (typically four years in the United States), with optional fellowship training in subspecialties such as retina or cornea.
