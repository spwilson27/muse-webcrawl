Hindsight bias

Hindsight bias, also known as the knew-it-all-along phenomenon or creeping determinism, is the tendency for people to perceive past events as having been more predictable than they actually were. After an event has occurred, people often believe they would have predicted or expected the outcome, even when there was little objective basis for doing so beforehand.

Research

The effect was demonstrated experimentally in the 1970s by Baruch Fischhoff and others, who showed that knowledge of an outcome increases its perceived inevitability and distorts memory of one's own earlier predictions. Hindsight bias has been documented across many domains, including medical diagnosis, legal judgments, politics, sports, and business decisions.

Explanations and effects

Proposed mechanisms include memory distortion, selective recall of evidence consistent with the known outcome, and a sense-making drive to impose order on events. The bias can lead decision-makers to be judged unfairly by outcomes, discourage reasonable risk-taking, and impede learning from experience, since events that seem inevitable in retrospect seem not to require explanation or preparation.
