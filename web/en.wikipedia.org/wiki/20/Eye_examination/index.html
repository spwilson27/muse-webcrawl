Eye examination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Eye examination
PurposeAssessment of vision and eye health; detection of refractive error and eye disease
Performed byOptometrist, ophthalmologist, or orthoptist
Key testsVisual acuity, refraction, slit-lamp examination, tonometry, ophthalmoscopy, visual field testing

An eye examination is a series of tests performed by an ophthalmologist, optometrist or orthoptist to assess vision and the ability to focus on and discern objects, as well as other tests and examinations pertaining to the health of the eyes. Health care professionals recommend that all people have periodic, thorough eye examinations as part of routine primary care, since many eye diseases are asymptomatic. Eye examinations can detect potentially treatable blinding diseases, ocular manifestations of systemic disease, or signs of tumours or other anomalies of the brain.

Components

A comprehensive eye examination generally begins with a history: symptoms, previous eye conditions, general health, medications and any family history of eye disease. The examination then typically includes:

  • Visual acuity — measured with a Snellen chart or similar logMAR chart, recorded as fractions such as 20/20 (6/6 in metric notation) for distance and near vision.
  • Refraction — determination of the lens prescription needed to correct refractive errors such as myopia, hyperopia and astigmatism, using a phoropter or trial lenses, often aided by objective retinoscopy or autorefraction.
  • Pupil and eye movement assessment — checking pupillary responses, alignment and motility, including cover tests for strabismus.
  • Slit-lamp examination — biomicroscopic inspection of the anterior segment: lids, cornea, anterior chamber, iris and crystalline lens.
  • Tonometry — measurement of intraocular pressure, a screening test for glaucoma, for example by Goldmann applanation.
  • Ophthalmoscopy — examination of the retina, optic disc and blood vessels, often after pupillary dilation.
  • Visual field testing — perimetry to detect blind spots from glaucoma, retinal or neurological disease.

Conditions detected

Beyond refractive error, eye examinations can reveal cataracts, glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy and hypertensive changes in the retinal vessels, as well as neurological signs such as papilloedema. In children, examinations detect amblyopia ("lazy eye") and strabismus at an age when treatment is most effective.

Frequency

Guidelines vary by country and organisation, but a typical recommendation is an examination every one to two years for adults, more often for children, older adults, contact lens wearers, and people with diabetes or a family history of eye disease.
