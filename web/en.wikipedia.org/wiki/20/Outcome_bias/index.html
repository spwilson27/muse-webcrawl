Outcome bias

Outcome bias
TypeCognitive bias
Studied byJonathan Baron, John Hershey (1988)
RelatedHindsight bias, moral luck

The outcome bias is an error made in evaluating the quality of a decision when the outcome of that decision is already known. Specifically, people judge a decision more favorably when it happens to produce a good outcome, and more harshly when it produces a bad one, even when the decision was identical and the outcome was determined by chance or by factors outside the decision-maker's control.

Research

Jonathan Baron and John Hershey demonstrated the bias in a classic 1988 series of experiments: participants rated the same medical or gambling decisions as better when told the outcome was good than when told it was bad. The bias persists even when subjects are warned about it, and it appears in judgments by professionals, including physicians reviewing peer decisions and auditors assessing colleagues.

Distinction from hindsight bias

Outcome bias is related to, but distinct from, hindsight bias: hindsight bias distorts memory of what one knew beforehand ("I knew it all along"), whereas outcome bias contaminates evaluation of the decision itself with knowledge of the result. In poker and investing, a version of the error is called "resulting" — judging a play by its result rather than by the quality of the reasoning at the time.

Implications

The bias affects malpractice litigation, performance reviews, military after-action assessments and historical judgments of leaders. Blinding evaluators to outcomes, where feasible, is a standard remedy.
