Myopia, also known as near-sightedness or short-sightedness, is a refractive error of the eye in which light from distant objects focuses in front of, instead of on, the retina. Distant objects appear blurred while near objects remain clear. It is the most common refractive error worldwide, and its prevalence has risen sharply, particularly in East and Southeast Asia.

Causes and risk factors

Myopia usually results from excessive axial length of the eyeball, though it can also arise from a cornea or lens with too much refractive power. Both genetics and environment contribute: children of myopic parents are at higher risk, and extensive near work with little time outdoors during childhood is associated with onset and progression. Spending time outdoors in bright light appears protective.

Diagnosis and correction

Diagnosis follows a standard eye examination with refraction. Correction uses diverging (minus) lenses in glasses or contact lenses to shift the focal point back onto the retina. Refractive surgery such as LASIK reshapes the cornea in adults. Measures studied to slow progression in children include low-dose atropine drops, orthokeratology lenses, and multifocal contact lenses.

Complications

High myopia (typically worse than −6.00 dioptres) increases the lifetime risk of retinal detachment, myopic macular degeneration, glaucoma and early cataract, and is a leading cause of visual impairment.
