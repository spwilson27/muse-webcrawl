Cinco de Mayo

Cinco de Mayo (Spanish for "Fifth of May") is a holiday celebrated annually on May 5 in parts of Mexico and the United States. It commemorates the Mexican Army's victory over the French Empire at the Battle of Puebla on May 5, 1862, under the leadership of General Ignacio Zaragoza.

Background

In 1861, Mexican president Benito Juárez declared a moratorium on foreign debt payments. France, under Napoleon III, used the debt as a pretext to invade Mexico and install a French-backed monarchy. On May 5, 1862, a poorly equipped Mexican force of about 4,000 soldiers defeated a larger, better-equipped French army at Puebla. The victory was a significant morale boost, though the French eventually captured Mexico City and installed Maximilian I as emperor in 1864; the French were ultimately expelled in 1867.

Celebrations

In Mexico, the day is primarily observed in the state of Puebla, where the battle took place, with military parades and reenactments. Cinco de Mayo is often mistakenly confused with Mexican Independence Day (September 16). In the United States, the day has evolved into a broad celebration of Mexican-American culture and heritage, marked by parades, mariachi music, traditional food, and festivals, particularly in cities with large Mexican-American populations such as Los Angeles, Chicago, and Houston.
