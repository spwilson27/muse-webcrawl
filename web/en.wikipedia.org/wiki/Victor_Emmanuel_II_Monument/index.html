The Victor Emmanuel II National Monument (Italian: Monumento Nazionale a Vittorio Emanuele II), also known as the Vittoriano or Altare della Patria ("Altar of the Fatherland"), is a vast white-marble monument in central Rome built in honour of Victor Emmanuel II, the first king of a unified Italy. It stands between Piazza Venezia and the Capitoline Hill and has become one of the most recognisable — and debated — landmarks of the Italian capital.

Design and construction

Designed by Giuseppe Sacconi, who won the competition in 1884, the monument was built between 1885 and 1935. Its gleaming white Botticino marble, classical colonnades, grand staircases and quadrigae (four-horse chariot statues) atop the structure contrast sharply with Rome's older ochre-toned architecture; Sacconi demolished a medieval quarter on the Capitoline slope to build it. At its centre stands a colossal bronze equestrian statue of Victor Emmanuel, before the Altar of the Fatherland, designed by Angelo Zanelli, with reliefs personifying Italian cities and regions. The monument was inaugurated by King Victor Emmanuel III in 1911 for the fiftieth anniversary of Italian unification.

Tomb of the Unknown Soldier

In 1921 the body of Italy's Unknown Soldier was interred beneath the statue of the goddess Roma at the Altar of the Fatherland, guarded by an eternal flame and sentries, making the monument a national shrine and the setting for official commemorations such as Republic Day (2 June) and Liberation Day.

Reception

Romans have long given the building irreverent nicknames — "the typewriter", "the wedding cake", "the false teeth" — and critics have condemned its scale and brilliance, though attitudes have softened; a panoramic lift to the rooftop terrace (the Terrazza delle Quadrighe) opened in 2007 and offers celebrated views over the Imperial Fora and the city. The monument houses the Central Museum of the Risorgimento and exhibition spaces.
