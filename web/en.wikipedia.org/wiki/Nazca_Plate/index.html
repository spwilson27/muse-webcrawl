The Nazca Plate is a tectonic plate in the eastern Pacific Ocean, off the west coast of South America. It is named after the Nazca region of southern Peru. The plate is subducting beneath the South American Plate along the Peru–Chile Trench, a process responsible for the uplift of the Andes mountains and for much of the seismic and volcanic activity of western South America.

Boundaries

The Nazca Plate is bounded to the west by the East Pacific Rise, a divergent boundary with the Pacific Plate; to the north by the Galápagos spreading centre, where it meets the Cocos Plate; to the south by the Chile Rise, separating it from the Antarctic Plate; and to the east by the convergent Peru–Chile Trench, where it descends beneath South America. Triple junctions at its northern and southern ends — the Galápagos Triple Junction and the Chile Triple Junction — complicate its geometry.

History

The Nazca Plate is a remnant of the ancient Farallon Plate, which began to break apart as it subducted beneath the Americas during the Cenozoic. Around 23 million years ago, near the Oligocene–Miocene boundary, the Farallon Plate split into the Nazca and Cocos plates. The precursor Nazca/Farallon lithosphere has been consumed steadily eastwards; flat-slab segments of subduction beneath Peru and central Chile are associated with gaps in the volcanic arc and with the subduction of buoyant features such as the Nazca Ridge and Juan Fernández Ridge.

Motion and effects

The Nazca Plate converges with South America at roughly 7–8 centimetres per year, one of the fastest absolute plate motions on Earth. This convergence has produced some of the largest recorded earthquakes, including the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile — the most powerful ever measured at magnitude 9.5 — and major events off Peru and northern Chile. Ongoing subduction continues to build the Andes and drive volcanism in the Central and Southern Volcanic Zones.
