Curing (food preservation)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Cured hams hanging in a cellar to age]

Curing is any of various food preservation and flavouring processes for meat, fish and some vegetables, centred on adding salt — often with sugar, spices and nitrates or nitrites — and frequently combined with drying, smoking or fermentation. Salt draws water out of the food and out of microbial cells, making the environment hostile to spoilage bacteria; nitrites fix the rosy colour of cured meats and specifically inhibit Clostridium botulinum. Cured products include ham, bacon, salami, prosciutto, corned beef, salt cod and gravlax.

Methods

In dry curing, the cure mix is rubbed onto the surface and the meat rests for days to months (as for prosciutto and jamón). In wet curing or brining — see brining — meat is submerged in or injected with a salt solution, the standard method for most commercial bacon and ham. Equilibrium curing weighs the salt as a fixed percentage of the meat for precise results. After curing, products may be smoked, dried or fermented.

Nitrates and nitrites

Saltpetre (potassium nitrate) was historically used, sometimes unknowingly via impurities in salt; today sodium nitrite (and nitrate for long cures) is added in regulated amounts. Nitrites give cured meat its colour and tang and protect against botulism, but they can form nitrosamines at high heat, which is why cured-meat consumption is advised in moderation and why "uncured" products using celery juice (a natural nitrate source) have become common.

Famous cured foods

Italy ages prosciutto crudo and culatello; Spain's jamón ibérico cures for years; China produces Jinhua ham; the American South dry-cures country ham; Scandinavia cures salmon with salt, sugar and dill as gravlax; and Newfoundland and the Mediterranean built trades on salt cod — bacalao/baccalà — that reshaped Atlantic history.
