Jewish literature

Jewish literature includes works written by Jews on Jewish themes, works in Jewish languages on any theme, and books written in any language by Jewish authors. It spans more than three millennia, from the Hebrew Bible through rabbinic, medieval, Haskalah, Yiddish, Hebrew-revival and modern diaspora writing.

Ancient and rabbinic literature

The foundational corpus is the Tanakh (Hebrew Bible), followed by the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) and the two Talmuds — the Jerusalem Talmud and the Babylonian Talmud — together with the Midrash, collections of biblical interpretation and legend. Second Temple literature includes the Dead Sea Scrolls, apocrypha and pseudepigrapha, and the histories of Josephus.

Medieval literature

Jewish writers flourished in both Islamic and Christian lands. In Muslim Spain a golden age produced the Hebrew poetry of Solomon ibn Gabirol, Moses ibn Ezra and Judah Halevi, whose Kuzari is a classic of Jewish philosophy. Maimonides wrote the Mishneh Torah legal code in Hebrew and the Guide for the Perplexed in Judeo-Arabic. Medieval Jews also wrote commentaries, responsa, liturgical poetry (piyyut) and, in the 13th century, the Zohar, the central text of Kabbalah.

Modern literature

The Haskalah (Jewish Enlightenment) of the 18th–19th centuries fostered Hebrew prose and poetry; the 19th century saw the flowering of Yiddish literature in the classics of Mendele Mocher Sforim, Sholem Aleichem and I. L. Peretz. The Hebrew revival produced writers such as Hayim Nahman Bialik and S. Y. Agnon, who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1966. Jewish authors — from Isaac Bashevis Singer (Nobel laureate, 1978) to Saul Bellow, Philip Roth and many others — have played a major role in modern world literature, while Israeli literature in modern Hebrew remains a vibrant national literature.
