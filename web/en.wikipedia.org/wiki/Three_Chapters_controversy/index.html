Three Chapters controversy

Datec. 543/544 – c. 698
LocationByzantine Empire; Italy
Key figuresJustinian I; Pope Vigilius; Facundus of Hermiane
OutcomeCondemnation ratified at the Second Council of Constantinople (553)

The controversy culminated in the Fifth Ecumenical Council of 553.

The Three Chapters (Greek: tria kephalaia) were three propositions, persons and writings singled out for condemnation in the sixth century: (1) the person and works of Theodore of Mopsuestia (died 428), teacher of Nestorius; (2) the writings of Theodoret of Cyrus directed against Cyril of Alexandria and the Council of Ephesus; and (3) the letter of Ibas of Edessa to Maris the Persian, which appeared to defend Nestorius. The Three Chapters controversy was the prolonged dispute, lasting roughly a century and a half, over whether these long-dead authors and their works should be anathematised.

Background

After the Council of Chalcedon (451), the Eastern churches remained divided between Chalcedonians and Miaphysites (often called Monophysites), who rejected Chalcedon's formula of Christ in "two natures". The Miaphysites complained that Chalcedon had rehabilitated Nestorian-leaning theologians: Theodoret and Ibas had been restored to their sees at Chalcedon, and Theodore, Nestorius's teacher, had never been condemned at all. To win back the Miaphysites, the emperor Justinian I was persuaded that condemning the Three Chapters would remove the scandal without reopening Chalcedon itself, which he insisted remained fully authoritative.

Justinian's edict

Around 543 or 544, Justinian issued an edict condemning the Three Chapters. The patriarchs of the East subscribed, including Menas of Constantinople. In the West, however, the edict provoked strong resistance: Theodore had died in communion with the Church in 428, and many Latin bishops regarded it as unjust and theologically incoherent to anathematise a dead man who had never been tried in his lifetime. The African bishop Facundus of Hermiane composed a lengthy Defence of the Three Chapters, arguing that condemning Theodore implied condemning Chalcedon, which had received Theodoret and Ibas without requiring them to renounce their earlier writings.

Pope Vigilius and the council of 553

Pope Vigilius was summoned to Constantinople, where he arrived in 547 after a long delay in Sicily. Under imperial pressure he issued his Iudicatum in 548, condemning the Three Chapters while expressly safeguarding Chalcedon; the outcry in the West forced him to withdraw it. Justinian then convened the Second Council of Constantinople (553), the Fifth Ecumenical Council, which Vigilius refused to attend. The council condemned the Three Chapters and added Theodore to its list of heretics. Vigilius, held in Constantinople and fearing deposition, finally capitulated: in December 553 he accepted the council in a letter to Patriarch Eutychius, and in February 554 he issued a second Constitutum condemning the chapters with his own reasoning.

The schism in the West

The condemnation was rejected in parts of Italy. The archbishops of Milan and Aquileia led a schism — the Schism of the Three Chapters — breaking communion with Rome and the other churches that had accepted the council. Milan returned to communion within a few decades, but Aquileia and its suffragans in Istria and Venetia held out, aided by the Lombard invasions after 568. The schism was only finally healed around 698, at the Synod of Pavia under the Lombard king Cunipert.

Assessment

The policy failed in its immediate aim: the Miaphysites were not reconciled, since they distrusted a settlement imposed by imperial edict and regarded the continuing honour paid to Chalcedon as the real obstacle. The affair also badly damaged papal prestige in the short term and demonstrated the extent of Justinian's willingness to coerce the Church in pursuit of religious unity within his empire.
