Georges Cuvier

Jean Léopold Nicolas Frédéric, Baron Cuvier (23 August 1769 – 13 May 1832), known as Georges Cuvier, was a French naturalist and zoologist, sometimes referred to as the "founding father of paleontology". As such, he was a major figure in natural sciences research in the early 19th century and was instrumental in establishing the fields of comparative anatomy and paleontology through his work in comparing living animals with fossils.

Early life and career

Cuvier was born in Montbéliard, then part of the County of Montbéliard in the Holy Roman Empire. He showed an early fascination with natural history, reading Buffon's Histoire naturelle as a boy. He studied at the Karlsschule in Stuttgart, and after a period as a tutor in Normandy — where he dissected marine animals and read his first papers to a local society — he was invited to Paris in 1795 to work at the newly reorganized Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle, where he spent the rest of his career.

Scientific work

Cuvier's work is considered the foundation of vertebrate paleontology. In 1796 he presented his famous paper on living and fossil elephants, demonstrating that the mammoth and the mastodon were species distinct from any living elephant, and that they were extinct. This was a turning point: it established extinction as a scientific fact at a time when many naturalists considered it impossible.

His principle of the correlation of parts held that every organism forms an integrated whole, such that each part's form implies the form of the others — famously, he claimed he could reconstruct an entire animal from a single bone. Applying this, he reconstructed numerous fossil vertebrates from the gypsum quarries of Montmartre in Paris, including Palaeotherium and Anoplotherium.

In his Discours sur les révolutions de la surface du globe (1812), Cuvier argued that the geological record showed a series of sudden catastrophes — "revolutions" — that had destroyed earlier faunas, a doctrine known as catastrophism. His great classification work, Le Règne Animal (1817), divided animals into four great branches or embranchements: Vertebrata, Mollusca, Articulata, and Radiata.

Debate with Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire

In 1830 Cuvier engaged in a celebrated public debate with his colleague Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire at the Academy of Sciences in Paris, defending functional adaptation against Geoffroy's doctrine of a single unified plan of animal structure. The debate attracted attention across Europe and was witnessed in its effects by Goethe, who considered it a landmark in the history of science.

Later life and legacy

Cuvier accumulated numerous state offices under Napoleon and the Bourbon Restoration, serving as a councillor of state and chancellor of the University of Paris, and was raised to the peerage. He died in Paris in 1832, during a cholera epidemic. His name is one of the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower. Cuvier's opposition to early evolutionary ideas, and his disputes with Lamarck and Geoffroy, long overshadowed his reputation, but his foundational role in comparative anatomy and paleontology is now universally recognized.
