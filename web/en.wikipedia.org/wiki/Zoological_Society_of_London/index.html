The Zoological Society of London (ZSL) is a charity devoted to the worldwide conservation of animals and their habitats. It was founded in London in April 1826 by Sir Stamford Raffles, Sir Humphry Davy, and other eminent naturalists and gentlemen. The Society's principal aims were to create a collection of animals for scientific study and to advance zoological knowledge.

History

ZSL received its Royal Charter from George IV in 1829. In 1828 it opened its zoological gardens in Regent's Park — the institution that became London Zoo — initially restricted to fellows and their guests for scientific purposes; it opened to the paying public in 1847 to raise funds. The Society was central to 19th-century zoology: Charles Darwin was a fellow, and the Society's meetings and publications provided a forum in which new species and evolutionary ideas were presented. Its library, founded with the Society, became one of the world's major zoological libraries. In 1931 the Society opened Whipsnade Zoo in Bedfordshire as a large open-range "zoological park".

Activities

Today ZSL operates London Zoo and Whipsnade Zoo, and runs the Institute of Zoology, a research institute affiliated with University College London, working on conservation biology, wildlife disease and population ecology. Its international conservation programmes work in dozens of countries; among its initiatives are the EDGE of Existence programme, which prioritises evolutionarily distinct and globally endangered species, the Living Planet Index (produced with WWF), and co-developing the IUCN Red List assessments for many taxa. ZSL publishes the Journal of Zoology and Animal Conservation, awards scientific medals, and hosts public scientific meetings.
