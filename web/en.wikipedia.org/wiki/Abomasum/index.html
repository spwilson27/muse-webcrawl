The abomasum, also known as the maw, rennet-bag or reed tripe, is the fourth and final stomach compartment in ruminants. It secretes rennet, the source of the first calf rennet used in cheese making. The word abomasum derives from the Latin ab- ("away from") + omasum ("intestine of an ox").

Function

The abomasum is the ruminant's equivalent of the monogastric stomach, and is the only stomach compartment lined with glands that secrete hydrochloric acid and digestive enzymes such as pepsin. Food that has been fermented in the rumen and reticulum, and partially processed in the omasum, passes into the abomasum, where it undergoes acid digestion much as food does in the stomach of a non-ruminant animal.

Clinical significance

A dairy cow may develop a displaced abomasum, particularly shortly after calving, when the abomasum shifts from its normal position on the floor of the abdomen. Left displacement is more common than right displacement; right displacement can progress to abomasal volvulus, a surgical emergency. Abomasal ulcers also occur in cattle and calves.

As food

The abomasum is eaten in some cuisines. In Italy, the abomasum of a young calf, known as lampredotto, is a traditional street food of Florence, typically served in a sandwich with green sauce. In Korea, abomasum (called 막창, makchang) is grilled as a delicacy.
