Archives

An archives is an accumulation of historical records or materials — in any medium — or the physical facility in which they are located. Archives contain primary source documents that have accumulated over the course of an individual or organization's lifetime, and are kept to show the history and functions of that person or organization.

Professional archivists and records managers preserve, organize, describe, and provide access to these materials, which may include letters, diaries, photographs, official records, maps, films, sound recordings, and born-digital files.

Distinction from libraries

Unlike libraries, whose collections consist largely of published items acquired individually, archival materials are generally unique, unpublished, and meaningful only in the context of the whole body of records created by their source — a principle expressed in the concepts of provenance and original order. Archivists arrange records hierarchically (fonds, series, files, items) and describe them in finding aids rather than cataloguing each piece separately.

History

Record-keeping is as old as writing: clay tablets from Mesopotamian palaces, the papyri of Egyptian temples, and the Chinese imperial archives attest to ancient practice. The modern public archives emerged from the French Revolution, when the Archives nationales (1790) declared state records the property of the nation and opened them to citizens. National repositories such as the British Public Record Office (1838) and the U.S. National Archives (1934) followed. In the digital era, archives grapple with preserving email, websites, databases, and social media, and with web archiving projects that capture the internet itself.

Uses

Archives serve genealogists, historians, journalists, lawyers, and citizens verifying rights and entitlements. They underpin accountability of governments and preserve cultural memory — from medieval charters to the punishment books of institutions and the records of social movements.
