Adi Shankara (Śaṅkara, traditionally 788–820 CE) was an Indian philosopher and theologian who consolidated the doctrine of Advaita Vedānta, the non-dualistic interpretation of the Upanishads. Born in Kaladi in present-day Kerala, he renounced household life as a boy, studied under Govinda Bhagavatpāda, and in a traditionally brief life of 32 years travelled across India debating rival schools.

Thought

Shankara taught that Brahman — pure, attributeless consciousness — alone is ultimately real; the world of plurality is māyā, an appearance superimposed on Brahman through ignorance (avidyā), as a rope is misperceived as a snake. The individual self (ātman) is not other than Brahman, as the great sayings (mahāvākya) of the Upaniṣads declare: "That thou art" (tat tvam asi). Liberation (Moksha) is not produced but recognised, through knowledge. He distinguished two standpoints of truth — the conventional (vyāvahārika) and the ultimate (pāramārthika) — allowing worldly and religious practice their validity at their own level.

Legacy

Tradition credits Shankara with founding four cardinal monasteries (maṭhas) at Sringeri, Puri, Dwarka and Joshimath, and the Dashanami order of monks. His commentaries set the terms for later Vedānta, against which Rāmānuja's qualified non-dualism and Madhva's dualism defined themselves. Many hymns attributed to him, such as Bhaja Govindam, remain popular; their authorship is debated.
