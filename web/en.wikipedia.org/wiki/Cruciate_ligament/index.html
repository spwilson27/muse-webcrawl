Cruciate ligament

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diagram of the knee joint, showing the cruciate ligaments crossing in its centre.

The cruciate ligaments are a pair of ligaments arranged like a letter X in the centre of the human knee joint. They join the femur (thigh bone) to the tibia (shin bone) and are essential for stabilizing the joint during movement. The name derives from the Latin crux, meaning cross, because the two ligaments cross over each other.

Structure

The two cruciate ligaments are:

  • The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL), which runs upward, backward, and laterally from the front of the tibia to the femur. It prevents the tibia from sliding forward relative to the femur and limits rotational movement.
  • The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL), which runs upward and forward from the back of the tibia to the femur. It is stronger than the ACL and prevents the tibia from being displaced backward.

The ligaments lie within the knee joint capsule but outside the synovial cavity. They are composed of dense bands of collagen fibres and receive their blood supply largely from the middle genicular artery. Together with the collateral ligaments and the menisci, they guide the rolling and gliding motion of the knee.

Clinical significance

Injury to the cruciate ligaments is common in sport. The anterior cruciate ligament is one of the most frequently injured ligaments of the knee, typically torn by sudden twisting, pivoting, or landing movements in sports such as football, basketball, and skiing. ACL rupture causes instability of the knee and is often treated with surgical reconstruction using a tendon graft, followed by rehabilitation.

Posterior cruciate ligament injuries are less common and usually result from a direct blow to the front of the bent knee, as in a dashboard injury in a car accident or a fall onto a flexed knee. Many isolated PCL injuries are managed without surgery.

Diagnosis of cruciate ligament injury relies on physical examination tests such as the Lachman test and the anterior and posterior drawer tests, together with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Cruciate ligaments also occur in other animals, and their injury is a well-known problem in veterinary orthopaedics, particularly in dogs.
