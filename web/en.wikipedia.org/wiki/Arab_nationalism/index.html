Arab nationalism (Arabic: al-qawmiyya al-ʿarabiyya) is a political ideology asserting that the Arabs constitute a single nation and promoting the cultural and political unity of Arab peoples. It emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries in the Arab provinces of the Ottoman Empire, partly in reaction to Turkish nationalist policies, and drew strength from the Nahda, the Arab literary and cultural renaissance.

Origins and the Arab Revolt

Thinkers such as Sati' al-Husri grounded Arab nationhood in shared language and history rather than religion, making the ideology in principle secular and inclusive of Christian Arabs such as Michel Aflaq, co-founder of the Ba'ath Party. During the First World War, Sharif Hussein of Mecca launched the Arab Revolt (1916) against the Ottomans in expectation of an independent Arab state; instead the region was divided under the Sykes–Picot Agreement and League of Nations mandates, a betrayal that fuelled interwar nationalism.

Nasserism and Ba'athism

After the Second World War, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt became the movement's charismatic leader, especially after the 1956 Suez Crisis. Egypt and Syria briefly united as the United Arab Republic (1958–61). The Ba'ath Party, preaching "Unity, Freedom, Socialism", came to power in Syria (1963) and Iraq (1963, and again 1968), entrenching regimes such as that of the Assad family in Syria.

Decline

The Arab defeat in the 1967 Six-Day War badly damaged the ideology's credibility, and from the 1970s political Islam increasingly displaced secular pan-Arabism, though Arab nationalist themes persist in regional politics and in the symbols — flags, anthems, institutions such as the Arab League (founded 1945) — of the Arab states.
