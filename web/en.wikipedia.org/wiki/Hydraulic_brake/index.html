Hydraulic brake

A hydraulic brake is an arrangement of braking mechanism which uses brake fluid, typically containing glycol ethers or diethylene glycol, to transfer pressure from the controlling mechanism to the braking mechanism. Hydraulic brakes are used on passenger cars, motorcycles, trucks and some bicycles, and work according to Pascal's principle: pressure applied to an incompressible fluid in a closed system is transmitted equally throughout the system.

Operation

When the driver presses the brake pedal, a piston in the master cylinder pressurizes the brake fluid. The pressure travels through steel lines and flexible hoses to calipers at each wheel, where pistons press friction pads against a rotating disc, or shoes against the inside of a drum in drum brakes. Because the fluid is nearly incompressible, force is transmitted almost instantly and can be multiplied by using different piston diameters.

History

Malcolm Loughead (later Lockheed) developed a hydraulic braking system in 1918, and it appeared on the 1921 Duesenberg Model A. Chrysler adopted hydraulic brakes across its range in the 1920s, and by the 1930s they had largely replaced mechanical rod-and-cable brakes on cars. Dual-circuit master cylinders became mandatory in the United States in 1967 so that a single leak could not disable all braking.

Modern systems

Modern hydraulic brakes are combined with vacuum or electric boosters, anti-lock braking systems (ABS), electronic brakeforce distribution and stability control. Bicycle hydraulic disc brakes, widespread since the late 1990s, use small master cylinders in the brake levers and either DOT fluid or mineral oil.

Maintenance

Glycol-based brake fluids absorb water over time, lowering their boiling point, so periodic replacement and bleeding of air from the system are required.
