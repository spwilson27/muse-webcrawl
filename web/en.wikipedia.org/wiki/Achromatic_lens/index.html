Achromatic lens

An achromatic lens, or achromat, is a lens designed to limit chromatic aberration — the failure of a simple lens to focus all colours to the same point, because its refractive index varies with wavelength. The classic achromat combines two elements made of glasses with different dispersions so that two wavelengths (typically red and blue) are brought to a common focus.

Design

The most common form is the achromatic doublet: a positive element of crown glass cemented or mounted with a weaker negative element of flint glass. The flint's stronger dispersion cancels the crown's colour error while only partly cancelling its power, leaving a net converging lens largely free of colour fringing at two chosen wavelengths. Residual error at other wavelengths, the "secondary spectrum", is reduced further in apochromatic designs using three elements or special low-dispersion glasses.

History

The invention of the achromatic doublet in the 1730s–1750s was disputed between Chester Moore Hall, who devised the combination around 1733, and the optician John Dollond, who patented it in 1758 and commercialised it for telescopes. Achromats transformed refracting telescopes and microscopy, and remain standard in binoculars, camera lenses and microscope objectives.
