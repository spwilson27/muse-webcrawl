Ordoliberalism

Ordoliberalism is the German variant of economic liberalism developed by the Freiburg school of economics in the 1930s and 1940s. It holds that markets can only deliver prosperity and freedom if the state creates and maintains a strong legal and institutional framework — an "economic constitution" (from Latin ordo, order) — that ensures competition, stable money, private property, freedom of contract and liability, while refraining from discretionary intervention in market processes.

Thinkers

Leading ordoliberals included the economists Walter Eucken, Franz Böhm and Hans Großmann-Doerth (the 1936 Freiburg manifesto), Alexander Rüstow and Wilhelm Röpke. Their journal ORDO, founded in 1948, gave the school its name. Alfred Müller-Armack coined the term "social market economy" for the related policy programme.

Principles

Eucken's "constituting principles" of a competitive order include a functioning price system, primacy of sound money, open markets, private property, freedom of contract, full liability and constancy of economic policy; "regulating principles" then address monopolies, externalities, abnormal labour supply and extreme income inequality. The state should set and enforce the rules of the game, not direct outcomes.

Influence

Ordoliberal ideas shaped post-war West German policy under Economics Minister Ludwig Erhard, the independence of the Bundesbank, German and later EU competition law, and — critics argue — the euro area's fiscal rules and the handling of the eurozone debt crisis.
