Entropy encoding

In information theory, entropy encoding (or entropy coding) is any lossless data compression scheme that assigns codewords to symbols such that the codeword lengths are based on the probabilities of the symbols. The most frequent symbols are given the shortest codewords, so that the average length approaches the entropy of the source, the theoretical lower bound established by Shannon's source coding theorem.

Techniques

The principal families of entropy encoding are:

  • Huffman coding — builds an optimal prefix code from symbol frequencies; used in formats such as DEFLATE (ZIP, gzip, PNG).
  • Arithmetic coding — encodes the entire message into a single number in the interval [0, 1), achieving compression closer to the entropy limit than Huffman coding.
  • Asymmetric numeral systems (ANS) — a newer family that combines the compression ratio of arithmetic coding with speeds comparable to Huffman coding; widely used in modern compressors such as zstd.
  • Range coding — closely related to arithmetic coding.

Applications

Entropy encoding forms the final stage of most lossy compression pipelines as well: in JPEG image compression, quantised transform coefficients are entropy-coded (with Huffman or arithmetic coding), and video codecs such as H.264 use context-adaptive schemes (CAVLC and CABAC) built on the same principles.
