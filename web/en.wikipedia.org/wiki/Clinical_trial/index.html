Clinical trial

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A clinical trial is a research study in human volunteers that tests whether a medical intervention — a drug, vaccine, device, procedure or behavioural change — is safe and effective. Trials are the main route by which new treatments reach patients, and their modern design, with randomisation and control groups, is a cornerstone of evidence-based Medicine.

Phases

Drug development proceeds through phases: Phase I tests safety and dose in a small group; Phase II explores effectiveness and side effects in a few hundred patients; Phase III compares the treatment with standard care in large, usually randomised, double-blind trials; Phase IV monitors effects after licensing. Trials must be ethically approved, registered publicly, and use informed consent; many candidate drugs fail along the way — only a minority entering Phase I reach approval.

History and safeguards

Landmarks include James Lind's 1747 scurvy comparison and the 1948 streptomycin trial, a model of randomisation. Safeguards were hardened after abuses such as the Tuskegee study and the thalidomide disaster, producing research-ethics codes (Declaration of Helsinki) and medicines regulation. Adaptive and platform trials, prominent during COVID-19, test several treatments efficiently under one protocol.
