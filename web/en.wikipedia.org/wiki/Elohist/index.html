Elohist

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Elohist (abbreviated E) is one of four sources of the Torah proposed by the documentary hypothesis, alongside the Jahwist (J), the Deuteronomist (D), and the Priestly source (P). It is named for its characteristic use of Elohim as the name of God in the period before the revelation of the divine name to Moses. The Elohist source is generally thought to have originated in the northern Kingdom of Israel and is usually dated to the ninth or eighth century BC, earlier than the Priestly source but later than, or contemporary with, the Jahwist.

Characteristics

Elohist passages tend to emphasise prophecy, dreams and visions as the media of divine communication, the fear of God as a moral motive, and northern Israelite concerns and locations such as Shechem and Bethel. God is portrayed more abstractly and less anthropomorphically than in the Jahwist source: he speaks in dreams rather than walking in gardens. Moses plays a larger role in E than in J, and figures such as Abraham — for example in the account of the binding of Isaac (Genesis 22) — are presented as prophets and models of obedience.

Extent of the source

Because the Elohist material survives only as interwoven with the Jahwist text, its exact extent is debated. It is often held to begin around Genesis 15 or Genesis 20 and to include the Abraham and Jacob cycles in part, the Exodus narrative's account of the plagues and the covenant at Mount Horeb, and the Decalogue. Some scholars have questioned whether E was ever an independent continuous document, suggesting instead that it may represent a collection of fragments or a redactional layer.

Modern scholarship

The classical documentary hypothesis of Julius Wellhausen assigned E a central place in the reconstruction of Israelite religion. Later scholars — including proponents of the supplementary and fragmentary hypotheses — have variously reduced, split, or re-dated the Elohist, and in much European scholarship the very existence of a distinct E source is now contested, though it remains widely used in North American and Israeli scholarship.
