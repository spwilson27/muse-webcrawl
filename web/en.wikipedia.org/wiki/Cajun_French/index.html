Cajun French (French: français cadien) is a variety of the French language spoken mainly in the state of Louisiana in the United States. It is the language of the Cajun people, descendants of Acadian exiles who settled in southern Louisiana after being deported from Acadia (in present-day Canada) by the British beginning in 1755, an event known as le Grand Dérangement.

History

The Acadian settlers adapted their 17th-century Canadian French to new conditions in Louisiana, absorbing vocabulary from Spanish administrators, Native American peoples, African slaves and later Anglo-American settlers. For much of the 19th and early 20th centuries French was the everyday language of the bayou parishes.

A 1921 state constitution effectively prohibited French instruction in public schools, and children were punished for speaking French on school grounds. Coupled with the influence of English-language media and the oil industry, this caused a steep decline in transmission of the language.

Revitalisation

In 1968 Louisiana created CODOFIL (Council for the Development of French in Louisiana) to promote French education. French immersion programmes have since operated in several parishes, though they often teach standard French rather than the Cajun variety. Organisations and musicians, including Zachary Richard and the band Lost Bayou Ramblers, have worked to keep Cajun French visible in popular culture.

Characteristics

Cajun French preserves some archaic features lost in modern standard French, distinctive vocabulary (e.g. char for car, asteur "now", from à cette heure), and characteristic expressions such as laissez les bons temps rouler ("let the good times roll"). It differs from Louisiana Creole, a separate French-based creole language, and from the colonial French spoken around New Orleans.

Status

The language is considered endangered: most fluent speakers are elderly, and the number of speakers has fallen from around one million in the 1960s to a small fraction of that today.
