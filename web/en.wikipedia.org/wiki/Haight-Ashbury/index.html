Haight-Ashbury is a district of San Francisco, California, named for the intersection of Haight and Ashbury streets. It is also called the Haight and the Upper Haight. The neighbourhood is known as one of the centres of the American counterculture of the 1960s and the birthplace of the hippie movement.

History

The area was developed in the 1880s–1900s with Victorian houses for middle-class families, near Golden Gate Park. By the 1960s the grand but subdivided Victorians offered cheap rents, attracting students, artists and, from about 1965, the first hippies. Psychedelic rock bands including the Grateful Dead (at 710 Ashbury), Jefferson Airplane and Janis Joplin's Big Brother and the Holding Company lived in the district; the Psychedelic Shop opened on Haight Street in 1966, and the Diggers ran a free store and free food distribution.

The neighbourhood became the epicentre of the Summer of Love in 1967, when as many as 100,000 young people converged on San Francisco. The Human Be-In at Golden Gate Park in January 1967 and intense media coverage made the Haight a symbol of peace, psychedelic drugs and communal living. Overcrowding, hard drugs, crime and disease soon overwhelmed the scene; activists staged a mock funeral, the "Death of Hippie", in October 1967. In later decades the Upper Haight gentrified, though it retains head shops, vintage clothing stores, Amoeba Music and a countercultural identity.
