Overview

The Vienna Convention on Consular Relations is an international treaty that defines a framework for consular relations between sovereign states, signed in Vienna in 1963 and in force since 1967. It codifies the work of consuls — protecting their nationals abroad, issuing visas and documents, assisting trade and shipping — and the privileges and immunities of consular posts, which are narrower than diplomatic ones.

Article 36 and the courts

Its most litigated provision is Article 36: a detained foreign national must be informed "without delay" of the right to have their consulate notified and to communicate with it. The International Court of Justice enforced this right in LaGrand (Germany v. United States, 2001) and Avena (Mexico v. United States, 2004), both involving death-row prisoners not informed of their rights; the US Supreme Court in Medellín v. Texas (2008) held the ICJ judgment not directly enforceable in US courts absent legislation. The United States withdrew from the Convention's Optional Protocol on dispute settlement in 2005.
