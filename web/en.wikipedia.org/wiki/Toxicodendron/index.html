Toxicodendron is a genus of flowering plants in the sumac family, Anacardiaceae. It contains trees, shrubs and woody vines, including poison ivy, poison oak, poison sumac and the lacquer tree. All species produce the skin-irritating oil urushiol, which can cause severe allergic contact dermatitis in most people. The generic name is derived from the Greek words τοξικόν (toxikon), "poison", and δένδρον (dendron), "tree".

Description

Members of the genus have alternate, pinnately compound leaves, often with three leaflets — the origin of the warning "leaves of three, let it be". The flowers are small, greenish or white, and the fruits are small whitish or grey drupes, a feature that distinguishes Toxicodendron from true sumacs (Rhus), which have red fruit. The foliage often turns brilliant red, orange or yellow in autumn. Members of the genus were formerly placed within Rhus, and some classifications still include them there.

Urushiol

All parts of the plants contain urushiol, a mixture of pentadecylcatechols, present in resin canals. Contact with the oil — even tiny amounts — causes an allergic rash in roughly 85% of people, appearing one to three days after exposure as itching, redness, swelling and blistering. Sensitivity tends to increase with repeated exposure. Urushiol is also the active component of the sap of the lacquer tree (Toxicodendron vernicifluum), used for centuries in East Asia to make durable urushi lacquerware; the polymerised, dried lacquer is inert and food-safe.

Species

Well-known species include:

  • Toxicodendron radicans — eastern poison ivy, widespread in eastern North America
  • Toxicodendron rydbergii — western poison ivy
  • Toxicodendron diversilobum — Pacific (western) poison oak
  • Toxicodendron pubescens — Atlantic poison oak
  • Toxicodendron vernix — poison sumac, of swamps in eastern North America, considered the most allergenic
  • Toxicodendron vernicifluum — the Chinese lacquer tree
  • Toxicodendron succedaneum — the wax tree of Asia and Australasia

Distribution and ecology

The genus is centred on North America and eastern Asia, with species extending into Central and South America and Australasia. The white berries are eaten by many birds, which are unaffected by urushiol and disperse the seeds. Deer and other animals browse the foliage without harm.

Avoidance and treatment

Recognition and avoidance are the main defences. Washing exposed skin promptly with soap and cool water, or with specialised cleansers, can remove urushiol before it binds to the skin; tools and clothing should also be washed, since the oil remains active on surfaces for years. Most cases of the rash resolve in one to three weeks; severe cases may require corticosteroids.
