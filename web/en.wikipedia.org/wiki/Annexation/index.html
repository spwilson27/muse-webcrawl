Annexation

Annexation
Type: Unilateral acquisition of territory
Field: International law
Modern status: Annexation by force prohibited under the UN Charter
Related: Cession, occupation, secession
The proclamation of the annexation of Hawaii by the United States in 1898, an example of annexation effected by joint resolution of the annexing state's legislature.

Annexation is the unilateral acquisition of territory by one state from another, in which the annexing state proclaims sovereignty over the territory and incorporates it as its own. It differs from cession, in which territory is transferred by agreement or treaty, and from military occupation, which is in principle temporary and does not transfer sovereignty.

Historical practice

Annexation following conquest was a recognised mode of acquiring territory under classical international law. Examples include the Prussian annexations of German states after the Austro-Prussian War of 1866, the United States' annexation of Texas (1845) and Hawaii (1898), and Germany's annexation of Austria in the Anschluss of 1938.

Prohibition under modern law

The Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928) and, decisively, the Charter of the United Nations (1945) outlawed war as an instrument of national policy and prohibited the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity of any state. The 1970 Declaration on Principles of International Law (the Friendly Relations Declaration) states that no territorial acquisition resulting from the threat or use of force shall be recognised as legal. The annexation of territory seized by force is therefore widely considered invalid, and other states are expected not to recognise it — a position associated with the Stimson Doctrine of non-recognition, first articulated after Japan's seizure of Manchuria in 1932.

Examples in the modern era

Annexations since 1945 have generally attracted widespread non-recognition. Examples discussed in these terms include Indonesia's annexation of East Timor (1975, never recognised by the UN), Israel's annexation of the Golan Heights (1981, declared "null and void" by UN Security Council Resolution 497), Turkey's role in Northern Cyprus, Morocco's annexation of Western Sahara, and Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014 and of four Ukrainian oblasts in 2022, the latter condemned by the UN General Assembly as attempted illegal annexation.
