Ange-Jacques Gabriel (23 October 1698 – 4 January 1782) was the principal architect of the reign of Louis XV of France and a leading figure in the transition from Rococo to early Neoclassicism. As Premier architecte du Roi from 1742, he reshaped the Palace of Versailles and the face of Paris.

Major works

Gabriel came from a dynasty of architects: his father, Jacques Gabriel, had worked under Jules Hardouin-Mansart. At Versailles, Ange-Jacques completed the palace's north wing and the Royal Opera (1770), and designed the Petit Trianon (1762–1768), a masterpiece of restrained classical taste built for Madame de Pompadour and later associated with Marie Antoinette.

In Paris he designed the twin palaces and colonnades of the Place de la Concorde (then Place Louis XV) and the École Militaire. Elsewhere he built the château de Compiègne's main ranges and worked at Fontainebleau and the Louvre.

Style

Gabriel's architecture is noted for its serene proportions, smooth ashlar surfaces and chaste ornament — a deliberate reaction against Rococo exuberance, and a bridge between the French classical tradition and the Neoclassicism of the later eighteenth century.
