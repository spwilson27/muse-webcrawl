Greco-Persian Wars

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Greek hoplites advancing in phalanx, in a modern reconstruction]

The Greco-Persian Wars (499–449 BC) were a series of conflicts between the Achaemenid Empire of Persia and the Greek city-states, beginning with the Ionian Revolt and ending, traditionally, with the Peace of Callias. The wars' most famous episodes — the Persian defeats at Marathon (490 BC), Salamis (480 BC) and Plataea (479 BC) after the heroic stand at Thermopylae — became foundational stories of Greek, and later Western, identity: small, free citizen communities resisting a vast empire. Herodotus' Histories, written to explain the wars, effectively founded the discipline of history.

From revolt to Marathon

The Greek cities of Ionia in Asia Minor, under Persian rule since Cyrus, revolted in 499 BC with fleeting Athenian and Eretrian aid, burning Sardis before being crushed by 493 BC. Darius I's punitive expedition of 490 BC destroyed Eretria but was defeated at Marathon by Athenian and Plataean hoplites under Miltiades — 192 Athenian dead by Herodotus' count against thousands of Persians — a victory Athens celebrated for centuries.

Xerxes' invasion and its defeat

Xerxes I's immense invasion of 480 BC — Herodotus counts millions, modern scholars perhaps 100,000–300,000 soldiers — forced the pass of Thermopylae after Leonidas' Spartans died holding it, and burned Athens. But the Greek fleet destroyed the Persian navy in the straits of Salamis; the next year the allied army annihilated Mardonius' forces at Plataea and the fleet won again at Mycale. Athens then led the Delian League in decades of counter-offensive until hostilities faded by about 449 BC.

Legacy

The wars saved the Greek experiment — democracy, philosophy, drama flourished in their aftermath — and shaped Greek identity around the barbarian 'other'. Modern readings are more sceptical of the freedom-versus-despotism framing: Persia was a sophisticated empire, and many Greeks served or allied with it. The wars nonetheless remain the best-documented conflict of the early classical world, thanks to Herodotus and Aeschylus (himself a Marathon veteran).
