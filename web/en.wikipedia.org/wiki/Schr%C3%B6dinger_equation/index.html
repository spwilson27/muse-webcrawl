Schrödinger equation

The Schrödinger equation is a linear partial differential equation that governs the wave function of a quantum-mechanical system. Proposed by Austrian physicist Erwin Schrödinger in 1925 and published in 1926, it is the fundamental equation of non-relativistic quantum mechanics.

Formulation

In its time-dependent form, the equation states that iħ ∂Ψ/∂t = ĤΨ, where Ψ is the wave function, Ĥ the Hamiltonian operator, ħ the reduced Planck constant, and i the imaginary unit. The time-independent form, ĤΨ = EΨ, yields the allowed energy levels of a system.

Interpretation

Under Max Born's statistical interpretation, the squared modulus of the wave function gives the probability density of finding the particle at a given position. Solutions for the hydrogen atom correctly reproduce its spectral lines.

Significance

Schrödinger shared the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physics with Paul Dirac for the discovery of new productive forms of atomic theory. The equation underpins quantum chemistry and much of modern physics, though it breaks down at relativistic energies, where the Dirac and Klein–Gordon equations apply.
