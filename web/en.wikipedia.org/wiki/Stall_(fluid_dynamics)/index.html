In fluid dynamics, a stall is a reduction in the lift coefficient generated by a foil as its angle of attack increases. This occurs when the critical angle of attack of the foil is exceeded. The critical angle of attack is typically about 15 degrees, but it may vary significantly depending on the fluid, foil geometry, and Reynolds number.

Stalls in fixed-wing flight are often identified as a sudden reduction in lift generated by the wing when the pilot increases the angle of attack and exceeds its critical angle. A stall does not mean that the engine or airflow has stopped entirely; in aviation terminology it refers specifically to the loss of lift.

Formal definition

A stall is a condition in aerodynamics and aviation in which, if the angle of attack on an aircraft increases beyond a certain point, lift begins to decrease. The angle at which this occurs is called the critical angle of attack. If the angle of attack exceeds the critical angle, the airflow over the upper surface of the wing begins to separate from the wing surface, becoming turbulent and destroying the smooth flow that produces lift.

Variation with airspeed

The airspeed at which an aircraft stalls varies with the weight of the aircraft, the load factor, the centre of gravity, and other factors. However, an aircraft always stalls at the same critical angle of attack for a given configuration. The indicated stall speed therefore increases in turns and manoeuvres, because the effective angle of attack reached at a given speed is higher under load.

Stall in various flight regimes

Takeoff and landing

Stalls are most hazardous at low altitude during takeoff and landing, where there is little height available for recovery. Aircraft are therefore fitted with stall warning devices, and pilots practise stall recognition and recovery during training.

Accelerated stall

An accelerated stall occurs when a pilot increases the load factor, for example in a steep turn or abrupt pull-up, causing the wing to exceed the critical angle of attack at a speed well above the straight-and-level stall speed.

Deep stall

A deep stall (or super-stall) is a dangerous condition affecting certain aircraft designs, notably those with a T-tail, in which the turbulent wake from the stalled wing blankets the tailplane and renders the elevators ineffective, making recovery difficult or impossible.

Recovery

The standard recovery from a stall is to reduce the angle of attack, typically by moving the control column forward, while applying power and using the rudder to keep the wings level. Recovery requires sacrificing some altitude.

Compressor stall

A related phenomenon, compressor stall, occurs in axial compressors of jet engines when the compressor blades stall, disrupting airflow through the engine and potentially causing a surge.
