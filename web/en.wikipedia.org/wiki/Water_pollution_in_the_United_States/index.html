Water pollution in the United States

Industrial and municipal discharges were largely unregulated in the United States before 1972.

Water pollution in the United States is a significant environmental problem governed principally by two federal laws: the Clean Water Act (1972), which regulates discharges of pollutants into surface waters, and the Safe Drinking Water Act (1974), which sets standards for public drinking-water systems. Pollution enters waterways from point sources, such as factory and sewage plant outfalls, and from nonpoint sources — above all agricultural and urban stormwater runoff — which are harder to regulate and now constitute the largest remaining source of impairment.

History

Through the 19th and most of the 20th century, American rivers served as open sewers for cities and industry. Symbolic disasters galvanized reform: the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland, choked with oil and debris, caught fire most famously in 1969, and the chemical dump at Love Canal, New York, became a national scandal in the late 1970s. Congress responded with the Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments of 1972 (the Clean Water Act), requiring technology-based effluent limits and permits, with a goal of making all waters "fishable and swimmable". Since then, gross pollution from point sources has declined markedly, municipal wastewater treatment has become near-universal, and many once-dead rivers have recovered.

Contemporary problems

Modern challenges include nutrient runoff driving eutrophication and the hypoxic "dead zone" in the Gulf of Mexico at the Mississippi River's mouth; combined sewer overflows in older cities; legacy and emerging contaminants such as lead service lines — exposed by the Flint, Michigan water crisis beginning in 2014 — and PFAS "forever chemicals"; and unregulated or lightly regulated agricultural drainage. Large numbers of assessed river miles, lake acres and coastal waters still fail to meet water quality standards.
