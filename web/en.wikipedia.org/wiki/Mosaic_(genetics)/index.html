Mosaic (genetics)

Mosaicism
FieldGenetics
CausePost-zygotic mutation or mitotic error during development
TypesSomatic; germline (gonadal); tissue-limited
Distinguished fromChimerism (fusion of two zygotes)

In genetics, a mosaic is an individual composed of two or more populations of cells with different genotypes, all derived from a single fertilised egg. Mosaicism arises from a mutation, chromosome non-disjunction, or other mitotic error occurring after fertilisation: the earlier in development the change happens, the more of the body it affects.

Types

  • Somatic mosaicism — the variant is present in some body cells but not others. It underlies such conditions as McCune–Albright syndrome and Proteus syndrome, and acquired mosaic mutations drive cancers.
  • Germline (gonadal) mosaicism — the variant is present in some eggs or sperm, so a parent without the condition can have more than one affected child.
  • Chromosomal mosaicism — some cells carry an abnormal chromosome number, e.g. mosaic Down syndrome or mosaic Turner syndrome, often with milder features than the full condition.

Examples in nature

Every female mammal is a functional mosaic: random X-chromosome inactivation in early development silences one X in each cell, producing the patchwork coat of calico cats. Genetic mosaicism has also been exploited as a research tool, for instance in Drosophila "mosaic analysis" and in MARCM techniques that label single mutant clones in the brain. A related phenomenon, chimerism, differs in that the distinct cell lines come from two separate zygotes.

Clinical significance

Mosaicism complicates genetic testing, since blood may not reflect the genotype of affected tissue, and it modifies prognosis and recurrence-risk counselling. Modern deep sequencing increasingly reveals low-level mosaic variants in development disorders and in healthy ageing blood (clonal haematopoiesis).
