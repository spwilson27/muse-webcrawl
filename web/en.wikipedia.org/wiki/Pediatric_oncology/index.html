Pediatric oncology

Pediatric oncology is the branch of medicine concerned with the diagnosis and treatment of cancer in children and adolescents. Childhood cancers are often quite different from adult cancers: they most frequently arise from embryonic or developing tissues rather than from long environmental exposure, and they tend to respond differently to treatment.

Common types

The most common childhood cancers include leukemias (especially acute lymphoblastic leukemia), brain and central nervous system tumors, neuroblastoma, Wilms tumor, lymphomas (Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin), rhabdomyosarcoma, retinoblastoma, and bone cancers such as osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma.

Treatment

Treatment is usually delivered at specialized children's cancer centers and frequently involves enrollment in clinical trials coordinated by cooperative groups such as the Children's Oncology Group. Modalities include chemotherapy, surgery, radiation therapy, immunotherapy, and stem cell transplantation. Because children are still growing, long-term follow-up for late effects of treatment — including secondary cancers, cardiac damage, and cognitive effects — is a major component of care.

Prognosis

Overall five-year survival for childhood cancer in high-income countries has risen to above 80 percent, though outcomes vary greatly by type and remain much poorer in low-income settings. Survivorship care and research into less toxic targeted therapies are central goals of the field.
