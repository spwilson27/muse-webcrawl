Mongolian cuisine

Mongolian cuisine comprises the culinary traditions of Mongolia and Mongolic peoples, shaped by the country's extreme continental climate and its predominantly nomadic, pastoral way of life. The diet centres on meat — especially mutton, beef, horse, goat and camel — and dairy products from the "five animals" (sheep, goats, cattle/yaks, horses and camels). Vegetables and spices are comparatively limited, though urban cuisine has diversified in recent decades.

Dairy products

Dairy ("white foods", tsagaan idee) dominates the summer diet. Milk from mares is fermented into airag (kumis), a lightly alcoholic drink central to hospitality. Other staples include dried curds (aaruul), clotted cream (öröm), butter, cheese (byaslag) and milk tea (süütei tsai), made with water, milk, tea leaves and salt.

Meat dishes

Meat ("red foods") predominates in winter. Signature dishes include:

  • Buuz — steamed dumplings filled with minced mutton or beef, eaten in quantity at Tsagaan Sar (Lunar New Year).
  • Khuushuur — deep-fried meat-filled pastry pockets, popular at the Naadam festival.
  • Khorkhog — meat (often mutton or goat) cooked in a sealed container with hot stones and a little water.
  • Boodog — goat or marmot cooked from the inside with hot stones placed in the body cavity.
  • Tsuivan — a stew of hand-pulled noodles with meat and vegetables.

Customs

Food is inseparable from hospitality: a guest in a ger is offered milk tea and dairy, and refusing is impolite. Traditional rules govern the carving and offering of meat, and vodka, introduced in the Soviet era, accompanies celebrations.

Modern developments

Since the 1990s, urban Mongolian cooking has incorporated more vegetables, rice and international influences. The "Mongolian barbecue" popularised abroad — meat and vegetables stir-fried on a griddle — was actually created in Taiwan in the 1950s and bears little relation to traditional Mongolian cooking.
