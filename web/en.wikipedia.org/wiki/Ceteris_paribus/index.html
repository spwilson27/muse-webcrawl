Ceteris paribus

LanguageLatin
Literal meaning"Other things being equal"
Used inEconomics, logic, philosophy of science

Ceteris paribus (also spelled caeteris paribus) is a Latin phrase meaning "other things being equal" or "all other relevant things considered, are equal". A prediction or statement about a causal, empirical or logical relation between two states of affairs is ceteris paribus if it is acknowledged that the prediction, although usually accurate in expected conditions, can fail because of intervening factors.

Use in economics

The phrase is fundamental to economic analysis. The law of demand, for example, states that, ceteris paribus — holding income, tastes and the prices of other goods constant — a rise in a good's price reduces the quantity demanded. By changing one variable at a time, economists isolate relationships that would otherwise be obscured by simultaneous changes in many factors. Alfred Marshall popularised the device of partial equilibrium analysis built on ceteris paribus assumptions, famously likening disturbing factors to forces kept "in a pound" for later release.

Use in science and philosophy

In the experimental sciences, control of variables is the practical counterpart of a ceteris paribus clause. In philosophy, ceteris paribus laws are generalisations that hold only under normal or unspecified conditions — characteristic of the special sciences such as biology and psychology — and their logical status (whether they merely hedge against exceptions or express genuine but defeasible laws) is much debated. Critics note that without specification of the excluded factors, such clauses can render claims unfalsifiable.

Related expressions

The complementary phrase mutatis mutandis ("with the necessary changes made") signals that a comparison applies once relevant differences are taken into account. In econometrics and causal inference, holding other factors constant is approximated by statistical control and regression analysis.
