Brocard (law)

A brocard is a legal principle expressed in Latin, traditionally a short, pithy maxim that states a general rule of law. Brocards were widely used in medieval and early modern European jurisprudence, and many survive in modern legal usage as shorthand expressions of doctrines in common law and civil law systems.

Origin of the term

The word is traditionally said to derive from Burchard of Worms (died 1025), bishop and canon lawyer whose collection of canon law, the Decretum, was known as the Brocardus or Brucardica lex. Later generations of jurists, the glossators and commentators on Roman law, distilled the Corpus Juris Civilis into numbered regulae iuris — rules of law — of which the brocards are the popular descendants.

Examples

  • Nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege — no crime, no punishment without law.
  • Audi alteram partem — hear the other side.
  • Nemo dat quod non habet — no one gives what they do not have.
  • Salus populi suprema lex — the welfare of the people is the highest law.
  • In pari delicto potior est conditio defendentis — where both parties are at fault, the defendant's position is the stronger.
  • Volenti non fit iniuria — no injury is done to one who consents.

Use

Brocards were mnemonic devices for students and advocates and frequently framed judicial reasoning. Although modern courts cite them sparingly, they remain part of the vocabulary of legal education, and some — such as stare decisis and res ipsa loquitur — name living doctrines.
