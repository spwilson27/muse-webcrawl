Seismic wave

A seismic wave is a mechanical wave of acoustic energy that travels through the Earth or another planetary body. It can result from an earthquake (or generally, a quake), volcanic eruption, magma movement, a large landslide, and large man-made explosions that give out low-frequency acoustic energy. Seismic waves are studied by seismologists, who record the waves using seismometers, hydrophones (in water), or accelerometers.

Types

Body waves travel through the interior of the Earth. Primary waves (P-waves) are compressional, longitudinal waves — the fastest seismic waves, travelling at roughly 5–8 km/s in the crust — and can pass through solids, liquids, and gases. Secondary waves (S-waves) are shear waves with transverse motion; they are slower than P-waves and cannot propagate through liquids or gases, a fact that revealed the liquid nature of Earth's outer core.

Surface waves travel along the Earth's surface and are typically the most destructive in earthquakes. Rayleigh waves roll the ground in an elliptical motion, while Love waves shake it horizontally from side to side. Surface waves travel more slowly than body waves but have larger amplitudes and longer durations at teleseismic distances.

Propagation and use

Seismic waves are refracted and reflected at boundaries between materials of differing density and elasticity, following principles analogous to optics. By analysing arrival times and waveforms recorded worldwide, seismologists image the Earth's internal structure — crust, mantle, liquid outer core, and solid inner core — and locate and characterise earthquakes. Controlled sources of seismic waves underpin exploration geophysics for oil, gas, minerals, and groundwater.
