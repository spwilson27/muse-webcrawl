Richmond Castle

Richmond Castle in Richmond, North Yorkshire, England, is one of the earliest Norman stone castles in Britain. It was founded in 1071 by the Breton noble Alan Rufus on land granted by William the Conqueror, overlooking a crossing of the River Swale. Its well-preserved keep, built in the late 12th century, dominates the town, and its great courtyard is among the most complete Norman castle enclosures in the country.

History

The castle formed the centre of the Honour of Richmond, a great feudal lordship. The original curtain walls and the Scolland's Hall — one of the oldest great halls in England — date from the 11th century; the great keep was added around 1170–1186, probably under Henry II. Ownership passed between the dukes of Brittany, the Crown, and the Nevilles and Dukes of Richmond.

Later use

By the 16th century the castle was ruinous, though it remained a picturesque landmark painted by Turner and others. In the 19th century it became headquarters of the North York Militia. During the First World War it held the "Richmond Sixteen," conscientious objectors who were taken to France in 1916 and sentenced to death (commuted to hard labour) for refusing orders. The cells preserve their pencil graffiti.

Today

The castle is managed by English Heritage and is a scheduled monument and Grade I listed building, with a visitor centre and the peaceful Cockpit Garden within its walls.
