Wrought iron is an iron alloy with a very low carbon content (less than 0.08%) in contrast to that of cast iron (2.1% to 4.5%). It is a semi-fused mass of iron with fibrous slag inclusions (up to 2% by weight), which give it a wood-like "grain" that is visible when it is etched, rusted, or bent beyond its ultimate tensile strength. Wrought iron is tough, malleable, ductile, corrosion resistant, and easily forge welded, but is more difficult to weld electrically.

The name comes from the past participle of the verb "to work": it is iron that has been wrought—worked by hammering. Historically, it was produced by smelting iron ore in a bloomery, and later in a finery forge or puddling furnace, processes that never fully melted the metal. The resulting bloom or puddled ball was hammered (shingled) to expel slag and consolidate the iron, then rolled into bar iron.

History

Wrought iron was the standard form of malleable iron for millennia, used for tools, weapons, horseshoes, and hardware. Industrial production expanded with Henry Cort's puddling process (1784), which allowed pig iron to be decarburised using coal fuel. Wrought iron became a major structural material in the 19th century: it was used for rails, ships' hulls, bridges, and the Eiffel Tower (built of puddled iron). Production peaked in the 1860s–70s and then declined as cheap mild steel from the Bessemer and open-hearth processes displaced it.

Properties and uses

The slag fibres in wrought iron inhibit crack propagation and give it notable resistance to fatigue and corrosion compared with mild steel. It cannot be hardened by heat treatment because of its low carbon content. Because genuine wrought iron is no longer widely produced commercially, most decorative "wrought iron" work today—gates, railings, furniture—is actually made from mild steel. Small quantities of genuine material are still produced for heritage restoration work, notably in the United Kingdom.
