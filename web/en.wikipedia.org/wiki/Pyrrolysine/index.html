Pyrrolysine

Pyrrolysine (symbol Pyl or O) is an amino acid used by some methanogenic archaea and at least one bacterium in enzymes that are part of their methane-producing metabolism. It is encoded in mRNA by the UAG codon, which in most organisms is a stop codon. Pyrrolysine is often called the 22nd proteinogenic amino acid, after selenocysteine, the 21st.

Structurally, pyrrolysine is similar to lysine, but with an added pyrroline ring linked to the terminal nitrogen of the lysine side chain by an amide bond. Specifically it is N6-[(2R,3R)-3-methyl-3,4-dihydro-2H-pyrrol-2-ylcarbonyl]-L-lysine.

Discovery

Pyrrolysine was discovered in 2002 by researchers at Ohio State University studying the archaeon Methanosarcina barkeri. The group found that the gene for a methylamine methyltransferase contained an in-frame UAG codon that was translated rather than terminating the protein, and that the resulting residue was a modified lysine bearing a pyrroline ring.

Genetic encoding

Unlike the standard amino acids, pyrrolysine is not charged directly onto its tRNA by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase in its final form in all organisms; in many organisms it is synthesized on the tRNA from a precursor. The dedicated tRNA (tRNAPyl, with anticodon CUA) is encoded by the pylT gene, and the synthetase PylS charges it with pyrrolysine synthesized by the products of the pylB, pylC, and pylD genes. Organisms with this system can translate UAG as pyrrolysine when the appropriate context is present.

Occurrence

The pyrrolysine system is found in methanogenic archaea of the order Methanosarcinales and in a number of other archaea and bacteria that use methylamines. Enzymes containing pyrrolysine include the methylamine methyltransferases involved in methanogenesis from methylamine substrates.
