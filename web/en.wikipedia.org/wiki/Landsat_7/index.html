Landsat 7 is the seventh satellite of the Landsat program, launched on 15 April 1999. Its primary goal is to refresh the global archive of satellite imagery of Earth, providing up-to-date and cloud-free images. The satellite is managed by NASA and the United States Geological Survey (USGS). Its sole instrument, the Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus (ETM+), improved on earlier sensors with a 15-metre panchromatic band and enhanced thermal resolution.

Instrument

The ETM+ is a whisk-broom scanner recording eight spectral bands: visible and near- and shortwave-infrared bands at 30 m resolution, a thermal band at 60 m, and a panchromatic band at 15 m. Each scene covers a swath 185 km wide. The satellite orbits at about 705 km in a sun-synchronous orbit, imaging the whole Earth every 16 days.

Scan line corrector failure

On 31 May 2003, the scan line corrector (SLC) of the ETM+ failed permanently. The SLC compensated for the forward motion of the spacecraft; without it, images are acquired in a zigzag pattern with wedge-shaped gaps, losing up to about 22% of each scene, particularly toward the edges. "SLC-off" imagery nevertheless remains scientifically valuable, and products are often gap-filled using images from other dates or from Landsat 8 and 9.

Later operations and retirement

Landsat 7 far exceeded its five-year design life. In 2021–2022 its orbit was lowered as Landsat 9 assumed its role in the operational constellation, significantly reducing Landsat 7's imaging capacity; science acquisition ended in January 2024, and the mission moved toward final decommissioning as its orbit decays. Together with the rest of the Landsat archive — made freely available by the USGS from 2008 — Landsat 7's two decades of data form a cornerstone of land-use and environmental monitoring.
