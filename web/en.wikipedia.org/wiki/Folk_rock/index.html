Folk rock is a hybrid music genre that combines the elements of folk music with rock music. The genre arose in the United States around the mid-1960s, when folk musicians began incorporating electric instrumentation, prominent harmony vocals, and the rhythms of rock and roll into songs drawn from, or in the style of, the folk revival.

The term is usually credited as having been coined in June 1965, when American music press used it to describe the sound of the Byrds, whose debut album Mr. Tambourine Man — featuring a rock arrangement of a Bob Dylan song — topped the charts that year. Dylan's own electrified performance at the Newport Folk Festival in July 1965 became one of the most famous and controversial moments in popular music history.

History

British groups such as the Animals and the Searchers had already scored hits with electrified versions of folk material, while American folk-revival groups like the Kingston Trio and Peter, Paul and Mary had mainstreamed acoustic folk. The Byrds fused Dylan's material with a Beatles-influenced beat and Roger McGuinn's jangling twelve-string Rickenbacker guitar, creating a template followed by acts including the Lovin' Spoonful, the Mamas & the Papas, Buffalo Springfield, and Simon & Garfunkel.

In Britain, folk rock developed along somewhat different lines through Fairport Convention, Pentangle, and Steeleye Span, who electrified traditional British folk songs. Fairport's 1969 album Liege & Lief is widely regarded as the founding document of British folk rock.

Characteristics

Folk rock typically features literate, socially conscious lyrics; clear vocal harmonies; and a mixture of acoustic and electric instrumentation. Songs frequently drew on traditional ballads, protest themes, and the singer-songwriter tradition.

Legacy

The genre laid groundwork for country rock (the Flying Burrito Brothers, Eagles), the singer-songwriter movement of the 1970s, and later revivals such as folk punk and 21st-century indie folk acts including Mumford & Sons and Fleet Foxes.
