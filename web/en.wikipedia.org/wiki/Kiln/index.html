Kiln

A kiln is a thermally insulated chamber, a type of oven, that produces temperatures sufficient to complete a process such as hardening, drying or chemical change. Kilns have been used for millennia to fire ceramics and pottery, and are also essential for making brick, lime, cement and charcoal, for calcining ores, and for cremation and incineration. Firing temperatures range from a few hundred degrees Celsius in drying kilns to over 1,400 °C in cement and high-fired porcelain kilns.

Principle

A kiln's controlled heating transforms its contents irreversibly: clay minerals lose chemically bound water and fuse (vitrify) into ceramic; limestone (calcium carbonate) decomposes into quicklime and carbon dioxide; cement raw meal clinkers at about 1,450 °C. The atmosphere inside — oxidising or reducing — strongly affects results, notably the colour of pottery and glazes.

Types

  • Intermittent kilns are loaded, fired, cooled and unloaded in batches; they include traditional updraft and downdraft kilns, bottle kilns and Japanese climbing kilns such as the noborigama and single-chamber anagama.
  • Continuous kilns keep a steady temperature profile while ware moves through, as in tunnel kilns used by modern brick and sanitaryware factories.
  • Rotary kilns — long, slowly rotating cylinders — are used for cement, lime and ore processing.
  • Electric kilns dominate studio pottery and hobby ceramics for their cleanliness and control.

History

Open firing and pit kilns predate recorded history; enclosed kilns appeared in the Neolithic and were refined by Egyptian, Greek, Roman and Chinese potters, whose high-temperature wood-fired kilns enabled stoneware and porcelain. The Industrial Revolution brought coal-fired bottle kilns and later gas and tunnel kilns.
