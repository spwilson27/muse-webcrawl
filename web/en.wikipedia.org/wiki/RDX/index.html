RDX

RDX (abbreviation of Research Department eXplosive or Royal Demolition eXplosive) is an organic compound with the formula (O2N2CH2)3. It is a white solid widely used as an explosive and as a component of plastic explosives. Chemically, it is classified as a nitramide; it is more energetic than TNT and was used extensively in the Second World War. Its systematic name is 1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazinane; it is also known as cyclonite and hexogen.

History

RDX was patented in 1898 by Georg Friedrich Henning in Germany, originally with proposed medical uses. Its explosive potential was recognized in the 1920s. During the Second World War, large-scale production processes were developed in Britain, Canada, and the United States, including the Bachmann process. RDX formed the explosive fill of many munitions and was the main ingredient, with plasticizers, of Composition C plastic explosives such as C-4.

Properties and production

RDX is a stable, powerful high explosive with a detonation velocity of about 8,750 m/s. It is produced by nitration of hexamine (in the traditional Woolwich process, with by-production issues) or by the Bachmann process using hexamine, nitric acid, ammonium nitrate, and acetic anhydride, which gives higher yields and also some HMX as a by-product.

Uses

Beyond military explosives and plastic explosives, RDX has been used in detonating cords, shaped charges, and as the base charge in detonators. It has also been used, controversially, as a rodenticide. RDX is toxic and persists in groundwater near training ranges, making its environmental remediation an active research topic.
