Pyrrhic victory

A Pyrrhic victory is a victory that inflicts such a devastating toll on the victor that it is tantamount to defeat. Such a victory negates any true sense of achievement or damages long-term progress.

Origin

The phrase originates with King Pyrrhus of Epirus, whose army suffered irreplaceable casualties in defeating the Romans at the battles of Heraclea (280 BCE) and Asculum (279 BCE) during the Pyrrhic War. Plutarch reports that after Asculum, Pyrrhus remarked, in effect, "If we are victorious in one more battle with the Romans, we shall be utterly ruined" — often paraphrased as "Another such victory and I am undone." Pyrrhus's officer corps and best troops, whom he could not replace, were destroyed even as he won the field.

Usage

The term is applied to battles such as Bunker Hill (1775) for the British, and more broadly to any costly success: a lawsuit won at ruinous expense, a political fight that destroys the winner's standing, or a military campaign that exhausts the victor's capacity to continue. It is related to, but distinct from, a hollow victory and the concept of winning the battle but losing the war.
