Robert Edward Lee (January 19, 1807 – October 12, 1870) was a Confederate general during the American Civil War, best known as the commander of the Army of Northern Virginia from 1862 until its surrender in 1865. A son of Revolutionary War officer Henry "Light Horse Harry" Lee, he graduated second in his class at West Point and served with distinction as an engineer and staff officer in the Mexican–American War.

Early career

Lee spent nearly three decades in the United States Army, building fortifications and serving as superintendent of West Point (1852–55). In 1859 he commanded the detachment that captured John Brown at Harpers Ferry. Although he opposed secession and called slavery a moral and political evil, when Virginia seceded in April 1861 he resigned his US commission, declining command of the Union field army, and offered his services to his home state.

Civil War

After early setbacks in western Virginia and coastal defence duties, Lee took command of the Army of Northern Virginia on 1 June 1862, driving McClellan from Richmond in the Seven Days Battles. His victories at Second Bull Run, Fredericksburg, and Chancellorsville — despite being outnumbered — made his reputation, offset by defeats in his invasions of the North at Antietam (1862) and Gettysburg (1863). In 1864–65 he resisted Grant's Overland Campaign and the Siege of Petersburg before surrendering at Appomattox Court House on 9 April 1865. In February 1865 he had been named general-in-chief of all Confederate armies.

Later life and legacy

After the war Lee became president of Washington College in Lexington, Virginia (now Washington and Lee University), urging reconciliation between North and South. He died in 1870. Celebrated in the Lost Cause tradition as a model of Southern honour, in the 21st century his legacy has been re-examined in light of his defence of a slaveholding society and his own slaveholding, leading to the removal of many public monuments to him.
