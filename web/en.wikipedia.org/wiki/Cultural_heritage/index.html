Cultural heritage is the heritage of tangible and intangible assets of a group or society inherited from past generations. Tangible heritage includes buildings, monuments, landscapes, books, works of art and artefacts; intangible heritage includes folklore, traditions, language, rituals, performing arts and knowledge; and natural heritage, though often classified separately, includes culturally significant landscapes and biodiversity. Cultural heritage is studied and protected under international and national law because it embodies identity, memory and meaning for communities.

Protection

The modern protection regime dates from the aftermath of the Second World War. The 1954 Hague Convention protects cultural property in armed conflict — its distinctive Blue Shield emblem marks protected sites — and the 1970 UNESCO Convention targets illicit trafficking in antiquities. The 1972 World Heritage Convention, UNESCO's flagship programme, lists sites of "outstanding universal value" (more than 1,200), from the Pyramids of Giza to the Alhambra. The 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage extends similar listing to practices such as traditional craftsmanship, cuisine and festivals.

Issues

Heritage faces threats from war and deliberate destruction (the Taliban's demolition of the Bamiyan Buddhas in 2001, ISIS attacks on Palmyra), looting and the antiquities black market, development, tourism pressure and climate change. Repatriation disputes — over the Parthenon Marbles, the Benin Bronzes and Indigenous remains and artefacts — raise contested questions about colonial acquisition and rightful ownership. Scholars also debate "heritage" itself: whose past is preserved, how expert-led ("authorized") discourse may marginalise community meanings, and how heritage is mobilised for nationalism or tourism.
