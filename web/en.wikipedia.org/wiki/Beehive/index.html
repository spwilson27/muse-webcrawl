A beehive is an enclosed structure in which a colony of honey bees lives and raises its young. Natural beehives are nests that bees build in hollow trees and rock cavities; artificial beehives are constructed by beekeepers to house colonies for honey production, beeswax, and crop pollination. The location where hives are kept is called an apiary.

Natural nests

Wild honey bee colonies nest in cavities of roughly 40 litres, building parallel wax combs suspended from the top. A colony consists of a single queen, tens of thousands of female workers, and, seasonally, male drones. Bees regulate the nest temperature near 35 °C (95 °F) year-round.

Artificial hives

Traditional hives include skeps — inverted baskets of straw or wicker — whose harvest historically required destroying the colony, and hollow-log hives still used in parts of Africa and Europe. The breakthrough in modern beekeeping came with the discovery of "bee space" and the movable-frame hive patented by Lorenzo Lorraine Langstroth in 1852. The Langstroth hive — stacked boxes containing removable frames spaced so bees build comb within them without sealing the gaps — remains the world standard, allowing inspection and honey harvest without harming the colony. Alternative designs include the top-bar hive, in which bees build comb from single bars, and the Warré hive, designed to mimic a natural tree cavity.

Colony management

Beekeepers inspect hives for the queen's laying pattern, food stores, pests such as the Varroa mite, and diseases including American foulbrood. Honey is harvested by removing frames of capped honey, extracting it by centrifuge, and returning the empty comb to the bees. In commercial practice, hives are trucked between regions to provide pollination services for crops such as almonds.

Symbolism

The beehive is a longstanding symbol of industry and cooperation, appearing in heraldry, Freemasonry, and the iconography of Utah, whose nickname is the Beehive State.
