Monotropism

Monotropism is a cognitive theory and strategy of attention proposed as a central underlying feature of autism. A monotropic mind tends to focus its attention intensely on a small number of interests at any time, to the relative exclusion of others — sometimes described as an "attention tunnel". The theory was developed by autistic researchers Dinah Murray, Mike Lesser, and Wenn Lawson, and was first formally published in 2005 in the journal Autism.

Theory

Monotropism contrasts with polytropism, the tendency to distribute attention across many interests simultaneously. In the theory, attention is treated as a limited resource: monotropic individuals allocate it more narrowly and deeply. This is proposed to explain many autistic experiences, including intense "special interests", flow states, difficulty with unexpected transitions and multitasking, inertia in starting or stopping activities, and distress when attention is forcibly redirected.

Development and reception

Murray began developing the idea in the early 1990s, influenced by her work with autistic people and by the concept of attention in cognitive science. The theory has become influential within the autistic community and in neurodiversity-affirming practice, where it is often viewed as a positive, descriptive account of autistic cognition rather than a deficit model. Empirical research testing the theory, including questionnaire measures of monotropic tendencies, has grown since the 2010s, and monotropism has been discussed in relation to ADHD as well.

Implications

Applied monotropism suggests supporting autistic people by allowing sustained engagement with interests, giving warning before transitions, and reducing competing demands on attention — for example in education and workplace design.
