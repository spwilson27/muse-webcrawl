Cell nucleus

The cell nucleus (pl. nuclei) is a membrane-bound organelle found in eukaryotic cells that contains most of the cell's genetic material, organised as DNA complexed with histone proteins into chromatin. Its main functions are to maintain the integrity of the genome and to control the activities of the cell by regulating gene expression, including the transcription of messenger RNA and the assembly of ribosomal subunits. The nucleus was the first organelle to be discovered and described, by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek and drawn by Franz Bauer in 1804.

Structure

The nucleus is enclosed by the nuclear envelope, a double membrane continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum and pierced by nuclear pore complexes that regulate the transport of molecules between the nucleus and cytoplasm. Inside, the DNA is organised into linear chromosomes; the dense nucleolus is the site of ribosomal RNA synthesis and ribosome assembly. A protein scaffold called the nuclear lamina underlies the inner membrane and gives the nucleus mechanical support.

Function

The nucleus directs protein synthesis by controlling which genes are transcribed; it replicates its DNA during the S phase of the cell cycle and, during mitosis in most eukaryotes, the nuclear envelope breaks down so the chromosomes can segregate. Cells typically have one nucleus, though exceptions include multinucleate muscle fibres and anucleate mammalian red blood cells.
