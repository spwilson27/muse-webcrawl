Cross-cultural psychology

Cross-cultural psychology is the scientific study of human behavior and mental processes, including both their variability and invariance, under diverse cultural conditions. Through expanding research methodologies to recognize cultural variance in behavior, language, and meaning, it aims to extend and develop psychology.

Definition and scope

Since psychology as a field first emerged primarily in the United States, cross-cultural researchers have challenged the assumption that findings from Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic ("WEIRD") samples generalize to humanity as a whole. Cross-cultural psychology compares psychological phenomena across cultures, while the closely related field of cultural psychology examines how cultural practices shape psychological processes within a single culture.

History

The field grew substantially in the latter half of the 20th century. The Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology was founded in 1970, and the International Association for Cross-Cultural Psychology (IACCP) was established in 1972. Early influential work included studies of perception, such as research on susceptibility to optical illusions among different populations, and large-scale value surveys such as Geert Hofstede's cultural dimensions research.

Key topics

  • Individualism and collectivism — the degree to which people are integrated into groups.
  • Self-concept — independent versus interdependent construals of the self.
  • Cognition — analytic versus holistic thinking styles.
  • Emotion — cultural display rules and emotion recognition.
  • Development — parenting styles and child-rearing practices across societies.

Methods

Researchers must guard against ethnocentrism and measurement bias, and establish equivalence of constructs, methods and samples when comparing cultures. Common approaches include cross-national surveys, ethnographic observation, and experimental studies adapted for local contexts.
