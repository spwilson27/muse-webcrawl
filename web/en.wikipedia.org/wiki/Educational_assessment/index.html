Educational assessment

Educational assessment or educational evaluation is the systematic process of documenting and using empirical data on the knowledge, skill, attitudes, aptitude and beliefs of learners to refine educational programs and improve student learning. Assessment data can be obtained by examining student work directly or based on data criteria from an entire population of learners.

Overview

The term "assessment" is often used interchangeably with "test" or "measurement," but assessment encompasses a broader range of methods, including observations, portfolios, projects, interviews, and self-reports, as well as conventional examinations. Assessment serves several purposes: it can certify achievement, select students for further study or employment, diagnose difficulties, guide teaching, and evaluate the effectiveness of courses and institutions.

Types of assessment

Formative assessment takes place during instruction and is intended to give feedback that improves learning; examples include classroom questioning, draft essays with comments, and short quizzes. Summative assessment occurs at the end of a course or programme and sums up achievement, typically in a grade or pass/fail decision. Diagnostic assessment identifies a learner's strengths and weaknesses before or during teaching. Assessments may also be classified as criterion-referenced, where performance is judged against a fixed standard, or norm-referenced, where performance is compared with that of a reference group.

Quality criteria

Assessment specialists evaluate instruments using criteria such as validity (whether the assessment measures what it claims to measure), reliability (consistency of results), fairness, and practicality. Threats to validity include construct under-representation and construct-irrelevant variance, for example when a mathematics test inadvertently measures reading proficiency.

Standardised testing

Large-scale standardised tests, such as the SAT and ACT in the United States, the GCSE and A-level examinations in England, and international surveys such as PISA, TIMSS and PIRLS, play a major role in admissions, accountability, and cross-national comparison. Their use is debated: supporters argue they provide objective, comparable information, while critics contend they narrow the curriculum and disadvantage some groups of students.
