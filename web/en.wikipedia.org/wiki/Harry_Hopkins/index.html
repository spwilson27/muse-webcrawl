Harry Lloyd Hopkins (August 17, 1890 – January 29, 1946) was an American statesman and social worker who became one of President Franklin D. Roosevelt's closest advisers. He directed the great relief programmes of the New Deal, served as Secretary of Commerce, and during the Second World War acted as Roosevelt's personal emissary to Winston Churchill and Joseph Stalin, administering the Lend-Lease programme.

Early life

Hopkins was born in Sioux City, Iowa, and raised largely in Grinnell, where he graduated from Grinnell College in 1912. He moved to New York City and became a social worker, rising quickly in settlement-house and relief organisations; during the First World War he directed Red Cross work in the South. In the early 1930s he ran New York State's Temporary Emergency Relief Administration under Governor Roosevelt.

New Deal administrator

When Roosevelt became president in 1933, Hopkins came to Washington to head the Federal Emergency Relief Administration, then the short-lived Civil Works Administration, which put some four million people to work in a single winter. From 1935 he directed the Works Progress Administration (WPA), the largest New Deal work-relief agency, which employed millions on construction, arts and research projects. Critics in Congress attacked the WPA as wasteful and political; Hopkins defended it vigorously. He served as Secretary of Commerce from 1938 until ill health forced his resignation in 1940.

Wartime emissary

Despite chronic illness — he had lost much of his stomach to cancer surgery in 1937 and lived on a restricted diet — Hopkins moved into the White House in 1940 and became Roosevelt's indispensable lieutenant. In January 1941 he travelled to London to assure Churchill of American support, and in July 1941 he flew to Moscow to meet Stalin, reporting that the Soviet Union would hold out against Germany. As administrator of Lend-Lease he oversaw the flow of billions of dollars of aid to Britain, the USSR and other allies, and he attended the great wartime conferences at Tehran and Yalta.

Death and legacy

Hopkins died in New York on January 29, 1946, less than a year after Roosevelt. His closeness to the president and his role in aid to the Soviet Union made him a controversial figure in early Cold War debates, but historians generally regard him as one of the most effective administrators of his era.
