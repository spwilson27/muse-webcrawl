Rubin causal model

Potential outcomes under treatment and control

The Rubin causal model (RCM), also known as the Neyman–Rubin causal model, is an approach to the statistical analysis of cause and effect based on the framework of potential outcomes. It was developed by Donald Rubin, building on ideas first formalized by Jerzy Neyman in 1923.

Potential outcomes

For each unit, the framework posits two potential outcomes: the outcome that would occur if the unit received the treatment, and the outcome if it received the control. The causal effect is the difference between them. Because only one outcome is ever observed for a given unit, causal inference is framed as a missing-data problem — the "fundamental problem of causal inference".

Key assumptions

Identification typically requires the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA), requiring that one unit's treatment does not affect another's outcome, and unconfoundedness: treatment assignment is independent of the potential outcomes given observed covariates. Methods within the framework include randomization inference, matching, propensity score methods and stratification.

Influence

The Rubin causal model underlies much modern work in statistics, econometrics, epidemiology and political science, and stands alongside Judea Pearl's structural causal models as one of the two dominant frameworks for causal inference.
