Acts of Paul and Thecla

The Acts of Paul and Thecla (Acta Pauli et Theclae) is an early Christian apocryphal text, written in the second century AD, recounting the travels of the apostle Paul and the conversion and adventures of his follower Thecla, a young noblewoman of Iconium. It originally formed part of the larger Acts of Paul but circulated widely as an independent work.

Content

Hearing Paul preach on chastity from a neighboring window, Thecla abandons her fiancé Thamyris and follows the apostle. Condemned by the authorities, she is miraculously saved from burning at the stake and later from wild beasts in the arena at Antioch, where a lioness defends her and she baptizes herself in a ditch of water. Disguising herself in men's clothing, she rejoins Paul and is commissioned by him to teach. She settles in Seleucia, where tradition holds she lived in ascetic devotion into old age.

Authorship and reception

The church father Tertullian, writing around AD 200, reports that the work was composed by a presbyter in Asia Minor "out of love for Paul," and complains that some used Thecla's example to justify women teaching and baptizing. Despite such criticism, the text was enormously popular: Thecla became one of the most venerated female saints of late antiquity, styled "protomartyr among women" and "equal to the apostles," with pilgrimage shrines in Seleucia (in modern Turkey) and elsewhere, and her acts surviving in Greek, Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, and other versions.

Scholarly significance

The work is often compared to ancient Greek romances in its narrative structure and is a key source for debates about women's roles in early Christianity, asceticism, and the reception of Paul.
