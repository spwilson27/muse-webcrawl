The Labor Management Relations Act of 1947, commonly known as the Taft–Hartley Act, is a United States federal law that restricts the activities and power of labor unions. It was enacted on 23 June 1947 when Congress overrode President Harry S. Truman's veto.

Provisions

The Act amended the National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act) of 1935. It outlawed the closed shop, jurisdictional strikes and secondary boycotts; permitted states to enact "right-to-work" laws banning union shops; allowed the President to seek injunctions in strikes deemed national emergencies; and required union leaders to sign affidavits that they were not communists (a provision later struck down).

Legacy

Organised labour has repeatedly sought the Act's repeal, denouncing it as a "slave-labor bill"; Truman vetoed it with that criticism. It remains a central, controversial framework of US labour law.
