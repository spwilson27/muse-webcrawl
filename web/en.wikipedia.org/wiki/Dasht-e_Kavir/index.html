The Dasht-e Kavir (Persian: دشت کویر, lit. "Low Plains"), also known as the Great Salt Desert, is a large desert lying in the middle of the Iranian Plateau. It is about 800 kilometres (500 mi) long by 320 kilometres (200 mi) wide, with a total surface area of about 77,600 square kilometres (30,000 sq mi), making it one of the largest deserts in the world. It stretches from the Alborz mountain range in the northwest to the Dasht-e Lut in the southeast.

Geography and climate

The desert is named after the salt marshes (kavirs) located there. A kavir is a salt-encrusted depression in which rainwater collects briefly in winter and evaporates, leaving salt pans and dried mud flats patterned with polygonal cracks. The climate is hyper-arid, with almost no rainfall and summer temperatures among the highest recorded anywhere on Earth, sometimes approaching those of the neighbouring Dasht-e Lut.

Geology

The surface consists largely of Pliocene and Miocene sediments, salt domes, and clay pans. Beneath the salt crust, marshy ground can be treacherously soft, a hazard that historically made crossing the desert dangerous for caravans. Travel across the kavir was traditionally undertaken in winter, when the surface firmed, and along established routes between oasis towns.

Settlements and ecology

The desert is almost uninhabited in its interior, though towns such as Garmsar, Khur, Jandagh and Aradan sit on its fringes. Hardy wildlife, including the Asiatic cheetah and the Persian onager, survives in protected areas around the margins, and parts of the desert fall within Kavir National Park.
