Armand Jean du Plessis, 1st Duke of Richelieu (9 September 1585 – 4 December 1642), known as Cardinal Richelieu, was a French statesman and cleric who served as chief minister to King Louis XIII from 1624 until his death. Nicknamed l'Éminence rouge ("the Red Eminence") for the scarlet robes of a cardinal and his eminence in power, he is regarded as one of the founders of the modern French centralised state and a master practitioner of raison d'état — the subordination of all other considerations to the interests of the state.

Early life and rise

Richelieu was born in Paris to a family of the lesser nobility of Poitou; his father, François du Plessis, was provost of the household of Henry III. Destined first for a military career, Armand instead took holy orders to preserve the family's bishopric of Luçon, becoming — after a famous interview with Pope Paul V, to whom he understated his age — one of France's youngest bishops in 1607. He proved a serious reforming bishop, and his eloquence as deputy of the clergy at the Estates-General of 1614 brought him to court. He rose as a client of the queen mother, Marie de' Medici, becoming secretary of state in 1616, was exiled with her after the fall of her favourite Concini, negotiated her reconciliation with Louis XIII, and was made a cardinal in 1622. Appointed to the royal council in 1624, he quickly became the king's dominant minister — a partnership that, despite friction, lasted until his death.

Domestic policy

Richelieu's domestic programme had three aims: break the political and military power of the Huguenots, humble the great nobility, and make the king's authority absolute in France. He saw the Huguenots' fortified "state within the state," protected by the Edict of Nantes, as intolerable. After the great Siege of La Rochelle (1627–28) — which he directed in person, building a mole across the harbour to starve the city — the Peace of Alès (1629) stripped the Huguenots of their fortresses, armies and political assemblies while preserving their freedom of worship.

Against the nobility he was ruthless: he suppressed duelling, demolished unauthorised feudal castles, crushed a series of aristocratic conspiracies (those of Chalais, Montmorency and finally Cinq-Mars in 1642), and executed plotters of the highest rank. Administration was centralised through royal intendants sent into the provinces, bypassing local governors and parlements, while new taxes to fund war (notably heavy increases in the taille) provoked peasant revolts such as the Croquants and the Nu-Pieds, which were put down harshly.

Foreign policy and the Thirty Years' War

Abroad, Richelieu's goal was to break the encirclement of France by the Spanish and Austrian Habsburgs. Though a Catholic cardinal, he subsidised Protestant powers against them — the Dutch, the Danes, and above all Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden under the Treaty of Bärwalde (1631) — practising raison d'état above confessional solidarity. After Swedish power waned, France declared war directly on Spain in 1635 and intervened openly in the Thirty Years' War. The conflict outlasted him, but under his direction French armies checked Spanish power at Rocroi (won months after his death) and laid the foundations for French predominance confirmed at the Peace of Westphalia (1648) and the Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659).

Culture

Richelieu was a great patron and institution-builder of French culture. In 1635 he founded the Académie Française to regulate and elevate the French language. He rebuilt the Sorbonne (whose chapel holds his tomb), collected a great library and art collection at his Paris residence — the Palais-Cardinal, later the Palais-Royal — supported the theatre (including, ambivalently, Corneille), and ran an extensive propaganda and intelligence network; his newspaper La Gazette, founded by Théophraste Renaudot under his patronage, was France's first newspaper.

Death and legacy

Chronically ill for years (probably with tuberculosis and its complications, along with migraines and haemorrhoids), Richelieu died in Paris on 4 December 1642, recommending his protégé Jules Mazarin as his successor. Louis XIII survived him by only five months.

Richelieu's historical reputation has swung between demonisation — as the spider-like villain of Alexandre Dumas' The Three Musketeers (1844), conspiring with his agent Milady — and admiration as the far-sighted architect of France's rise to European dominance and of the modern centralised state. French warships, including an aircraft carrier planned but never completed, have borne his name, and his red robes remain an instantly recognised symbol of the fusion of church and state power.
