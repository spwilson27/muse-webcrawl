Isostasy

Isostasy (from Greek ísos "equal" and stásis "standstill") is the state of gravitational equilibrium between the Earth's crust and mantle such that the crust "floats" at an elevation that depends on its thickness and density. The concept, invoked to explain how different topographic heights can exist at the Earth's surface, was coined by the American geologist Clarence Dutton in 1889.

When a certain area of the lithosphere reaches the state of isostasy, it is said to be in isostatic equilibrium. Isostasy does not upset equilibrium but instead restores it — a process called isostatic adjustment. The Earth is generally in near-isostatic equilibrium, except in tectonically active regions.

Models

Three principal models are used:

  • Airy model — named after George Biddell Airy. The crust is assumed to have uniform density but variable thickness: higher mountains have deeper "roots" of crust projecting into the denser mantle, like icebergs in water.
  • Pratt model — named after John Henry Pratt. The depth of the crust–mantle boundary is uniform, but the density of crustal columns varies laterally: higher topography is underlain by lower-density rock.
  • Vening Meinesz (flexural) model — the lithosphere behaves as an elastic plate, and loads are supported regionally rather than purely locally.

Causes of isostatic movement

Changes in surface load disturb equilibrium. Erosion lightens a mountain range, causing uplift of the remaining mass; deposition of sediment in a basin causes subsidence. The growth and melting of ice sheets produce the largest effects: during the last glacial maximum, Scandinavia and Canada were depressed by hundreds of metres, and they are still rising today — Hudson Bay and the Gulf of Bothnia by up to about a centimetre per year — in post-glacial rebound. This ongoing adjustment also slowly changes the viscosity estimates for the mantle.

Effects

Isostatic depression in one place is commonly accompanied by forebulge uplift nearby, and the resulting changes in relative sea level are recorded in raised beaches and submerged forests. Isostatic anomalies — departures from equilibrium — are used in gravimetry to detect subsurface structure.
