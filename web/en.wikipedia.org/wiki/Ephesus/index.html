Ephesus was a city in ancient Greece and later a major Roman metropolis, on the coast of Ionia in present-day Turkey, about 3 km southwest of today's Selçuk. It was built in the 10th century BC on the site of the former Arzawan capital by Attic and Ionian Greek colonists. During the classical Greek era it was one of the twelve cities of the Ionian League, and under Rome it became the capital of the province of Asia and one of the largest cities of the empire, with a population estimated at 200,000 or more.

Temple of Artemis and landmarks

Ephesus was famous for the nearby Temple of Artemis (Artemision), completed around 550 BC and counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World; only foundations and fragments remain today. The ruins of Roman Ephesus are among the best preserved in the Mediterranean: the two-storey façade of the Library of Celsus (built c. 114–117 AD), the Great Theatre seating some 25,000, the marble Curetes Street, the Terrace Houses with their frescoes and mosaics, and the Odeon and Temple of Hadrian.

Christian history and decline

Ephesus is prominent in early Christianity: the Apostle Paul lived and preached there in the 50s AD and addressed his Epistle to the Ephesians to its church; tradition holds that the Apostle John and the Virgin Mary spent their last years nearby. The city hosted the Council of Ephesus in 431. The harbour progressively silted up from sediment carried by the Küçükmenderes River, and by the Byzantine and Ottoman periods the city was abandoned. Excavations, begun by Austrian archaeologists in the 19th century, continue today; the site was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2015.
