Li Jing, also known as the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King Li (Chinese: 托塔李天王), is a guardian deity in Chinese folk religion, Taoism, and Chinese Buddhism. He is a major character in the Ming-dynasty novels Fengshen Yanyi (Investiture of the Gods) and Journey to the West, in which he serves as a marshal of the heavenly armies of the Jade Emperor. He should not be confused with the historical Tang-dynasty general of the same name, on whom elements of the character drew.

Mythology

In Fengshen Yanyi, Li Jing is a general stationed at Chentang Pass and the father of three sons: Jinzha, Muzha, and the famously rebellious Nezha. His relationship with Nezha collapses after the boy kills the son of the Dragon King Ao Guang and later takes his own life to absolve his parents of the debt; when Nezha is reborn in a lotus body and seeks revenge, Li Jing is saved by the intervention of the Buddha (or, in some versions, the Taoist immortal Randeng Daoren), who gives him a golden pagoda with which to subdue his son — the origin of his title "Pagoda-Bearing." Thereafter he is depicted holding the miniature pagoda in his hand, able to trap demons within it.

In Journey to the West, Heavenly King Li commands celestial troops and is repeatedly dispatched against Sun Wukong, with Nezha fighting at his side; father and son, reconciled, form part of the heavenly order that the novel both celebrates and satirizes.

Origins and worship

The deity developed through the fusion of the Tang general Li Jing with the Buddhist guardian king Vaiśravaṇa, who is depicted in Indian and Central Asian art carrying a stupa or pagoda. As Buddhism spread into China, the pagoda attribute transferred to Li Jing, and his family drama was elaborated in storytelling and opera. Temples to the Heavenly King are found across China and in Chinese communities abroad, where he is venerated as a protector against demons and disorder.
