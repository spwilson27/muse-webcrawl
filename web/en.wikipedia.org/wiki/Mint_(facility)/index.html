Mint (facility)

A mint is an industrial facility which manufactures coins that can be used as currency. Mints are usually state-owned or state-licensed, since the right to coin money — seigniorage and monetary control — has historically been a prerogative of sovereigns. The word derives from the temple of Juno Moneta in Rome, where Roman silver was coined.

History

The earliest coins were struck in Lydia and the Greek cities in the 7th century BC, hammered by hand between engraved dies. Medieval mints proliferated across Europe under feudal lords and bishops. Mechanisation — screw presses, then steam-powered machinery pioneered at Matthew Boulton's Soho Mint and adopted by the Royal Mint — standardised coinage and made counterfeiting harder. Famous mints include the United States Mint (established 1792), the Royal Mint of the United Kingdom, the Monnaie de Paris, and the Perth Mint.

Process

Modern production involves melting and rolling metal into strips, blanking out discs, annealing, rimming, and striking the blanks between dies at high speed, followed by inspection and counting. Mints also produce bullion coins, commemorative issues, and medals, and some strike coinage under contract for foreign governments.
