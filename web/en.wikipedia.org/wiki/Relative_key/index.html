Relative key

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In music theory, the relative key of a major or minor key is the key that shares the same key signature. The relative major of a minor key lies a minor third above its tonic; the relative minor of a major key lies a minor third below its tonic, and is the sixth degree (submediant) of the major scale.

Examples

C major and A minor are relative keys: both have no sharps or flats in their key signatures, and A natural minor uses the same collection of pitches as C major. Likewise G major (one sharp) is relative to E minor, and F major (one flat) is relative to D minor. On the circle of fifths, each major key is conventionally printed outside the circle with its relative minor directly inside it.

Use

Because relative keys share all their notes, moving between them — a relative modulation — is among the smoothest changes of key available to composers, and is common in sonata form, popular song (the "four chords" progression moves between relative major and minor), and folk music. The relationship is distinguished from the parallel key, in which a major and minor key share the same tonic but different signatures.
