Future of Earth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The future of Earth will be determined by a range of factors: the gradually increasing luminosity of the Sun, the loss of heat from the Earth's core, perturbations by other bodies in the Solar System, plate tectonics, and surface biochemistry. On timescales of billions of years, the brightening Sun will end the oceans and all life, long before the Sun's red giant phase finally engulfs or incinerates the planet.

Earth in about five billion years: an arid world beneath the red giant Sun.

Climate and the biosphere

As solar luminosity rises roughly 10 per cent per billion years, the carbonate–silicate cycle will draw down atmospheric carbon dioxide in compensation. Within about 500–900 million years CO₂ should fall below the threshold for C3 photosynthesis, collapsing most terrestrial ecosystems; the last multicellular life is expected to disappear by about 1.5 billion years, leaving only microbes in refuges such as high mountains and polar or subsurface habitats before the oceans are lost in a runaway greenhouse.

Ultimate fate

About 7.6 billion years from now the Sun will become a red giant with a radius near or beyond Earth's present orbit. Models differ on whether tidal drag pulls the Earth in — most calculations suggest it will be engulfed despite losing mass and pushing its orbit outward — after which the Sun will shed its outer layers and settle as a white dwarf, leaving any surviving remnant a frozen, irradiated rock.
