86-DOS was an operating system developed by Seattle Computer Products (SCP) in 1980 for its 8086-based computer kits. Written by Tim Paterson, it was originally known as QDOS (Quick and Dirty Operating System). Microsoft purchased 86-DOS in 1981, adapted it for the IBM Personal Computer, and it became the foundation of PC DOS and MS-DOS.

86-DOS was written for Seattle Computer Products' 8086 kits.

Development

Paterson designed 86-DOS as a 16-bit counterpart to Digital Research's popular 8-bit CP/M, so that CP/M software could be translated to it easily; its API deliberately mirrored CP/M's function calls, and even its file system entry layout was similar, though 86-DOS used the new FAT12 file allocation table format. The first version was completed in roughly six weeks in mid-1980.

Sale to Microsoft

Microsoft first licensed 86-DOS in December 1980 and purchased all rights in July 1981 for a total of about $75,000, hiring Paterson in the process. IBM shipped it as PC DOS 1.0 with the IBM PC in August 1981; Microsoft's retention of the right to license it to other manufacturers, as MS-DOS, became one of the most consequential deals in computing history.
