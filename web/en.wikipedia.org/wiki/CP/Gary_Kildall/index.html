Gary Arlen Kildall (May 19, 1942 – July 11, 1994) was an American computer scientist and microcomputer entrepreneur. In 1974 he created CP/M, the first commercially successful operating system for microcomputers, and founded Digital Research to sell it. He is recognized as a pioneer of the personal computer revolution, having also introduced the concept of the BIOS as a hardware-abstraction layer.

Kildall created CP/M in 1974 while teaching at the Naval Postgraduate School.

Career

Kildall earned a PhD in computer science from the University of Washington in 1972 and taught at the Naval Postgraduate School in Monterey. While consulting for Intel, he wrote PL/M and then CP/M (Control Program for Microcomputers), which he and his wife Dorothy licensed to manufacturers through Digital Research; by the early 1980s it ran on hundreds of machine models and supported a vast software library.

The IBM episode

In 1980 IBM approached Digital Research about licensing CP/M-86 for its forthcoming personal computer. The negotiations failed — legend, denied by those present, holds that Kildall was out flying his plane — and IBM instead adopted Microsoft's PC DOS, an adaptation of 86-DOS whose API closely followed CP/M's. Kildall later obtained an agreement for IBM to offer CP/M-86 alongside PC DOS, but at a much higher price it sold poorly. The episode made Kildall a famous "what might have been" figure in computing history.

Kildall went on to co-host the PBS programme Computer Chronicles, and pursued work in CD-ROM knowledge systems. He died in 1994 at age 52.
