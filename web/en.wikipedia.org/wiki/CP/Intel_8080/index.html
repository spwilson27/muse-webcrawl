Intel 8080 in CP/M Systems

The Intel 8080, released in April 1974, was the microprocessor around which the first generation of CP/M computers was built. CP/M was originally written in PL/M and assembly language specifically for the 8080, and the operating system's conventions — such as loading transient programs at address 0100h — were dictated by the 8080's memory model.

Role in the CP/M ecosystem

Machines such as the MITS Altair 8800, the IMSAI 8080, and countless S-100 bus systems used the 8080 as their CPU. Because CP/M abstracted hardware differences through the BIOS, software written for one 8080 machine generally ran on another, creating the first large market for portable microcomputer software.

Technical summary

The 8080 is an 8-bit processor with a 16-bit address bus (64 KB of addressable memory), seven 8-bit registers plus an accumulator, and a clock speed of 2 MHz in its original form. It required three supply voltages (+5 V, −5 V, +12 V) and auxiliary support chips for clock generation and bus control — shortcomings its successor, the single-supply 8085, addressed.

Successors

The Zilog Z80, designed by former Intel engineers, was instruction-set compatible with the 8080 and became the most popular CPU for CP/M systems, while Intel's own 8085 and 8086 lines moved in other directions. The 8080's instruction set also influenced the x86 architecture through the 8086's design goals.
