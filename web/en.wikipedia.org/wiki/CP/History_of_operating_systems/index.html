History of Operating Systems: The CP/M Era

The history of operating systems on microcomputers is inseparable from CP/M (Control Program for Microcomputers), written by Gary Kildall of Digital Research in 1974. CP/M was one of the first operating systems to separate hardware-dependent code (the BIOS) from the machine-independent core (the BDOS and console command processor), allowing the same software to run on many different machines built around the Intel 8080 and later the Zilog Z80.

Origins

Kildall developed CP/M while consulting for Intel, originally as a tool to load and test PL/M programs on the Intellec-8 development system. When Intel showed little interest in marketing it, Kildall and his wife Dorothy founded Digital Research (originally Intergalactic Digital Research) to sell it directly. By the late 1970s CP/M had become the de facto standard operating system for business microcomputers.

Architecture and influence

CP/M's three-part structure — BIOS, BDOS, and CCP — and its simple file model influenced nearly every microcomputer operating system that followed. When IBM sought an operating system for its Personal Computer in 1980, the failure of negotiations with Digital Research led IBM to Microsoft, whose PC DOS (derived from Seattle Computer Products' 86-DOS, itself heavily inspired by CP/M) inherited many CP/M conventions, including 8.3 filenames and drive letters.

Legacy

Although CP/M was displaced by MS-DOS in the 1980s, its design decisions shaped personal computing for decades. Thousands of applications — word processors such as WordStar, spreadsheets, databases such as dBase, and programming languages — were written for it, forming one of the largest software libraries of the early microcomputer age.
