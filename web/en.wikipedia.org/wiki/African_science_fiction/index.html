African science fiction is a body of speculative fiction written by African authors and set, wholly or partly, in Africa or among African diasporas. It encompasses a range of styles and philosophies, including Afrofuturism and the more recent coinages Africanfuturism and Africanjujuism, coined by author Nnedi Okorafor to describe speculative fiction centred on African cultures, histories, and cosmologies rather than filtered through Western tropes.

History

Precursors include the 19th-century Yoruba writer Daniel O. Fagunwa's forest-of-a-thousand-daemons novels and Amos Tutuola's The Palm-Wine Drinkard (1952). Modern African science fiction took shape with authors such as Sony Lab'ou Tansi, Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's The Wizard of the Crow, and Nigerian novelist Tade Thompson's Rosewater trilogy. The 2012 anthology AfroSF: Science Fiction by African Writers, edited by Ivor Hartmann, was a landmark collection, followed by African Risen (2022), edited by Oghenechovwe Donald Ekpeki, Zelda Knight, and Sheree Renée Thomas.

Notable works

  • Nnedi Okorafor — Who Fears Death, Lagoon, Binti (Hugo and Nebula awards)
  • Tade Thompson — Rosewater (Arthur C. Clarke Award)
  • Namwali Serpell — The Old Drift
  • Tochi Onyebuchi — Riot Baby

Themes

African science fiction frequently engages with colonialism and its aftermath, resource extraction, biotechnology, artificial intelligence, and climate change, often reworking indigenous mythologies and cosmologies into futuristic settings. Critics note its tendency to decentralize the Western future and to imagine technological modernity on African terms.

Film and other media

Cinematic touchstones include Jean-Pierre Bekolo's Les Saignantes (2005), the Kenyan short Pumzi (2009) by Wanuri, and Marvel's Black Panther (2018), set in the fictional African nation of Wakanda — a film whose production design drew heavily on Africanfuturist aesthetics.
