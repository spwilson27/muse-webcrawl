United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1 (1953), is a landmark Supreme Court case that established the modern American state secrets privilege, a rule of evidence allowing the government to withhold information whose disclosure would endanger national security.

Background

On October 6, 1948, a United States Air Force B-29 Superfortress engaged in a secret mission to test electronic equipment crashed near Waycross, Georgia, killing nine of the thirteen men aboard, including three civilian engineers employed by RCA. The widows of the three engineers sued under the Federal Tort Claims Act and sought the official accident investigation report and statements of the surviving crew. The Air Force refused, claiming the documents contained military secrets.

Decision

The Court, in an opinion by Chief Justice Fred M. Vinson, held that the privilege had been properly invoked: where there is a "reasonable danger" that disclosure would expose military matters that should not be divulged in the interest of national security, the evidence is absolutely privileged, and a court need not inspect the documents in camera where the circumstances show a sufficient likelihood of harm. The claims were remanded, and the government settled.

Aftermath

When the accident report was declassified decades later and obtained by the families in 2000, it contained no obvious secrets but did reveal evidence of the Air Force's negligence, including known fire hazards in the B-29 fleet. The families sued alleging the government had committed fraud on the Supreme Court; the renewed litigation, culminating in Herring v. United States (3d Cir. 2005), failed to reopen the judgment. The case remains the leading authority on the state secrets privilege and is frequently invoked in litigation over surveillance and counterterrorism programs.
