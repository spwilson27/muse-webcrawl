Manchukuo (Japanese: 満州国, Manshūkoku; Chinese: 滿洲國) was a puppet state of the Empire of Japan in Northeast China and Inner Mongolia, existing from 1932 to 1945. Established after the Japanese conquest of Manchuria following the Mukden Incident of 1931, it was presented by Japan as a pan-Asian "paradise of the kingly way" while serving as an industrial base, a buffer against the Soviet Union, and a vehicle for Japanese settler colonisation.

Establishment

After the Kwantung Army seized Manchuria, Japan installed Puyi, the last Qing emperor, as chief executive in 1932, proclaiming the state's supposed independence. The League of Nations' Lytton Report condemned the invasion; Japan's rejection of the report and withdrawal from the League in 1933 made Manchukuo an international pariah, recognised only by a handful of Axis and client states.

Rule and economy

Manchukuo was ruled in form as a monarchy — Puyi was enthroned as emperor in 1934 — but in reality Japanese advisers controlled every ministry, and the Kwantung Army garrisoned the country. Japanese zaibatsu built a heavy-industrial economy around Anshan's iron and steel, Fushun's coal, and the South Manchuria Railway; millions of Chinese farmers were displaced for Japanese settlers. Unit 731 conducted biological-warfare experiments in Manchukuo, and forced labour was widespread.

End

The Soviet invasion of Manchuria in August 1945 destroyed the Kwantung Army in days; the Manchukuo army disintegrated, Puyi was captured by Soviet forces, and the state collapsed. Manchuria was returned to China, and Manchukuo remains a byword for Japan's aggressive imperialism, though debates continue about the extent of genuine collaboration by local elites.
