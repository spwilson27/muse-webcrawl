Cumulate rock

[Image: Layered cumulate gabbro from a layered intrusion, showing rhythmic modal layering]
Cumulate rock
TypeIgneous rock
FormationFractional crystallisation in magma chambers
ExamplesBushveld Complex, Stillwater Complex, Skaergaard intrusion

Cumulate rocks are igneous rocks formed by the accumulation of crystals that settle out of a magma, typically on the floor of a large magma chamber, during fractional crystallisation. The resulting rocks are characterised by distinct layering and by mineral compositions that record the order in which minerals crystallised from the melt.

Formation

As a magma cools, minerals crystallise in a sequence governed by Bowen's reaction series. Early-formed crystals such as olivine and pyroxene are denser than the remaining melt and sink, accumulating in layers at the base of the chamber; buoyant crystals such as plagioclase may float and accumulate near the roof. The residual liquid trapped between accumulated crystals is called the intercumulus liquid, and its proportion distinguishes adcumulates (very little trapped liquid) from orthocumulates (abundant trapped liquid).

Layered intrusions

Cumulates are best developed in large layered intrusions such as the Bushveld Complex of South Africa, the Stillwater Complex of Montana, and the Skaergaard intrusion of Greenland. Rhythmic layering in such bodies can repeat over hundreds of metres. These rocks are economically important: cumulate chromitite and magnetite layers host much of the world's chromium and vanadium, and the Bushveld's Merensky Reef is the world's richest source of platinum-group elements.

Terminology

The cumulate nomenclature proposed by Wager, Brown and Wadsworth (1960) names rocks after their cumulus minerals in order of abundance, e.g. olivine–chromite cumulate, with prefixes describing the nature of the intercumulus material.
