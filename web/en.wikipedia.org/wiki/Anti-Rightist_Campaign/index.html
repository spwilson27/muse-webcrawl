Anti-Rightist Campaign

The Anti-Rightist Campaign (simplified Chinese: 反右运动; traditional Chinese: 反右運動) in the People's Republic of China, which lasted from 1957 to roughly 1959, was a political campaign to purge alleged "rightists" within the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and the wider society. The campaign was launched by Mao Zedong in reaction to the Hundred Flowers Campaign, in which intellectuals and others had been encouraged to criticise the party and the government.

Background

During the Hundred Flowers Campaign of 1956–57, criticism of the CCP grew sharper than the leadership expected. In mid-1957 Mao reversed course, declaring that critics were "rightists" seeking to overthrow the party. An editorial in the People's Daily in June 1957 marked the turn.

The campaign

Work units across China were assigned quotas of "rightists" to identify. Those labelled were subjected to struggle sessions, demotion, dismissal, imprisonment or exile to labour camps and remote regions. Estimates of the number persecuted generally range from about 300,000 to 550,000 people, including prominent intellectuals such as the writer Ding Ling and the economist Ma Yinchu's associates; the physicist and others suffered long-lasting ruin.

Aftermath

The campaign silenced intellectual dissent for a generation and helped clear the way for the Great Leap Forward. After Mao's death, the Deng Xiaoping leadership rehabilitated nearly all of those labelled rightists in 1978–80, though a small number of prominent figures were pointedly excluded from rehabilitation.
