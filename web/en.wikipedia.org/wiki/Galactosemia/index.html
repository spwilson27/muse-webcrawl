Galactosemia

Galactosemia
SpecialtyMedical genetics, endocrinology
SymptomsFeeding difficulties, jaundice, hepatomegaly, cataracts, developmental delay
CausesDeficiency of galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (classic type) or related enzymes
InheritanceAutosomal recessive
TreatmentGalactose-restricted (lactose-free) diet
FrequencyAbout 1 in 30,000–60,000 newborns (varies by population)

Galactosemia is a rare genetic metabolic disorder that affects an individual's ability to metabolise the sugar galactose properly. The classic and most severe form results from deficiency of the enzyme galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (GALT), an enzyme of the Leloir pathway encoded by the GALT gene on chromosome 9. Rarer forms involve deficiencies of galactokinase (GALK1) or UDP-galactose-4-epimerase (GALE).

Signs and symptoms

Affected infants appear normal at birth but, after ingesting breast milk or lactose-containing formula, develop poor feeding, vomiting, jaundice, hepatomegaly, and lethargy; without treatment they may progress to liver failure, sepsis (classically with E. coli), cataracts, and death. Galactokinase deficiency chiefly causes cataracts.

Diagnosis

Galactosemia is included in newborn screening programmes in many countries, measured by total galactose and GALT enzyme activity in dried blood spots, with confirmatory enzyme assay and genetic testing. It should be distinguished from galactose in the urine caused by other liver diseases.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment is immediate and lifelong restriction of dietary galactose — removal of milk and lactose-containing foods, using soy-based or elemental formulas in infancy. Early treatment prevents the acute neonatal illness and cataracts, but many patients with classic galactosemia still develop long-term complications, including speech and learning difficulties, tremor and ataxia, and premature ovarian insufficiency in most women, probably because the body produces galactose endogenously.
