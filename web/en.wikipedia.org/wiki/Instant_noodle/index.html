Instant noodles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A block of instant ramen noodles in a bowl with seasoning being added]

Instant noodles are pre-cooked, dried (usually flash-fried) noodle blocks sold with seasoning powder or sauce, rehydrated in hot water in about three minutes. They were invented in 1958 in Osaka, Japan, by Momofuku Ando, founder of Nissin Foods, who — after watching his wife fry tempura — flash-fried cooked noodles to dry them into a shelf-stable, instantly revivable form, and sold the result as Chicken Ramen. His Cup Noodles (1971), inspired by seeing Americans in California eat ramen broken into a cup with a fork, completed the format. The world now eats well over one hundred billion servings a year.

A global staple

Instant noodles localised everywhere they landed: Indonesia's Indomie (1972) and its mi goreng became a national icon; South Korea's Shin Ramyun (1986) set the spicy standard; India's Maggi defined a generation's snack (surviving a 2015 food-safety ban and recall to reclaim its market); China's market is the largest by servings. The packets are dorm food, disaster relief, mountaineering rations and, in American prisons, a documented currency. Ando is commemorated by Cup Noodles museums in Ikeda and Yokohama — and by a packet of his Space Ramen, developed for astronaut Soichi Noguchi's 2005 shuttle flight.

Criticisms

The standard block is high in salt and refined carbohydrate and low in fibre and micronutrients; public-health studies in heavy-consumption countries link frequent eating to poorer diet quality overall. Defenders note the food's role in food security and price stability, and manufacturers increasingly fortify, air-dry and lower sodium. Few foods have been so thoroughly studied, scorned and eaten.
