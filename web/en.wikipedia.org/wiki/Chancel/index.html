Chancel

In church architecture, the chancel is the space around the altar, including the choir and the sanctuary, at the liturgical east end of a traditional Christian church building. It may terminate in an apse.

A chancel with altar, reredos and choir stalls, separated from the nave by a screen.

Overview

The chancel is reserved for the clergy and choir, and is usually separated from the nave, where the congregation sits, by a chancel arch, a rood screen or a communion rail. The word derives from the Latin cancelli, the lattice or railing that originally screened off the area. In larger churches the chancel may include choir stalls on either side, the bishop's throne (cathedra) in a cathedral, and sedilia for the officiating clergy.

History

As church buildings developed from the early Christian basilica, the area around the altar became increasingly distinct. In medieval English parish churches, responsibility for repairing the chancel fell on the rector (or lay impropriator) rather than the parishioners, a division of liability that persists in English law as "chancel repair liability." After the Reformation, many chancels were simplified, though the 19th-century Gothic Revival restored elaborate chancels influenced by the Oxford Movement.

Modern usage

In modern church design, especially after the liturgical reforms of the 20th century, the distinction between chancel and nave has often been softened, with altars brought forward into a crossing or nave sanctuary. In some traditions the term "sanctuary" is now used for the area the chancel once denoted.
