Hunting dog

A hunting dog is a canine that hunts with or for humans. There are several different types of hunting dog, developed for various tasks and purposes. The major categories include hounds, terriers, dachshunds, cur-type dogs, and gun dogs. Further subdivisions exist within gun dogs — pointers, retrievers, setters, spaniels, and water dogs — and among hounds, which are divided into sighthounds and scenthounds according to whether they hunt primarily by sight or by smell.

History

Dogs have assisted human hunters since prehistoric times. Selective breeding over thousands of years produced specialized types: fleet sighthounds such as the Greyhound and Saluki for coursing game in open country; scenthounds such as the Bloodhound and Beagle for trailing quarry by odour; terriers for going to ground after foxes and badgers; and, after the spread of firearms, gun dogs trained to locate, flush, and retrieve birds without damaging them. Many modern breeds derive from 19th-century British and continental European breeding programmes, when kennel clubs first codified breed standards.

Types

Hounds pursue mammals, either by scent (Beagle, Foxhound, Basset Hound) or by sight (Greyhound, Whippet, Irish Wolfhound). Gun dogs work with shooters of game birds: pointers and setters indicate the location of birds by freezing in a pointing stance; spaniels flush birds from cover; retrievers fetch shot birds from land or water. Terriers pursue quarry underground or in dense cover. Curs and feist-type dogs of North America tree game such as squirrels and raccoons. Dachshunds were developed to hunt badgers in their burrows.

Training and work

Hunting dogs are trained to respond to voice, whistle, and hand signals, to remain steady to shot, and to deliver game with a "soft mouth". Field trials and hunting tests evaluate these skills under standardized conditions. Many hunting dogs also serve as family companions, and breeds developed for hunting are among the most popular pets worldwide, including the Labrador Retriever.
