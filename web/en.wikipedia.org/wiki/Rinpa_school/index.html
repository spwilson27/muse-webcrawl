Rinpa school

Rinpa (also written Rimpa) is one of the major historical schools of Japanese painting, known for bold, highly decorative compositions, lavish use of gold and silver leaf, and motifs drawn from nature and classical literature. The school arose in Kyoto in the early 17th century and was revived in successive generations over the following centuries.

Origins

Rinpa was founded by Hon'ami Kōetsu, a connoisseur, calligrapher and craftsman, and Tawaraya Sōtatsu, a painter of folding screens and fans. The name "Rinpa" was coined later, combining the final syllable of the name of Ogata Kōrin (1658–1716), the school's most celebrated master, with the word for school. Kōrin and his brother Kenzan, a potter, brought the style to maturity; a century later the Edo painter Sakai Hōitsu revived and codified it, publishing reproductions of Kōrin's works.

Style and subjects

Rinpa works characteristically depict simplified, strongly stylised natural subjects — pine trees, waves, flowers, birds and cranes — set against gold-leaf grounds, often on folding screens (byōbu), fans, lacquerware, ceramics and kimono designs. The technique of tarashikomi, pooling paint or ink in wet layers to create mottled effects, is a hallmark. Themes frequently allude to the classical literature of the Heian court, such as The Tale of Genji and The Tales of Ise.
