Glass–Steagall legislation

The Glass–Steagall legislation refers to four provisions of the United States Banking Act of 1933 (sections 16, 20, 21 and 32) that separated commercial banking from investment banking. Named for their sponsors, Senator Carter Glass of Virginia and Representative Henry B. Steagall of Alabama, the provisions barred commercial banks from underwriting or dealing in most securities, prohibited investment banks from taking deposits, and forbade interlocking directorates between the two. A separate 1932 law, the Glass–Steagall Act of 1932, had earlier liberalised Federal Reserve collateral rules and is sometimes confused with the 1933 provisions.

Background and enactment

After the stock market crash of 1929 and the failure of thousands of banks, congressional investigations — notably the Pecora Commission hearings of 1932–34 — exposed abuses in which commercial banks had channelled depositors' funds into speculative securities affiliates. The Banking Act of 1933, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on 16 June 1933 as part of the New Deal, responded with both the Glass–Steagall separation provisions and the creation of federal deposit insurance through the FDIC (championed by Steagall).

Operation and erosion

For decades the Act defined the structure of American finance: commercial banks such as Chase National were forced to spin off securities operations (giving rise to firms such as Morgan Stanley, split from J.P. Morgan & Co. in 1935). From the 1960s onward, regulators and courts progressively widened the permitted activities of bank holding companies — allowing, for example, limited underwriting of municipal revenue bonds, commercial paper and later asset-backed and corporate securities under Section 20 subsidiaries, with the permitted share of revenue raised to 25% by 1997. The 1998 merger of Citicorp and Travelers Group, creating Citigroup, was approved in anticipation of legislative change.

Repeal and aftermath

The Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act of 1999 repealed sections 20 and 32, formally permitting affiliations between commercial banks, investment banks and insurers; sections 16 and 21 were left largely in place. After the financial crisis of 2007–2008, the repeal was widely debated: critics argued that the erosion of Glass–Steagall had enabled the growth of systemically dangerous universal banks, while others noted that the institutions at the centre of the crisis (such as Lehman Brothers and AIG) were not products of the repeal. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 responded instead with the Volcker Rule, restricting proprietary trading by banks. Proposals to enact a "21st-century Glass–Steagall" have been introduced repeatedly, and the separation principle was endorsed in the platforms of both major U.S. parties in 2016.
