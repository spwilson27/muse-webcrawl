Faience

Faience is a term used for two related glazed ceramic materials. In its original and widest sense it refers to Egyptian faience, a glazed non-clay ceramic of crushed quartz or sand with a bright blue-green alkaline glaze, produced from the Predynastic period of ancient Egypt onwards. In modern usage "faience" (French faïence, from the Italian city of Faenza) usually denotes tin-glazed earthenware, also known as maiolica or delftware.

Egyptian faience

Egyptian faience was used for beads, amulets, figurines, tiles, vessels and shabti figures. Its luminous turquoise-blue colour, achieved with copper compounds, was associated with life, fertility and rebirth, and the material was sometimes called tjehenet, "dazzling". Famous examples include the blue faience hippopotamus figurines of the Middle Kingdom.

Tin-glazed earthenware

The European faience tradition grew from Islamic and Italian maiolica: pottery coated with an opaque white tin glaze that provided a ground for painted decoration in blue, green, yellow and other colours. Major centres included Faenza, Delft in the Netherlands (Delftware), Rouen and Nevers in France, and various German and Scandinavian factories. It remained a major form of fine pottery from the 16th to the 18th century, before being largely displaced by creamware and porcelain.
