The Irish War of Independence (Irish: Cogadh na Saoirse), also known as the Anglo-Irish War, was a guerrilla war fought in Ireland from 1919 to 1921 between the Irish Republican Army (IRA) — the army of the Irish Republic proclaimed in 1916 and reasserted by Dáil Éireann in 1919 — and British forces: the British Army, the Royal Irish Constabulary (RIC), and its paramilitary auxiliaries, the Black and Tans and the Auxiliary Division.

Course of the war

The war is conventionally dated from the Soloheadbeg ambush of 21 January 1919, the same day the First Dáil met in Dublin. Under Michael Collins's intelligence network and local "flying columns", the IRA targeted the RIC to make British administration ungovernable, while the Dáil built a parallel state with courts and a national loan. British reprisals — including the burning of Cork and the Croke Park shootings on Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920 — drew international criticism. Martial law was imposed on much of Munster.

The Government of Ireland Act 1920 partitioned Ireland, creating Northern Ireland, whose opening was accompanied by sectarian violence in Belfast. Escalation in mid-1921 brought a truce on 11 July 1921.

Treaty and aftermath

Negotiations in London produced the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 6 December 1921, establishing the Irish Free State as a dominion. The Treaty split the nationalist movement, leading to the Irish Civil War (1922–23), while Northern Ireland opted out and remained in the United Kingdom — reversing, for most of Ireland, the union created by the Acts of Union 1800. Around 2,300 people died in the conflict, including some 900 civilians.
