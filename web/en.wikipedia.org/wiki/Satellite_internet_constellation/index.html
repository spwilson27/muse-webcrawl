Satellite internet constellation

A satellite internet constellation is a system of many artificial satellites working together to provide internet access, typically from low Earth orbit (LEO), where short signal paths give low latency comparable with terrestrial broadband. Unlike geostationary satellite internet, which suffers round-trip delays of about 600 ms, LEO constellations at altitudes of roughly 500–1,200 km achieve latencies of 25–50 ms but require hundreds or thousands of satellites so that at least one is always above the horizon for every user.

History

Early ventures of the 1990s — Teledesic, Iridium and Globalstar — largely failed commercially; Iridium and Globalstar survived as narrowband services after bankruptcy. The concept revived in the 2010s with cheaper launches and mass-produced satellites. SpaceX's Starlink began launching in 2019 and grew to several thousand satellites and millions of subscribers, becoming by far the largest constellation ever flown. Other systems include OneWeb (about 630 satellites, focused on enterprise and government), Amazon's Project Kuiper, and China's planned Guowang and Thousand Sails constellations.

Technology

User terminals employ electronically steered phased-array antennas that track satellites passing overhead. Satellites route traffic either down to ground stations ("bent pipe") or across optical inter-satellite laser links. Constellations raise concerns about orbital debris, collision risk and the brightening of the night sky for astronomy, prompting regulation and design mitigations.
