Satellite dish

A satellite dish is a dish-shaped type of parabolic antenna designed to receive or transmit information by radio waves to or from a communication satellite. The term most commonly means a dish which receives direct-broadcast satellite television from a direct broadcast satellite in geostationary orbit. Dishes are also used for satellite Internet services such as those provided by satellite Internet constellations, for radio astronomy, and for deep-space communications.

Satellite dish
Type: Parabolic antenna
Invented: 20th century
Uses: Television reception, data, radio astronomy

Principle of operation

The parabolic shape of the dish reflects radio signals arriving from the satellite onto a single focal point in front of the dish, where a feedhorn and low-noise block downconverter (LNB) collect and amplify them. Conversely, when transmitting, signals fed at the focal point are reflected outward in a narrow, parallel beam. The larger the dish diameter relative to the wavelength, the narrower the beam and the higher the gain.

History

Parabolic antennas were pioneered in the 1880s by Heinrich Hertz and developed for radar during the Second World War. Large dishes were built for the first satellite ground stations in the 1960s, such as the Goonhilly Satellite Earth Station in Cornwall. In the late 1970s and 1980s, hobbyists in North America installed large C-band dishes (often 3 metres across) to receive satellite television, giving rise to the "big ugly dish" era. The launch of direct-broadcast satellites in the 1990s, using the higher-frequency Ku band, shrank consumer dishes to 45–90 cm.

Types

Prime-focus dishes place the feed at the focal point supported by legs; offset dishes use a section of a larger paraboloid so the feed arm does not block the aperture. Flat phased-array panels, used by modern LEO services such as Starlink, are electronically steered antennas rather than true dishes, though they serve the same purpose.

Uses

Beyond television and Internet access, satellite dishes are used in very-small-aperture terminal (VSAT) networks for businesses, in satellite news gathering, in amateur radio moonbounce (EME) communication, and in the large radio telescopes of the Deep Space Network.
