The lean startup is a methodology for developing businesses and products that aims to shorten product development cycles by combining business-hypothesis-driven experimentation, iterative product releases, and validated learning. Popularised by Eric Ries in his 2011 book The Lean Startup, it draws on lean manufacturing and on Steve Blank's "customer development" ideas.

Principles

Ries treats a startup's plan as a set of untested hypotheses. Rather than building a complete product from a fixed business plan, the team builds a minimum viable product (MVP) — the smallest version that allows a full turn of the Build–Measure–Learn loop — measures real customer behaviour with "innovation accounting," and uses the results either to persevere or to pivot: a structured change of strategy while retaining what has been learned. Other principles include small batches, continuous deployment (in software), split testing, and actionable ("vanity-free") metrics.

Adoption and criticism

The method spread widely through technology startups and was adopted in large companies and government (e.g. "lean" innovation programmes). Critics argue that the MVP is often misunderstood as an excuse for low quality, that pivoting can become aimless, and that the framework fits software better than deep tech or regulated industries. It remains a standard vocabulary of entrepreneurship education.
