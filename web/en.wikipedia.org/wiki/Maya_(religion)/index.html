Maya (religion)

Maya (Sanskrit: माया, māyā) is a concept in Indian religions, especially Hinduism, Jainism, Buddhism, and Sikhism. The word carries a range of meanings — "illusion," "magic," "appearance," and the creative power by which the phenomenal world is manifested — often summarized as the veil that conceals ultimate reality and makes the world appear as it does.

In Hinduism

In the Vedas, maya originally denoted the wondrous power by which gods, especially Indra and Varuna, produced forms and transformations. In the Upanishads and later philosophy it came to signify the power that projects the world of multiplicity. In Advaita Vedanta, systematized by Adi Shankara, maya (closely tied to avidya, ignorance) is the beginningless superimposition that makes the individual self mistake the transient world for reality and fail to recognize its identity with Brahman; it is described as neither real nor unreal (anirvacaniya). By contrast, theistic schools such as Vishishtadvaita and the Bhakti traditions regard maya as God's real creative energy (shakti), and in Shaktism the goddess herself is Mahamaya.

In Buddhism and Jainism

In early Buddhism, Maya is also the personal name of the Buddha's mother, Queen Maya. As a concept, related notions of illusion and deceptive appearance appear in Mahayana thought, where the world is likened to a dream, a mirage, or a magician's trick — similes used to teach emptiness (shunyata). In Jainism, maya denotes deceit, one of the four passions (kashaya) that bind karma to the soul and must be overcome on the path to liberation.
