Spinning (textiles)

Spinning is a textile manufacturing process in which fibres—such as cotton, wool, flax, or synthetic polymers—are drawn out and twisted together to form yarn or thread. The twisting binds the fibres together through friction, giving the yarn strength. Spinning is one of the oldest human technologies and remains a fundamental stage of textile production, preceding weaving and knitting.

History

Spinning originated in the Stone Age, when fibres were twisted by rolling them against the thigh or with a hand-held spindle weighted by a whorl. The spindle and distaff remained the standard tools for millennia. The spinning wheel, probably invented in India or China and reaching Europe by the late Middle Ages, greatly increased productivity. In the 18th century, mechanised spinning—the spinning jenny (1764), Richard Arkwright's water frame (1769), and Samuel Crompton's spinning mule (1779)—was a driving force of the Industrial Revolution, shifting textile production into factories.

Process

Before spinning, fibres are typically cleaned, carded to disentangle and align them, and combed to remove short fibres. The prepared fibre supply, called roving, is then drawn out (drafted) to the desired thinness while twist is inserted. In ring spinning, the dominant modern method for staple fibres, the yarn is wound onto a bobbin through a rotating ring and traveller. Rotor (open-end) spinning and air-jet spinning offer faster production for coarser yarns. Synthetic filaments may instead be extruded through spinnerets in melt, dry, or wet spinning.

Types of spinning

  • Ring spinning — produces strong, fine yarns; widely used for cotton and blends.
  • Rotor spinning — high-speed open-end method for shorter staple fibres.
  • Mule spinning — historically produced very fine yarns; now niche.
  • Hand spinning — continued by craft spinners using wheels and drop spindles.
