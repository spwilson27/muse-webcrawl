Photodetector

Photodetectors are sensors of light or other electromagnetic energy that convert absorbed photons into an electrical signal. A typical photodetector contains a p–n junction that converts light into current through the photovoltaic or photoconductive effect. Photodetectors are used individually and in arrays in applications ranging from cameras and optical communications to scientific instruments, smoke detectors, and remote controls.

Types

Common types include: photodiodes, including PIN diodes and avalanche photodiodes with internal gain; phototransistors; photoresistors (light-dependent resistors) based on photoconductivity; photomultiplier tubes, extremely sensitive vacuum devices; charge-coupled devices (CCDs) and CMOS image sensors, which integrate millions of photodetectors into a camera; photovoltaic cells (solar cells); and thermal detectors such as bolometers and pyroelectric sensors, which respond to heating by absorbed radiation. Superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors achieve near-unity efficiency and are used in quantum optics.

Performance measures

Photodetectors are characterised by responsivity (electrical output per unit incident light), quantum efficiency (fraction of photons producing a carrier), spectral response, dark current and noise-equivalent power, detectivity, and temporal response or bandwidth. Choice of semiconductor — silicon for visible light, InGaAs or germanium for near-infrared, mercury cadmium telluride for mid-infrared — sets the detectable wavelength range through the band gap.

Applications

Photodetectors are integral to fibre-optic communications, barcode readers, medical imaging, astronomy, lidar, and safety devices. Arrays of photodetectors form the image sensors of digital cameras and telescopes.
