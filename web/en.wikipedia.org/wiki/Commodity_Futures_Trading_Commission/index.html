Commodity Futures Trading Commission

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) is an independent agency of the United States government created in 1974 that regulates the U.S. derivatives markets, including futures, swaps, and certain kinds of options.

History

Futures regulation began in the Department of Agriculture in the 1920s under the Grain Futures Act and the Commodity Exchange Act of 1936. Congress created the CFTC in the Commodity Futures Trading Commission Act of 1974 in response to volatile commodity prices. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 greatly expanded its authority to include the swaps market after the 2008 financial crisis.

Mission and functions

The CFTC aims to promote competitive and financially sound markets, protect market participants against fraud and manipulation, and foster open, competitive, and financially sound derivatives trading. It oversees exchanges such as the CME Group and Intercontinental Exchange, clearing organisations, swap dealers, and futures commission merchants.

Organisation

The Commission has five commissioners appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for five-year terms; no more than three may belong to the same political party. One is designated chair. Divisions include Clearing and Risk, Enforcement, Market Oversight, and Swap Dealer and Intermediary Oversight.

Notable actions

Enforcement cases have addressed spoofing, manipulation in energy and metals markets, and fraud in retail forex and digital-asset commodities. The CFTC shares jurisdiction over digital assets with the Securities and Exchange Commission.
