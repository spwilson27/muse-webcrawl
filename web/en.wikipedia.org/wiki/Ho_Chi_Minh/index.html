Ho Chi Minh

Hồ Chí Minh (19 May 1890 – 2 September 1969), born Nguyễn Sinh Cung, was a Vietnamese revolutionary and statesman. He founded the Viet Minh, proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Vietnam in 1945, and served as its prime minister (1945–1955) and president (1945–1969), leading North Vietnam through the First Indochina War and into the Vietnam War. Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City in his honour.

Hồ Chí Minh
Born: 19 May 1890, Kim Liên, Nghệ An
Died: 2 September 1969, Hanoi
Roles: President of North Vietnam, founder of Viet Minh and Indochinese Communist Party

Early life and exile

Born in French Indochina, he worked as a cook and seaman, living in the US, Britain and France. In Paris he co-founded the French Communist Party (1920) after reading Lenin on colonialism, wrote anti-colonial journalism as Nguyễn Ái Quốc, trained in Moscow, and organised Vietnamese revolutionaries in Canton and Hong Kong, founding the Indochinese Communist Party in 1930.

Revolution and First Indochina War

Returning in 1941, he led the Viet Minh and, after Japan's surrender, read the Declaration of Independence in Hanoi on 2 September 1945. War with France ended with Viet Minh victory at Điện Biên Phủ (1954); Geneva partitioned Vietnam, with Ho ruling the North, carrying out land reform and building a socialist state.

Vietnam War and death

Ho supported the National Liberation Front in the South and the Ho Chi Minh Trail supply route. Though increasingly symbolic as Lê Duẩn directed policy, he remained the revolution's icon until his death from heart failure in 1969, before unification in 1975–76. His embalmed body lies in the Hanoi mausoleum, contrary to his wish for cremation; his testament and thought (Hồ Chí Minh Thought) remain official ideology.
