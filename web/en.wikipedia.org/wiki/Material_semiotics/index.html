Material semiotics

Material semiotics is an approach in science and technology studies (STS) and anthropology that studies how meanings, identities and social arrangements are produced through relations between both signs and material things. It treats entities — people, objects, technologies, institutions — as effects of networks of relations rather than as pre-existing, self-contained units.

Origins

The term was developed notably by the sociologist and anthropologist John Law, building on actor–network theory (ANT) associated with Bruno Latour, Michel Callon and Madeleine Akrich. It borrows from semiotics the idea that things acquire meaning relationally, but extends this beyond language and discourse to materials: hence "material" semiotics.

Key concepts

  • Relationality: entities take their form and attributes from their relations with others.
  • Performativity: realities are enacted in practices rather than simply described or discovered; Annemarie Mol's work on the "body multiple" in medicine is a key example.
  • Heterogeneity: networks mix human and non-human elements — instruments, texts, bodies, buildings.
  • Multiplicity and ordering: different practices may enact different versions of the same object, coordinated (or not) into singular realities.

Applications

Material semiotics has been applied to studies of medicine and the body, farming and food, technology design, markets, development, and animal–human relations. It overlaps with feminist technoscience, posthumanism and new materialism.
