Classical Cepheid variable

The light curve of Delta Cephei, the prototype classical Cepheid, showing the characteristic rapid rise and slower decline in brightness.

Classical Cepheids are a type of Cepheid variable star. They are young, Population I variable stars that exhibit regular radial pulsations with periods of several days to several tens of days and brightness variations of up to about two magnitudes. The prototype is Delta Cephei, whose variability was discovered by John Goodricke in 1784.

Classical Cepheids obey a tight period–luminosity relation (Leavitt's law): the longer the pulsation period, the more luminous the star. Discovered by Henrietta Swan Leavitt in 1908–1912 from Cepheids in the Small Magellanic Cloud, this relation makes classical Cepheids fundamental standard candles for measuring distances within the Milky Way and to nearby galaxies, and thereby for calibrating the extragalactic distance scale and the Hubble constant.

Properties

Classical Cepheids are yellow supergiants of spectral types F, G and early K, with masses roughly 4 to 20 times that of the Sun and luminosities from a few hundred to tens of thousands of solar luminosities. Their pulsations are driven by the kappa mechanism acting in a partial ionization zone of helium in their envelopes: as the layer is compressed it becomes more opaque, trapping heat and driving expansion, then cools and becomes transparent, allowing contraction to resume.

During a pulsation cycle a Cepheid's radius changes by several percent — millions of kilometres — and its surface temperature varies by roughly 1,000 K, producing the observed variation in brightness. Cepheids are found in young open clusters and the spiral arms of galaxies, consistent with ages of tens to a few hundreds of millions of years.

Subtypes

Most classical Cepheids pulsate in the fundamental mode (DCEP), but some pulsate in the first overtone (DCEPS), with shorter periods and more sinusoidal light curves. Multi-mode and s-Cepheids are further observational subclasses. They are distinguished from the older, lower-mass Type II Cepheids (W Virginis stars), which follow a different period–luminosity relation — a distinction established by Walter Baade that doubled the accepted size of the universe in 1952.
