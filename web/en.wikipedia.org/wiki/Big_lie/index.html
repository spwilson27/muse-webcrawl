Big lie

The big lie (German: große Lüge) is a propaganda technique and gross distortion or misrepresentation of the truth, used especially for political purposes. The expression was coined by Adolf Hitler in Mein Kampf (1925), where he accused Jews of using a lie so "colossal" that no one would believe anyone would distort the truth so infamously. In common usage the term describes the technique itself: a falsehood so enormous, repeated so insistently, that people come to accept it because they cannot imagine it being entirely fabricated.

Repetition and scale are the core of the technique.

Origins and Nazi usage

Hitler's passage attributed the big lie to Jews concerning Germany's defeat in the First World War and General Ludendorff. Ironically, Nazi propaganda minister Joseph Goebbels is frequently misquoted as endorsing the technique; historians note that the Nazis repeatedly accused their opponents of big lies while practising massive deception themselves. A 1943 United States Office of Strategic Services psychological profile of Hitler observed that his primary rules included never allowing the public to cool off and never admitting a fault.

Later usage

The term has since been applied to many political falsehoods, including Cold War propaganda, claims of weapons of mass destruction, and election denial — most prominently the false claim that the 2020 United States presidential election was stolen, which journalists and historians widely labelled a "big lie". Scholars of propaganda note the technique works in concert with repetition, control of media channels and the illusory truth effect.
