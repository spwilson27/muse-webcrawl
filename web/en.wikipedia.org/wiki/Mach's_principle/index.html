Mach's principle

In theoretical physics, particularly in discussions of gravitation theories, Mach's principle (or Mach's conjecture) is the name given by Albert Einstein to an imprecise hypothesis often credited to the physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach. The hypothesis is that the existence of absolute rotation (the distinction of local inertial frames vs. rotating reference frames) is determined by the large-scale distribution of matter, as exemplified by this anecdote:

You are standing in a field looking at the stars. Your arms are resting freely at your side, and you see that the distant stars are not moving. Now start spinning. The stars are whirling around you and your arms are pulled away from your body. Why should your arms be pulled away when the stars are whirling? Why should they stay at your side when the stars are not moving?

Mach's principle says that this is not a coincidence — that there is a physical law that relates the motion of the distant stars to the local inertial frame.

History

In his Science of Mechanics (1883), Mach criticised Newton's notion of absolute space, using Newton's own rotating-bucket argument: Mach suggested that the water in the bucket curves relative to the fixed stars, not relative to an invisible absolute space — famously remarking that no one can say how the experiment would turn out if the bucket's walls were made "several leagues thick". Einstein, who coined the term in 1918, took Mach's ideas as one of the guiding inspirations for general relativity, hoping a fully relational theory would make inertia an interaction with all the other mass in the universe.

Status in general relativity

General relativity is usually regarded as only partially "Machian": it allows solutions (such as the empty Minkowski and de Sitter universes, and the rotating Gödel universe) in which inertia seems to exist without distant matter, yet it also exhibits genuinely Mach-like effects, notably frame dragging (the Lense–Thirring effect), in which rotating masses influence local inertial frames. Gravity Probe B measured frame dragging around the Earth. Alternative theories such as Brans–Dicke theory were constructed to incorporate Mach's principle more fully.
