Dover Bronze Age Boat

MaterialOak planks
Createdc. 1575–1520 BC
Discovered1992, Dover, Kent
Present locationDover Museum, Kent

The Dover Bronze Age Boat is one of the world's oldest known seagoing boats, dating from around 1575–1520 BC. It was discovered in 1992 during roadworks in Dover, Kent, England, buried about 6 metres below the modern street surface in waterlogged sediments of a former river estuary. Approximately 9.5 metres of the boat's length was recovered; one end had been removed in antiquity and the remainder was left in the ground, so the original vessel is estimated to have been about 18 metres long.

Construction

The boat was built of oak planks sewn together with yew withies and wedged in place — an early form of the sewn-plank tradition also seen in the Ferriby boats from the Humber. Caulking of moss and beeswax made the seams watertight. Four large timbers formed the bottom and sides, and the flat-bottomed hull, about 2.3 metres wide, could have carried a crew of around 16 paddlers and a substantial cargo. Analysis suggests it was a sophisticated, purpose-built sea vessel rather than a river craft.

Significance

The boat is important evidence for cross-Channel trade in the Bronze Age, when tin, copper, gold and finished metalwork moved between Britain and continental Europe. Its scale and construction demonstrate advanced maritime technology in northern Europe some 3,500 years ago. A half-scale experimental reconstruction, named Bryher, was built and sea-trialled in 2012–2013 as part of an international research project.

Display

The preserved timbers were lifted in sections, recorded in detail, and conserved over several years. The boat now forms the centrepiece of the Bronze Age Boat Gallery at Dover Museum, one of the museum's principal attractions.
