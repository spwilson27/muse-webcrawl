A New Kind of Science is a book by Stephen Wolfram, published in 2002. It contains an extensive empirical and systematic study of computational systems such as cellular automata. Wolfram calls these systems simple programs and argues that the scientific philosophy and methods appropriate for the study of simple programs are relevant to other fields of science.

Content

The book's central claim is that extremely simple programs — most famously elementary cellular automata such as Rule 30 and Rule 110 — can produce behaviour of great complexity, and that this phenomenon underlies complexity throughout nature. It develops the idea of computational irreducibility: for many systems, the only way to determine their behaviour is to run the computation itself. Wolfram also proposed that Rule 110 is Turing complete, a result later proved by Matthew Cook.

Reception

The book was a commercial bestseller but received a mixed reception in the scientific community: reviewers praised the visualisations and the compilation of cellular automaton behaviour while criticising the originality claimed for some ideas and the self-published route of its release.
