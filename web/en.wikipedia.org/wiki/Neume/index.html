Neume

A neume (from Greek neûma, "sign" or "gesture") is the basic element of Western and Eastern systems of musical notation prior to the invention of five-line staff notation. Neumes were used in medieval manuscripts to notate Gregorian chant and other liturgical chant, indicating melodic contour — whether a phrase rose, fell, or repeated a pitch — rather than exact pitches or rhythms.

History

Neumatic notation emerged in Western Europe by the 9th century, possibly influenced by Byzantine ekphonetic notation and grammatical accent marks. The earliest surviving examples, such as those in the St. Gallen manuscripts, use "unheighted" neumes written in open space above the text (in campo aperto), which a singer who already knew the melody could use as a memory aid. From the 10th and 11th centuries, scribes began placing neumes at relative heights and adding staff lines — a development associated with Guido d'Arezzo — which gradually evolved into square notation on a four-line staff.

Types

Basic neume shapes include the punctum (a dot, for a single note), the virga (a vertical stroke), the two-note podatus (ascending) and clivis (descending), and three-note forms such as the torculus, porrectus, scandicus, and climacus. More elaborate signs — quilisma, oriscus, pressus — and "liquescent" neumes conveyed nuances of performance. Parallel systems developed in Byzantine chant and in other chant traditions.

Modern use

Neumatic square notation remains the standard notation for Gregorian chant in Roman Catholic liturgical books such as the Graduale Romanum, and its transcription and performance are studied by chant scholars and groups such as those following the Solesmes method.
