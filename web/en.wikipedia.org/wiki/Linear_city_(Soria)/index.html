The linear city was an urban plan proposed in 1882 by the Spanish engineer and planner Arturo Soria y Mata. Instead of growing radially around a congested centre, the city would be built as a long, narrow band — in Soria's scheme, some 500 metres wide and potentially unlimited in length — organised around a central spine carrying railways, roads and utilities.

The model

Houses and services would line both sides of the spine, so that every resident lived close both to fast transport and to open countryside. Soria summarised the idea in the maxim that the ideal city should "ruralise urban life and urbanise rural life". Linear development, he argued, would abolish the contrast between centre and periphery, ease congestion, and allow the city to extend indefinitely, in theory even linking existing cities across a country.

Ciudad Lineal, Madrid

Soria founded a company to build a demonstration project northeast of Madrid: the Ciudad Lineal, a five-kilometre tram-served strip of detached houses with gardens. Only part of the grand scheme, which imagined a ring and extended lines around Madrid, was completed, but the district survives today as a neighbourhood of Madrid, its main axis named Calle de Arturo Soria.

Influence

The linear idea resurfaced repeatedly in twentieth-century planning — in Soviet plans such as Milyutin's linear schemes for Stalingrad, in Le Corbusier's and Hilberseimer's linear proposals, and in later "linear city" megaprojects — making Soria's pamphlet one of the foundational texts of modern urbanism.
