Bear attack

A bear attack is an attack by a bear on another animal or a human. Although bears are large predators with formidable strength, attacks on humans are rare in most regions; the majority of attacks are defensive — a surprised bear, or a sow protecting cubs — rather than predatory. Fatalities occur but remain uncommon relative to the number of human–bear encounters.

Causes

Wildlife agencies classify most attacks as defensive: the bear perceives a threat at close range and charges, sometimes making contact before breaking off. Predatory attacks, in which a bear treats a person as food, are much rarer and are most often attributed to black bears and polar bears. Habituation to human food — bears that learn to associate people or camps with food — is a major risk factor, and food-conditioned bears are frequently involved in incidents. Dogs off leash can also provoke or redirect attacks.

Species

Brown bears (including grizzlies) account for most serious defensive attacks in North America and Eurasia; the largest land incidents include the 1915 Sankebetsu brown bear incident in Japan, in which a giant Ussuri brown bear killed seven people. American black bears are involved in many encounters but relatively few fatal attacks (roughly one fatality per year across North America on average). Polar bears, which live in sparsely populated Arctic regions, rarely encounter people but may regard them as prey. Sloth bears in India and Sri Lanka injure hundreds of people in some years, usually in surprise encounters, and are considered among the most dangerous bears relative to their size.

Prevention and response

Prevention advice includes making noise while hiking, storing food in bear-resistant containers or hangs, carrying bear spray (shown in studies to be highly effective), and never approaching cubs. Recommended responses differ by context: for defensive grizzly attacks, playing dead after contact is often advised, whereas for predatory attacks or black bear attacks, fighting back is recommended. Wildlife managers relocate or euthanise bears that repeatedly threaten people.

Notable cases

Famous incidents include the 2003 death of bear enthusiast Timothy Treadwell and Amie Huguenard in Katmai National Park, Alaska, the subject of the documentary Grizzly Man; the 1967 "Night of the Grizzlies" in Glacier National Park, in which two women were killed in separate incidents on one night, an event that transformed bear management policy; and the 2011 Svalbard attack in which a polar bear killed a British schoolboy.
