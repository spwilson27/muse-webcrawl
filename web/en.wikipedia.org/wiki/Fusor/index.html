Fusor

A fusor is a device that uses an electric field to accelerate ions to energies sufficient for nuclear fusion. Invented by Philo T. Farnsworth in the 1960s, the most common type is the Farnsworth–Hirsch fusor, in which ions are confined by inertial electrostatic confinement (IEC) between concentric wire grid electrodes inside a vacuum chamber.

Operation

A vacuum chamber contains a wire cage held at a large negative voltage relative to the chamber wall. Deuterium gas is introduced at low pressure and ionized; the positive ions are accelerated toward the cage, passing through it and converging at the center, where some collide and fuse, releasing neutrons. Ions that miss continue to the far side and oscillate back and forth, giving multiple chances to fuse.

Applications

Although fusors are far from net energy production, they are a practical neutron source and are popular with hobbyists, who build "fusor" devices capable of genuine deuterium fusion. Commercial IEC devices have been used as sealed neutron generators for analysis and detection applications.
