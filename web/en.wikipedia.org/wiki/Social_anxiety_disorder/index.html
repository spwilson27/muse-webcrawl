Social anxiety disorder (SAD), also known as social phobia, is an anxiety disorder characterised by a marked and persistent fear of social or performance situations in which the person is exposed to possible scrutiny by others. The individual fears acting in a way that will be humiliating or embarrassing — showing anxiety symptoms, making a mistake, or being negatively evaluated or rejected — and typically avoids the feared situations or endures them with intense distress.

Signs and symptoms

Common feared situations include public speaking, meeting new people, being watched while eating or writing, attending parties, talking to authority figures and participating in meetings or classes. Physical symptoms can include blushing, sweating, trembling, palpitations, dry mouth, nausea and difficulty speaking. Anticipatory anxiety may begin days or weeks before an event, and afterwards the person often ruminates over perceived failures ("post-event processing"). At its most severe, the disorder can lead to social isolation, educational underachievement, occupational impairment, depression and substance misuse.

Causes

Social anxiety disorder arises from a combination of genetic and environmental factors. It tends to run in families, and temperament traits such as behavioural inhibition in childhood increase risk. Negative social experiences (bullying, public embarrassment), overprotective or rejecting parenting styles, and cultural factors may contribute. Neurobiologically, the disorder is associated with heightened reactivity of the amygdala and other fear circuitry to social threat cues, and with serotonergic and other neurotransmitter systems.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, based on criteria such as those in the DSM-5: marked fear or anxiety about one or more social situations, fear of negative evaluation, near-invariable anxiety in those situations, avoidance or endurance with intense fear, symptoms out of proportion to the actual threat, persistence (typically six months or more), and clinically significant distress or impairment. The DSM-5 includes a "performance only" specifier for individuals whose anxiety is restricted to speaking or performing in public.

Treatment

Psychotherapy

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) — including exposure to feared situations, cognitive restructuring of distorted self-imagery and predictions, and social-skills or attention training — is the best-supported psychological treatment and is recommended as a first-line intervention.

Medication

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as sertraline and paroxetine, and the serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor venlafaxine, are the usual first-line medications. Other options include certain MAOIs and, for discrete performance anxiety, short-term beta blockers; benzodiazepines are generally avoided for long-term use because of dependence risk.

Epidemiology

Social anxiety disorder is among the most common anxiety disorders, with lifetime prevalence estimates around 7–12% in the United States and lower reported rates in many other countries. Onset is typically in early adolescence, and the disorder is diagnosed more often in women than in men, though the sex difference is modest.
