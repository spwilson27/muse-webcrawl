The Silesian Uprisings (Polish: Powstania śląskie; German: Aufstände in Oberschlesien) were three armed insurrections by Polish inhabitants of Upper Silesia in 1919, 1920 and 1921, seeking to detach the coal-rich region from Germany and join it to the newly re-established Polish state. The uprisings bracketed the plebiscite mandated by the Treaty of Versailles; the third and largest, in May–July 1921, led the League of Nations to partition Upper Silesia, awarding Poland the eastern industrial district including Katowice.

Background

Upper Silesia's population was mixed—Polish-speaking in much of the countryside and east, German in the towns and west—and its coalfields and zinc industry made it one of Europe's great industrial basins. Versailles ordered a plebiscite under an Inter-Allied Commission. Tensions rose amid strikes, mutual intimidation and clashes between German security police and the clandestine Polish Military Organisation (POW GŚl.), led politically by Wojciech Korfanty, the Polish plebiscite commissioner.

First uprising (August 1919)

Sparked by the shooting of Polish miners at Mysłowice, the first rising began on 16–17 August 1919 under Alfons Zgrzebniok. Poorly coordinated and lacking heavy weapons, it was suppressed within a week by German troops and border guards; several thousand Poles fled into Poland. An amnesty and changes to the local police followed.

Second uprising (August 1920)

Provoked by German violence during the Polish–Soviet War crisis—including the killing of the physician Andrzej Mielęcki—the second uprising of 19–25 August 1920 achieved its limited aims: the German Sicherheitspolizei was dissolved and replaced by a mixed plebiscite police. It was called off by Korfanty once these concessions were won.

Plebiscite and third uprising (1921)

The plebiscite of 20 March 1921 returned about 60% for Germany and 40% for Poland overall, though many eastern districts voted Polish. Expecting the Allies to award nearly all the industrial area to Germany on the basis of the overall vote, Korfanty launched the third uprising on the night of 2–3 May 1921. Insurgents seized most of the countryside east of the Oder; German Freikorps and the Selbstschutz counterattacked, storming the Polish-held Annaberg (Góra Świętej Anny) on 21–23 May in the uprisings' largest battle. Allied—mainly French—troops eventually imposed a ceasefire line. In October 1921 the League of Nations divided the region: Poland received roughly a third of the territory and population but about half the coal mines and most of the heavy industry. The settlement was regulated by the German–Polish Geneva Convention of May 1922.

Legacy

The uprisings are a foundational episode of modern Polish national memory in Silesia, commemorated by the Silesian Insurgents' Monument in Katowice and by the regional holiday; German memory traditionally emphasised the Selbstschutz defence of the region. Casualties across the three risings numbered several thousand.
