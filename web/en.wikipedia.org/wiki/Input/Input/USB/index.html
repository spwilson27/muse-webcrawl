USB connectors and devices.

Universal Serial Bus (USB) is an industry standard, developed by the USB Implementers Forum (USB-IF), that allows data exchange and delivery of power between many types of electronics. It specifies the architecture, in particular the physical interfaces, and communication protocols to and from hosts, such as personal computers, to and from peripheral devices, e.g. displays, keyboards, and mass storage devices, and to and from hubs that expand the number of hosts and devices.

History

USB was designed in the mid-1990s by a consortium including Intel, Compaq, Microsoft, and others, with Ajay Bhatt a prominent co-inventor, to replace the tangle of serial, parallel, keyboard, and proprietary ports with one plug-and-play connection. USB 1.0 (1996) offered 1.5 and 12 Mbit/s; USB 2.0 (2000) raised this to 480 Mbit/s and enabled flash drives and external disks. USB 3.0 (2008) added full-duplex SuperSpeed at 5 Gbit/s, later generations reaching 10 and 20 Gbit/s. USB4 (2019), built on Thunderbolt 3, reaches 40 Gbit/s, with a version 2.0 specification at 80 Gbit/s.

Connectors

Early USB used rectangular Type-A and squarish Type-B plugs, miniaturised into Mini and Micro variants for portable devices. The reversible USB-C connector (2014) supports any orientation, high power, and alternate modes carrying DisplayPort or Thunderbolt signals, and has become the standard connector for phones, laptops, and peripherals — mandated for many devices in the European Union from 2024.

Protocol and device classes

USB is a host-controlled bus: the host polls devices and schedules transfers of four types — control, bulk, interrupt, and isochronous — to endpoints described by descriptors. Standard class codes let generic drivers handle mass storage, human interface devices (keyboards and mice), audio, video, printers, and communications devices without vendor drivers. On attachment, the host enumerates the device, assigns an address, and reads its descriptors to select a configuration.

Power

USB supplies 5 V power alongside data. Charging specifications progressively raised available current, and USB Power Delivery (USB PD) negotiates up to 48 V and 240 W, enough for large laptops and monitors, over USB-C cables with electronic markers.
