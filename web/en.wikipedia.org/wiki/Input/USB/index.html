USB (Universal Serial Bus) is an industry standard, developed by the USB Implementers Forum (USB-IF), for digital data transmission and power delivery between many types of electronics. It specifies cables, connectors, and protocols for connection, communication, and power supply between computers and peripheral devices, and has largely replaced serial ports, parallel ports, and many proprietary chargers.

History

USB was designed in the mid-1990s by a group of companies including Intel — engineer Ajay Bhatt is commonly credited as a leading inventor — to simplify connecting peripherals and eliminate the need to restart computers when attaching them. USB 1.0 (1996) offered 1.5 and 12 Mbit/s; USB 2.0 (2000) raised this to 480 Mbit/s; USB 3.0 (2008) added SuperSpeed at 5 Gbit/s; later versions reached 10, 20, and, with USB4, 40 Gbit/s and beyond. The reversible USB Type-C connector (2014) became the common physical form, and the European Union mandated USB-C charging for many portable devices from 2024.

Architecture

USB is a host-controlled, tiered-star bus: a host controller polls devices and schedules transfers, so devices do not need to arbitrate for the bus. On connection, the host enumerates the device, reads its descriptors, assigns an address, and loads the driver matching its device class. Standard classes — including mass storage, audio, video, communications, and human interface devices — let operating systems ship generic drivers. Data moves in four transfer types: control, bulk, interrupt, and isochronous.

Power delivery

Besides data, USB supplies power: originally 5 V at up to 500 mA (USB 2.0), later specifications and the USB Power Delivery standard raise this to as much as 240 W (48 V, 5 A) with negotiated voltage and current, enabling a single cable to charge laptops and drive monitors.
