Innexin

Innexins are a family of transmembrane proteins that form gap junctions in invertebrates. They are functionally analogous to the connexin proteins of vertebrates, though the two families share no significant sequence homology and evolved independently — a frequently cited example of convergent evolution at the molecular level. The name "innexin" derives from "invertebrate connexin".

Structure

Like connexins, innexins have four transmembrane domains, two extracellular loops, a cytoplasmic loop, and cytoplasmic N- and C-termini. Eight innexin subunits assemble into a hemichannel called an innexon; two innexons in adjacent cells dock to form a complete gap junction channel, allowing ions and small molecules (up to roughly 1–2 kDa) to pass directly between cells. Innexins can also form unpaired hemichannels that release signalling molecules such as ATP into the extracellular space.

Distribution and genes

Innexins have been identified across invertebrate phyla, including arthropods, nematodes, molluscs, annelids and cnidarians. The genome of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans encodes 25 innexin genes, and the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster encodes eight. Individual innexins are expressed in specific tissues — nerve cords, gut, reproductive organs and epithelia — and different subunits can combine into heteromeric channels with distinct properties.

Functions

Innexin gap junctions mediate electrical coupling between neurons, coordinate muscle contraction, and are required for normal development, including germline development and left–right patterning in some species. Mutations in fly innexin genes such as shaking-B disrupt the giant fibre system that drives the escape response, while the gene ogre and others are involved in growth control in imaginal discs. Innexins are also the molecular targets of some antiparasitic drugs: the anthelmintic action that paralyses worms can involve disruption of innexin-based coupling in nematode muscle.

Pannexins

Vertebrate pannexins are homologues of innexins discovered by sequence similarity. Despite their ancestry, pannexins in vertebrates generally function as single-membrane channels rather than as gap junction proteins.
