Soto

Sōtō Zen (曹洞宗, Sōtōshū) is the largest of the three traditional sects of Zen Buddhism in Japan, the others being Rinzai and Ōbaku. The school emphasizes shikantaza, meditation with no objects, anchors, or content — "just sitting" — as the primary means of realizing one's Buddha nature. The sect was first established in China as the Caodong school during the Tang dynasty, and was transmitted to Japan in the 13th century by the monk Dōgen Zenji, who founded the head temple Eihei-ji in present-day Fukui Prefecture.

History

Chinese origins

The Caodong school traces its lineage to the Chinese Chan master Dongshan Liangjie (807–869) and his disciple Caoshan Benji (840–901), from whose names the school's name is compounded. The tradition is associated with the "five ranks" teaching attributed to Dongshan, a schematic of the interplay between the absolute and the relative.

Transmission to Japan

Dōgen traveled to China in 1223 and studied under the Caodong master Rujing, from whom he received dharma transmission. Returning to Japan in 1227, he eventually founded Eihei-ji in 1244. His voluminous writings, above all the Shōbōgenzō, remain the doctrinal foundation of the school. After Dōgen's death, his disciple Keizan Jōkin founded Sōji-ji and is credited with popularizing Sōtō Zen beyond the monastic elite, incorporating funerary and memorial services for lay patrons.

Practice

The signature practice of Sōtō Zen is shikantaza, "just sitting": seated meditation (zazen) in which the practitioner maintains alert posture and awareness without focusing on any object, mantra, or koan. Dōgen taught that practice and realization are one (shushō-ittō) — sitting is itself the expression of Buddhahood rather than a means to an external end. In contrast to the Rinzai emphasis on koan introspection, Sōtō is sometimes characterized as "silent illumination" (mokushō zen).

Organization

Today the Sōtō school (Sōtōshū) administers roughly 15,000 temples in Japan and functions as one of the largest Japanese Buddhist organizations. Its two head temples are Eihei-ji and Sōji-ji. Sōtō priests commonly conduct funerals and ancestral memorial rites, a central feature of Japanese "funerary Buddhism." Since the 20th century, Sōtō Zen has spread widely in North America and Europe through teachers such as Shunryu Suzuki, founder of the San Francisco Zen Center.
