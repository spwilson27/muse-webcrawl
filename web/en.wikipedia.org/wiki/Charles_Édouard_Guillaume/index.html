Charles Édouard Guillaume (15 February 1861 – 13 May 1938) was a Swiss physicist who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1920 in recognition of the service he had rendered to precision measurements in physics by his discovery of anomalies in nickel steel alloys — notably Invar, a nickel–iron alloy with almost no thermal expansion, and Elinvar, whose elasticity is nearly temperature-independent.

Charles Édouard Guillaume (1861–1938), Nobel laureate in physics

Life and career

Guillaume was born in Fleurier, Switzerland, the son of a watchmaker, and studied at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Zürich, obtaining his doctorate in 1883. After a brief period in military service he joined the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) at Sèvres, near Paris, in 1883, and remained there for his whole career, becoming its director in 1915.

Invar and Elinvar

At the BIPM, Guillaume worked on thermometry and on the materials used for standards of length. In 1896 he discovered that an alloy of roughly 36% nickel and 64% iron expands hardly at all with temperature — the alloy he named Invar (for "invariable"). Invar transformed precision engineering: it was used for surveyors' tapes, geodetic baselines, clock pendulums and components requiring dimensional stability. His later alloy Elinvar made possible balance springs for watches and chronometers whose rate does not vary with temperature, a major advance for horology, the craft of his father's trade.

Honours

Guillaume received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1920 — one of the few Nobel Prizes awarded for work in metallurgy and measurement. He was a member of many academies and a central figure in international metrology, contributing to the precision thermometry and standards on which the metric system rested. He died at Sèvres in 1938.
