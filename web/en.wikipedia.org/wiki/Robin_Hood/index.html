Robin Hood is a legendary heroic outlaw originally depicted in English folklore and subsequently featured in literature, theatre and cinema. According to legend, he was a highly skilled archer and swordsman who, with his band of Merry Men, robbed from the rich and gave to the poor, living in Sherwood Forest in Nottinghamshire in defiance of the Sheriff of Nottingham.

Origins

The earliest references to Robin Hood appear in fourteenth-century literature: a passing mention in William Langland's Piers Plowman (c. 1377) shows that tales of Robin were already popular. The oldest surviving ballads, such as A Gest of Robyn Hode and Robin Hood and the Monk, date from the fifteenth century. In these early tales Robin is a yeoman — not yet the dispossessed nobleman of later versions — devoted to the Virgin Mary and a loyal subject of the king, while his enemies are corrupt sheriffs and wealthy abbots.

The legend

Over the centuries the story accumulated new characters and episodes: Maid Marian as Robin's beloved, the jovial Friar Tuck, the minstrel Alan-a-Dale, and Will Scarlet joined the giant Little John among the Merry Men. Later tradition placed Robin in the time of Richard the Lionheart's captivity and Prince John's misrule, and made him a wronged Saxon earl, sometimes named Robert of Locksley. The famous archery contest, the quarterstaff fight with Little John on a bridge, and Robin's death at Kirklees Priory, bled by a treacherous prioress, are among the best-known episodes.

Historicity

No firm historical Robin Hood has been identified, although records contain various outlaws and fugitives with similar names — "Robehod" and "Hobbehod" appear as nicknames for criminals in thirteenth-century documents. Scholars have proposed several candidates, including Yorkshire outlaws of the thirteenth century, but the figure is generally treated as a composite folk hero rather than a single real person.

In popular culture

Robin Hood has been retold constantly: in the May Games of Tudor England, in Sir Walter Scott's Ivanhoe (1819), in Howard Pyle's The Merry Adventures of Robin Hood (1883), and in dozens of films, from Douglas Fairbanks's 1922 silent epic and Errol Flynn's The Adventures of Robin Hood (1938) to Disney's animated Robin Hood (1973) and the BBC television series Robin of Sherwood (1984–86) and Robin Hood (2006–09).
