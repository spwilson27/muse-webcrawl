Home-ownership

Home-ownership is the status of owning one's own residence — a house, apartment, condominium, or similar dwelling — rather than renting it. The home-ownership rate is the proportion of households in a given area that own their homes, a commonly cited social and economic indicator. In many countries home-ownership is promoted by public policy as a route to wealth accumulation, residential stability, and civic engagement, and home purchases are typically financed with mortgage loans.

International variation

Home-ownership rates vary widely: they are very high in many Eastern European countries (exceeding 90% in Romania, Slovakia, and Croatia, largely as a legacy of post-communist privatization), in Singapore (around 90%, under the Housing and Development Board leasehold system), and in China; they are around the middle range in the United States (roughly 65%), the United Kingdom, and France; and they are comparatively low in Germany and Switzerland (around 40–50%), where secure long-term renting is common. Rates also differ by age, income, and ethnicity within countries.

Costs and benefits

Compared with renting, owning entails purchase costs, mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, and maintenance, but builds equity as the loan is repaid and the property (potentially) appreciates; the owner gains security of tenure and freedom to alter the property, but bears market risk and reduced mobility. Economists debate the macroeconomic effects of high ownership, including whether policies promoting ownership fuel housing bubbles, and the concept of "imputed rent" — the housing services an owner effectively receives from their own property — features in national accounts and tax debates.
