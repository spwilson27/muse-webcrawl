In media, a serial is a work of narrative fiction published or broadcast in sequential instalments, each of which continues the story of the previous part. Unlike a series, in which episodes are largely self-contained, a serial tells one extended story across its instalments, often ending each part with a cliffhanger to compel the audience to return.

Serialised literature

The serial form flourished in nineteenth-century literature, when novels were issued in monthly or weekly parts in magazines or as standalone pamphlets. Charles Dickens popularised the form with works such as The Pickwick Papers (1836–37), and many canonical novels—including those of Thackeray, Dostoevsky, and Tolstoy—first appeared serially. Serial publication allowed readers to buy stories cheaply and let authors adjust plots in response to sales and feedback.

Film serials

From the silent era through the 1950s, cinemas screened film serials (also called chapter plays): stories divided into weekly chapters of about fifteen to twenty minutes shown before the feature film. Studios such as Republic Pictures and Columbia produced hundreds, including Flash Gordon (1936), The Perils of Pauline (1914, an early landmark), and the Batman and Captain Marvel serials. The form declined with the rise of television in the 1950s.

Radio, television, and podcasts

Radio carried the serial tradition into daytime drama, giving rise to the soap opera. Television adopted both soap operas and prime-time serialised drama; story-arc storytelling became dominant in prestige television from the 1990s onward, and streaming releases are often consumed as continuous serials. The podcast Serial (2014), a season-long true-crime investigation, revived the term and format for audio, becoming a cultural phenomenon and popularising serialised nonfiction podcasting.
