In ancient Greek and Roman architecture, a peristyle (from Greek περίστυλον, peristylon, from περί, "around", and στῦλος, "column") is a continuous porch formed by a row of columns surrounding the perimeter of a building or a courtyard. The corresponding Latin term is peristylium. A courtyard so surrounded — most familiar from the Roman domus — is often simply called a peristyle as well.

Roman houses

In the Roman town house, the peristyle was typically a garden courtyard ringed by colonnaded porticoes on all four sides, onto which the principal reception rooms opened. It came after the atrium in the sequence of domestic spaces and was the private heart of the house, planted with shrubs, fountains, statuary and sometimes fishponds. Well-preserved examples survive at Pompeii and Herculaneum, such as the House of the Faun and the House of the Vettii.

Temples and public buildings

A temple surrounded by a single row of columns on all sides is termed peripteral. In Hellenistic gymnasia, palaces and villas, large peristyle courts served as centres of circulation and display. The form passed into Byzantine and later Islamic architecture in courtyard houses, and its enclosed descendant in medieval monastic architecture is the cloister.

Modern use

The term is also used in modern architecture for colonnaded courtyards, for example in museums and university buildings modelled on classical precedents.
