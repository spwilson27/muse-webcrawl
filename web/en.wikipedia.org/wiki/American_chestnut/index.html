The American chestnut (Castanea dentata) is a large, fast-growing deciduous tree of the beech family native to eastern North America. Before the species was devastated by the chestnut blight, a fungal disease, it was one of the most important forest trees of eastern North America and the most abundant, sometimes composing a quarter of the hardwood forest of the Appalachian Mountains. An estimated four billion trees were killed in the first half of the 20th century, one of the worst ecological disasters in recorded history.

Description and ecology

Mature American chestnuts could reach 30 metres (100 ft) or more in height with trunks over 3 metres in diameter, growing straight and largely free of branches for the lower half. The leaves are lance-shaped with coarse teeth. The nuts, borne in spiny burs, were sweeter and smaller than those of the European chestnut and were a critical food source for wildlife — deer, bears, turkeys, and squirrels — as well as for livestock and rural people, who sold them in cities as a cash crop. The rot-resistant, straight-grained wood was used for timber, split-rail fences, telegraph poles, and tannin extraction.

Chestnut blight

The blight, caused by the fungus Cryphonectria parasitica introduced on imported Japanese chestnut trees, was first noticed at the Bronx Zoo in 1904. Spreading by wind-borne spores, it girdled and killed virtually every mature tree within its range by the 1950s. The species survives mainly as stump sprouts that repeatedly grow and die back, since the fungus does not kill the root system.

Restoration efforts

The American Chestnut Foundation (founded 1983) pursues a backcross-breeding programme crossing American chestnuts with blight-resistant Chinese chestnuts and breeding back toward American form. Other researchers have produced a transgenic blight-tolerant tree, "Darling 58", engineered with a wheat oxalate oxidase gene, whose proposed release has been the subject of regulatory review and scientific controversy.
