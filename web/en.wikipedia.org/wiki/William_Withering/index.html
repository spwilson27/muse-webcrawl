William Withering


Born 17 March 1741, Wellington, Shropshire, England
Died 6 October 1799 (aged 58), Sparkbrook, Birmingham
Known for Introducing digitalis (foxglove) into medical practice

William Withering (17 March 1741 – 6 October 1799) was an English physician, botanist, and member of the Lunar Society, celebrated for his scientific study of the foxglove and its use in treating dropsy.

Life and career

Withering studied medicine at Edinburgh and practised first at Stafford, then from 1775 at Birmingham, where he became physician to the Birmingham General Hospital and one of the most sought-after doctors in the Midlands. He was a central member of the Lunar Society of Birmingham alongside Erasmus Darwin, Joseph Priestley, and James Watt.

Digitalis

In 1775 Withering learned from a Shropshire herbalist, Mrs Hutton, of a folk remedy for dropsy (oedema) whose active ingredient he identified as foxglove (Digitalis purpurea). Over a decade he carefully recorded 163 cases, determining safe dosages and toxic effects, and published his findings in An Account of the Foxglove (1785). His work established digitalis — the source of modern cardiac glycosides such as digoxin — in the treatment of heart failure.

Botany and other work

Withering's Botanical Arrangement of British Plants (1776) was a widely used flora, and he also studied mineralogy and tuberculosis, from which he himself suffered. He died in 1799 and was buried at Edgbaston Old Church; a foxglove is carved on his memorial.
