Hōryū-ji (法隆寺, "Temple of the Flourishing Dharma") is a Buddhist temple in Ikaruga, Nara Prefecture, Japan. Founded in 607 by Prince Shōtoku and Empress Suiko, it is one of the oldest and most historically important temples in Japan. Its precinct contains the world's oldest surviving wooden buildings, dating from the Asuka period, and in 1993 Hōryū-ji was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site — the first in Japan — under the listing "Buddhist Monuments in the Hōryū-ji Area".

History

The temple was built to fulfil a vow made by Emperor Yōmei, who prayed for recovery from illness and died before its completion; his son Prince Shōtoku and Empress Suiko carried out the vow, completing the original temple (Wakakusa-garan) in 607. According to the Nihon Shoki, the temple burned down in 670, and it was rebuilt by the early eighth century. Whether the present main buildings date from the original foundation or from the rebuilding was a long-running scholarly controversy; dendrochronology and other evidence now favour a reconstruction completed around 711, using timber felled in the late seventh century.

Architecture

The temple grounds are divided into the Western Precinct (Sai-in) and the Eastern Precinct (Tō-in). The Sai-in centres on the kondō (main hall) and the gojū-no-tō, a five-story pagoda about 32.5 metres tall. Both are celebrated masterpieces of Asuka-period architecture, characterised by cloud-shaped bracket supports, entasis in the columns, and (in the pagoda) a central pillar suspended in a way thought to dampen earthquake motion. The kondō houses famous bronze images including the Shaka triad cast by the sculptor Tori Busshi in 623.

Treasures

Hōryū-ji holds an exceptional collection of early Buddhist art, much of it transferred to the Tokyo National Museum for safekeeping, including the Kudara Kannon statue and the Tamamushi Shrine, a miniature shrine decorated with the iridescent wing cases of jewel beetles. The kondō's wall paintings, depicting Pure Land paradises, were masterpieces of early Japanese painting; they were badly damaged by fire in 1949 during restoration work — an event that prompted Japan's Law for the Protection of Cultural Properties (1950) and the designation of Cultural Properties Protection Day.
