The vaquero (Spanish: [baˈkeɾo], lit. "cow man") is a horse-mounted livestock herder of a tradition that originates in Spain and was developed in Mexico and the southwestern United States. The vaquero tradition is the direct ancestor of the North American cowboy, and much of the cowboy's equipment, vocabulary and technique derives from it.

History

Ranching and mounted herding were introduced to the Americas by Spanish colonists from the 16th century onward, notably in the mission and hacienda systems of New Spain. Indigenous and mestizo horsemen trained in this system became the vaqueros of northern Mexico and what is now Texas, Arizona, New Mexico and California. In California and the Great Basin, the tradition developed into the buckaroo style, whose name is itself an anglicisation of "vaquero".

Tradition and equipment

The vaquero method emphasizes a slow, precise training of the horse through stages — hackamore, two-rein, and finally the spade bit "straight up in the bridle". Vaquero gear includes the rawhide reata (lariat), the horned stock saddle adapted for dally roping, and ornate silver-mounted bits and spurs. Modern "Californio" and buckaroo horsemen continue to practice and celebrate the tradition at events such as California's Vaquero Show.

Legacy

American cowboy vocabulary preserves many Spanish vaquero terms: lasso, rodeo, bronco, chaps (from chaparreras), stampede (from estampida) and ranch (from rancho). The vaquero has been recognized as a foundational figure in the cultural heritage of Mexico and the American West.
