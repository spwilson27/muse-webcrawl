The Algiers Agreement was a peace agreement between the governments of Ethiopia and Eritrea, signed on 12 December 2000 in Algiers, Algeria. It formally ended the Eritrean–Ethiopian War (1998–2000), one of the deadliest conflicts in recent African history, and built upon a cessation-of-hostilities agreement signed in June of the same year.

Background

The war began in May 1998 over disputed territory along the poorly demarcated border between the two countries, centred on the town of Badme. Fighting continued for two years, costing tens of thousands of lives, before Ethiopia's military offensive in May 2000 broke the stalemate. On 18 June 2000 the two sides signed an Agreement on Cessation of Hostilities, also in Algiers, under the auspices of the Organisation of African Unity chaired by Algerian president Abdelaziz Bouteflika.

Terms

Under the agreement, the parties committed to terminate hostilities permanently and to release and repatriate prisoners of war. The agreement established two neutral bodies: a Boundary Commission, charged with delimiting and demarcating the colonial-era border on the basis of pertinent colonial treaties and applicable international law, and a Claims Commission, tasked with deciding claims for loss, damage or injury arising from the conflict. The Boundary Commission's decision was to be final and binding. A 25-kilometre-wide Temporary Security Zone was created inside Eritrean territory, monitored by the United Nations Mission in Ethiopia and Eritrea (UNMEE).

Aftermath

The Eritrea–Ethiopia Boundary Commission delivered its decision in April 2002, awarding Badme to Eritrea. Ethiopia's subsequent refusal to accept the physical demarcation of the border produced a prolonged "no war, no peace" stalemate that lasted until 2018, when Ethiopian prime minister Abiy Ahmed accepted the commission's ruling and the two countries normalised relations.
