Nissl staining

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Nissl-stained neurons in cortical tissue

Nissl staining is a histological technique used to visualise neurons and the organisation of nervous tissue. It employs basic aniline dyes — most commonly cresyl violet — that bind to negatively charged nucleic acids (RNA and DNA) in cell nuclei and in the rough endoplasmic reticulum of the cytoplasm. The clumps of intensely stained rough endoplasmic reticulum in neuronal cell bodies are known as Nissl bodies or Nissl substance.

History

The method was developed in the 1880s–1890s by the German neuropathologist Franz Nissl, who, while still a medical student, won a prize for his staining technique using magenta and later methylene blue. Nissl staining became a foundational tool of cytoarchitectonics, enabling researchers such as Korbinian Brodmann to map the layered structure of the cerebral cortex.

Procedure

Tissue is fixed, sectioned, and immersed in a solution of cresyl violet acetate; excess dye is then removed ("differentiated") in alcohol until the desired contrast is reached, and the section is dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. The stain renders neuronal somata and proximal dendrites in deep purple-blue while leaving axons and glial nuclei comparatively pale.

Applications

Nissl staining remains standard in neuroanatomy for delineating nuclei and cortical layers, counting neurons, and studying neuronal injury. Damaged neurons undergo chromatolysis — the dispersal of Nissl substance — which can be detected with the stain. Because it labels all neurons (and glia) non-selectively, it is often used alongside immunohistochemistry to provide anatomical context.
