In graph theory, a planar graph is a graph that can be embedded in the plane — drawn on a flat surface so that its edges intersect only at their endpoints. Such a drawing is called a planar embedding; a planar graph drawn in this way, together with the faces of the drawing (including the unbounded outer face), forms a plane graph.

Characterisations

Kuratowski's theorem (1930) states that a graph is planar if and only if it contains no subdivision of the complete graph K5 or the complete bipartite graph K3,3. The latter corresponds to the classic "three utilities problem": connecting three houses to three utilities without crossings is impossible. Wagner's theorem gives an equivalent characterisation using graph minors.

For a connected planar graph, Euler's formula relates vertices, edges and faces: V − E + F = 2. From it follows that a simple planar graph with at least three vertices has at most 3n − 6 edges, that its average degree is less than 6, and hence that every planar graph has a vertex of degree at most five.

Colouring and duality

The faces of a planar embedding form the dual graph. Colouring the faces of a planar map is equivalent to colouring the vertices of its dual; the four colour theorem (proved by Appel and Haken in 1976) states that four colours always suffice. Six colours follow easily from the degree bound.

Algorithms and applications

Planarity can be tested in linear time. Planar graphs arise in road networks, printed circuit design, and geographic maps, and their special structure (for example the planar separator theorem) makes many otherwise hard problems easier on them.
