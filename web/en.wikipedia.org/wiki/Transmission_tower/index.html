Transmission tower

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A transmission tower, also known as an electricity pylon in the United Kingdom, is a tall steel structure used to support an overhead power line. Transmission towers carry the high-voltage conductors (typically 110 kV and above) of the bulk electricity transmission network, holding them high above the ground and separated from each other and from the tower by strings of insulators. Most are lattice towers assembled from angle steel, though tubular steel monopoles, concrete poles, and guyed masts are also used.

Design

The classic lattice tower is a self-supporting structure with a broad base tapering upward, cross-arms projecting horizontally to carry the conductor bundles for each phase of one or two three-phase circuits. A thinner earth (shield) wire is often strung between the tower peaks to intercept lightning. Tower height depends on voltage, terrain and span length: ordinary towers are 15–55 m tall, while special crossing towers — such as those spanning the Elbe or the Yangtze — exceed 200 m, among the tallest electricity pylons in the world.

Designs have national characters: the UK's standard L6/L12 lattice towers (designed in the 1920s), the barrel-shaped "waist-type" towers common in North America and China, the Danube towers of central Europe, and compact or aesthetic designs (such as Denmark's Eagle and Finland's antler-like towers) developed to reduce visual impact. In the British Isles the word pylon derives from the tapering shape, reminiscent of an ancient Egyptian gateway.

Types

  • Suspension (tangent) towers: carry the line straight through; conductors hang from insulator strings and are not mechanically terminated.
  • Tension (angle/dead-end) towers: stronger structures used where the line changes direction or at intervals along it; conductors are anchored, and jumper loops carry current around the tower.
  • Transposition towers: swap the positions of the phases to balance the line's electrical characteristics.
  • Terminal towers: connect the overhead line to a substation or an underground cable.

Insulators and conductors

Conductors (usually aluminium conductor steel-reinforced, ACSR, often in bundles of two or four sub-conductors at the highest voltages) are hung from the cross-arms by insulator strings of glass or porcelain discs, or by modern composite polymer insulators. The number of discs increases with voltage, giving a rough visual gauge of the line's rating.
