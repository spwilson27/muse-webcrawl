InventorHeinrich Geissler
Year1857
TypeGas-discharge tube
PrincipleElectrical discharge through low-pressure gas
SuccessorsCrookes tube, cathode-ray tube, neon lamp

A Geissler tube is an early gas-discharge tube, invented by German physicist and glassblower Heinrich Geissler in 1857. It consists of a sealed, partially evacuated glass tube — often blown into ornate spirals and shapes — with metal electrodes at each end; when a high voltage is applied, the rarefied gas inside glows.

Construction and operation

Geissler's mercury vacuum pump allowed him to evacuate tubes to pressures earlier workers could not reach, and his skill in glassblowing let him seal platinum electrodes into the glass. Filled with gases such as nitrogen, carbon dioxide or neon-bearing air at low pressure, the tubes produced bright, characteristically coloured glows; nested tubes with fluorescent liquids created striking displays that were exhibited as scientific amusements across Europe.

Influence

Geissler tubes were essential laboratory tools for spectroscopy and for studying electric discharge in gases. They directly led to the Crookes tube, cathode-ray research, J. J. Thomson's discovery of the electron, and ultimately neon signage and fluorescent lighting. Julius Plücker used them to observe cathode rays and their deflection by magnets as early as 1858.
