Parallel postulate

The parallel postulate is the fifth postulate of Euclid's Elements, a statement about parallel lines that is more complex than his other four postulates and whose status puzzled geometers for two thousand years. In Euclid's own formulation: if a straight line falling on two straight lines makes the interior angles on the same side less than two right angles, then the two lines, if produced indefinitely, meet on that side.

Equivalent forms

Many statements are equivalent to the postulate within neutral (absolute) geometry, including Playfair's axiom: through a point not on a given line, at most one line can be drawn parallel to the given line. Other equivalents include the claim that the angles of a triangle sum to 180°, that similar but non-congruent figures exist, and that a rectangle exists.

History of attempted proofs

Because the fifth postulate seemed less self-evident than the others, generations of mathematicians — including Proclus, Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, John Wallis, and Girolamo Saccheri — tried to derive it from the first four. Saccheri's 1733 work developed extensive consequences of denying it, hoping to reach a contradiction, but found none.

Non-Euclidean geometry

In the nineteenth century, János Bolyai, Nikolai Lobachevsky, and (independently and earlier) Carl Friedrich Gauss realized the postulate cannot be proved: consistent geometries exist in which it is false. Denying it yields hyperbolic geometry (many parallels through a point) and, with modification of other assumptions, elliptic geometry (no parallels). Eugenio Beltrami's models (1868) proved the postulate independent of the others. The discovery transformed mathematics and later provided the geometric language of general relativity, in which physical space need not be Euclidean.
