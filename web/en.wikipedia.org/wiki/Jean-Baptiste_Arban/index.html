Joseph Jean-Baptiste Laurent Arban (February 28, 1825 – April 8, 1889) was a French cornet virtuoso, conductor, composer, and pedagogue. He was appointed the first professor of cornet at the Paris Conservatoire, and his Grande méthode complète pour cornet à pistons et de saxhorn (1864) — universally known as the Arban Method — remains the foundational method book for trumpet and cornet players more than 150 years after its publication.

Life and career

Arban was born in Lyon and entered the Paris Conservatoire in 1841, studying trumpet with François Dauverne. He won first prize in 1849 while playing the recently developed cornet à pistons. He built a celebrated career as a soloist and conductor of popular concerts and balls in Paris, worked for a time in Russia as conductor and professor in St. Petersburg, and in 1869 was appointed professor of cornet at the Conservatoire, a post created for him. He also collaborated with the instrument maker Adolphe Sax and conducted early performances and experiments with the saxhorn family.

The Arban Method

The Grande méthode covers every aspect of cornet technique — embouchure, tonguing (including double and triple tonguing), slurring, ornaments, and characteristic studies — and concludes with a set of theme-and-variation solos. Its most famous piece, Arban's variations on "The Carnival of Venice," became a showpiece for generations of brass virtuosos. Revised editions by Edwin Franko Goldman, Claude Gordon, and others kept the method in print throughout the 20th century; brass players commonly call it "the trumpeter's bible."

Compositions

Beyond the method, Arban composed numerous fantasias and variations on operatic themes, polkas, and other salon pieces for cornet and piano, many of them recorded by modern soloists such as Wynton Marsalis, Maurice André, and Håkan Hardenberger, whose performances revived Arban's own music in the concert hall.
