Linear B is a syllabic script that was used for writing Mycenaean Greek, the earliest attested form of the Greek language. It was in use from about 1450 BC, mainly in the archives of the Bronze Age palaces of Crete and mainland Greece, until the collapse of Mycenaean civilization around 1200 BC. Linear B was adapted from the earlier, still undeciphered Linear A script used for the Minoan language.

Decipherment

The script was deciphered in 1952 by the English architect and linguist Michael Ventris, working with the classicist John Chadwick. Ventris built on the groundwork of Alice Kober, who had demonstrated that the script recorded an inflected language, and on Emmett Bennett's standardization of the signary. The decipherment showed that the tablets were written in an archaic form of Greek, pushing the documented history of the language back by several centuries.

The signary

Linear B comprises roughly 87 syllabic signs, most representing open syllables of the form consonant-plus-vowel, together with about 100 ideographic signs representing commodities such as grain, livestock, oil, wine, textiles and weapons. Numerals used a decimal system. The script was written left to right, and words were separated by dots or short strokes.

Corpus and use

Several thousand tablets and inscribed fragments survive, chiefly from Knossos, Pylos, Mycenae, Thebes, Tiryns and Chania. The texts are almost entirely administrative: inventories, land records, personnel lists and records of offerings to deities. They provide the earliest evidence for many Greek gods, including Zeus, Poseidon, Athena and Dionysus.
