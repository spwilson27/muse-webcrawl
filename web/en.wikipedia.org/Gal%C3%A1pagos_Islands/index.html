The Galápagos Islands (Spanish: Islas Galápagos) are an archipelago of volcanic islands in the Pacific Ocean, straddling the equator about 906 km (563 mi) west of continental Ecuador, of which they form a province. The islands are world-famous for their unique endemic wildlife, whose study by Charles Darwin during the 1835 voyage of HMS Beagle contributed decisively to the theory of evolution by natural selection.

Geography and geology

The archipelago consists of 18 major islands, formed by ongoing volcanism above the Galápagos hotspot; several volcanoes, notably on Isabela and Fernandina, remain active. The islands sit near the junction of the Humboldt and Panama currents, giving a surprisingly cool, dry climate for an equatorial location and supporting both tropical and temperate marine species.

Wildlife

Signature species include the Galápagos giant tortoises, after which the islands are named (from an old Spanish word for saddle, echoing their shells); marine iguanas, the world's only sea-going lizards; flightless cormorants; Galápagos penguins; and the finches — "Darwin's finches" — whose varied beaks illustrate adaptive radiation. Isolation has left native wildlife unusually tame but also vulnerable to introduced species.

Conservation

Ecuador created Galápagos National Park in 1959, and UNESCO inscribed the islands among the first World Heritage Sites in 1978. The Charles Darwin Research Station on Santa Cruz coordinates science and eradication of invasive species; the surrounding marine reserve is one of the largest in the world. Tourism is strictly regulated but remains the economic mainstay and a continuing management challenge.
