Abu Simbel is an archaeological site in Nubia, southern Egypt, comprising two massive rock-cut temples built during the reign of Pharaoh Ramesses II of the 19th Dynasty, around 1264 BC. The complex was carved out of the mountainside on the western bank of the Nile, near the border with present-day Sudan, and is best known for the four colossal seated statues of Ramesses II that adorn the facade of the Great Temple.

The temples were intended both as a monument to the pharaoh and his queen, Nefertari, and as a permanent demonstration of Egyptian power to the peoples of Nubia to the south. The smaller temple is dedicated to the goddess Hathor and to Nefertari, an honour rarely granted to a queen.

History

Construction of the temple complex began around 1264 BC and lasted about twenty years. Following its completion, the site gradually faded from memory and was eventually buried under desert sand. In 1813 the Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt discovered the top frieze of the main temple, and in 1817 the Italian explorer Giovanni Belzoni cleared the entrance and entered the interior for the first time in the modern era.

The temples

Great Temple

The Great Temple is dedicated to the gods Amun, Ra-Horakhty and Ptah, as well as to the deified Ramesses II himself. Its facade, about 38 metres wide and 32 metres high, is dominated by four seated colossi of the king, each roughly 20 metres tall. Inside, a series of halls decorated with reliefs celebrating the king's military campaigns, including the Battle of Kadesh, leads to a sanctuary where statues of the four gods sit. Twice a year, around 22 February and 22 October, the rising sun penetrates the sanctuary and illuminates the statues of Amun, Ra-Horakhty and Ramesses, while Ptah, a god associated with darkness, remains partly in shadow.

Small Temple

The Small Temple, dedicated to Hathor and Nefertari, has a facade decorated with six standing statues about 10 metres tall — four of Ramesses and two of Nefertari, unusually sculpted at the same scale as the king.

Relocation

In the 1960s the temples faced submersion beneath the rising waters of Lake Nasser, created by the Aswan High Dam. In an international campaign coordinated by UNESCO between 1964 and 1968, the entire complex was dismantled into large blocks, moved 65 metres higher and 200 metres back from the river, and reassembled on an artificial hill. The operation is regarded as one of the greatest feats of archaeological engineering in the world. Abu Simbel was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1979 as part of the "Nubian Monuments".
