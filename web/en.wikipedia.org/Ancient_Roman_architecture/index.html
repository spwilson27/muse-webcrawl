Ancient Roman architecture

Ancient Roman architecture adopted the external language of classical Greek architecture for its own purposes, which were so different from Greek buildings as to create a new architectural style. The Romans were indebted to their Etruscan neighbours and to the Greeks for much of their early building, but developed distinctive innovations — above all the arch, the vault, the dome, and concrete — that let them construct buildings on an unprecedented scale, a tradition still visible in cities such as Amman (Roman Philadelphia).

Innovations

Roman concrete (opus caementicium), made with volcanic ash (pozzolana), could set even underwater and freed builders from the size limits of cut stone. Combined with the round arch and barrel and groin vaults, it enabled vast interior spaces: basilicas, thermae (bath complexes), amphitheatres, and domed temples.

Building types

  • Pantheon (c. AD 118–128) — its 43.3 m concrete dome remains the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome.
  • Colosseum (AD 70–80) — the Flavian amphitheatre, seating some 50,000 spectators.
  • Basilicas — great halls for law and commerce, later the model for church plans.
  • Aqueducts — such as the Pont du Gard, supplying cities with water over long distances.
  • Insulae and domus — apartment blocks and houses of ordinary and wealthy Romans.

Orders and theory

Romans adapted the Greek orders, adding the Tuscan and Composite, and codified practice in Vitruvius's De architectura, the only substantial architectural treatise to survive from antiquity.

Legacy

Roman architecture profoundly influenced Byzantine, Romanesque, Renaissance, and Neoclassical building, and Roman engineering — roads, bridges, sewers — underpinned urban life for centuries after the empire's fall.
