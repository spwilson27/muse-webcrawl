Krummholz

Krummholz
MeaningGerman: "crooked wood"
HabitatSubalpine and subarctic tree line
Typical speciesSpruce, fir, pine, larch, birch

Krummholz (German for "crooked, bent wood") describes the stunted, wind-deformed trees and shrubs found at the tree line in subalpine and subarctic mountain environments. Constant exposure to freezing, abrasive winds — carrying ice crystals that blast exposed buds and needles — prevents trees from growing upright, so they survive only in the shelter of rocks or in dense, mat-like thickets.

Formation

Growth is killed back on the windward side, producing flagged, one-sided crowns, while branches protected beneath the winter snowpack survive and root where they touch the ground, a process called layering. The result is clonal islands of genetically identical trees that spread slowly across tundra. Species commonly forming krummholz include Engelmann spruce and subalpine fir in the Rocky Mountains, balsam fir in northeastern North America, and mountain pine and larch in the Alps.

Ecological role

Krummholz shelters wildlife, traps snow that feeds streams in spring, and serves as an indicator of climate change: warming temperatures allow upright "released" growth and an advancing tree line in many ranges. Related forms include the elfin forests of exposed coastal and tropical montane sites.
