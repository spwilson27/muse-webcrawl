Utraquism

Utraquism (from the Latin sub utraque specie, meaning "under both kinds") was the principal doctrine of the moderate Hussites of Bohemia in the 15th and 16th centuries: the belief that the laity, as well as the clergy, should receive communion in both kinds — both the bread and the wine — at the Eucharist. Its adherents, the Utraquists or Calixtines (from calix, chalice), adopted the chalice as their emblem, and their demand formed the first of the Four Articles of Prague (1420).

Origins

The practice of giving the chalice to the laity was introduced in Prague in 1414 by Jakoubek of Stříbro, with the support of Jan Hus, whose followers demanded reform of the Church. After Hus's execution at the Council of Constance in 1415, the chalice became the rallying symbol of the Hussite movement. During the Hussite Wars (1419–1434) the Utraquists represented the moderate, largely noble and urban wing, as opposed to the radical Taborites.

Recognition and decline

The Compactata of Basel (1436) conceded communion in both kinds to Bohemians, making Utraquism a legally recognised confession — a remarkable early breach in the religious uniformity of Latin Christendom. The Utraquist Church persisted as the majority church of Bohemia until the Habsburg victory at the Battle of White Mountain in 1620, after which re-Catholicisation suppressed it, and many adherents joined the Unity of the Brethren or went into exile.

Legacy

The chalice remains a symbol of Czech national and religious identity, appearing in the iconography of the Czechoslovak Hussite Church (founded 1920), and the Second Vatican Council's restoration of communion under both kinds in the Roman Catholic Church echoed the Utraquist demand five centuries later.
