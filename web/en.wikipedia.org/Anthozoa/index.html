Anthozoa is a class of marine invertebrates in the phylum Cnidaria that includes the sea anemones, stony corals, soft corals, sea fans and sea pens. The name comes from the Greek words for "flower animals", referring to the flower-like appearance of their polyps. Unlike most other cnidarians, anthozoans have no medusa stage in their life cycle: they exist only as polyps.

Anatomy and life cycle

The anthozoan polyp has a tubular body topped by a mouth surrounded by tentacles armed with stinging cells (nematocysts). The gut cavity is partitioned by vertical sheets of tissue called mesenteries, which increase surface area for digestion and house the gonads. Anthozoans may reproduce sexually, releasing eggs and sperm or brooding larvae called planulae, and asexually by budding or fission; many species are colonial, with polyps connected by living tissue.

Major groups

The class is traditionally divided by symmetry and tentacle number. The Hexacorallia include the stony (reef-building) corals and sea anemones, with anatomy based on multiples of six; the Octocorallia — soft corals, sea fans, sea pens and blue corals — have eightfold symmetry and eight feathery tentacles.

Ecology

Anthozoans are found in all oceans, from tide pools to the deep sea. Reef-building stony corals, which partner with photosynthetic zooxanthellae algae, construct coral reefs that support a large share of marine biodiversity; these ecosystems are threatened by ocean warming, acidification and coral bleaching.
