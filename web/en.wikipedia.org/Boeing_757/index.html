The Boeing 757 is an American narrow-body airliner designed and built by Boeing Commercial Airplanes. Developed alongside the wide-body 767, with which it shares a common type rating for pilots, the twinjet 757 was produced in two fuselage lengths: the original 757-200 and the stretched 757-300. It entered service in 1983 as a replacement for the Boeing 727 on short- and medium-haul routes.

Development

Boeing launched the 757 in 1978 with orders from Eastern Air Lines and British Airways. The aircraft introduced a two-crew glass cockpit, new turbofan engines (the Rolls-Royce RB211 or Pratt & Whitney PW2000), and a supercritical wing, giving it much better fuel efficiency than the trijet 727 it replaced. Production ran from 1981 to 2004 at Boeing's Renton, Washington factory.

Operational history

The 757's high thrust-to-weight ratio and short-field performance made it popular for "hot and high" airports and long, thin routes. Extended-range (ETOPS) certification allowed it to fly transatlantic services, and it became a mainstay of U.S. legacy carriers and charter and cargo operators. A freighter version, the 757-200PF, and converted freighters remain in widespread cargo service with carriers such as FedEx Express and UPS Airlines. Military derivatives include the C-32, used as Air Force Two.

Variants

The 757-200 seats 200–228 passengers with a range of about 3,915 nautical miles (7,250 km). The stretched 757-300, launched in 1996, carries up to 280 passengers but with a shorter range. No direct Boeing replacement has been produced; airlines have generally replaced 757s with 737 MAX and Airbus A321neo family aircraft, including the long-range A321XLR.
