Phobia

A phobia is an anxiety disorder defined by an intense, persistent and excessive fear of an object, situation, activity or feeling, which the person recognises as irrational but feels compelled to avoid. Exposure to the feared stimulus provokes an immediate anxiety response, which can take the form of panic; the avoidance or endurance with distress significantly interferes with normal life. The word derives from the Greek phóbos, meaning fear or horror.

Classification

The DSM-5 classifies phobias under anxiety disorders in three broad groups: specific phobias (of animals, heights, blood and injections, flying, enclosed places and others), social anxiety disorder (social phobia, fear of scrutiny and embarrassment), and agoraphobia (fear of situations from which escape might be difficult). Specific phobias are among the most common anxiety disorders, with lifetime prevalence estimates around 7–9% in many populations.

Causes

Phobias are thought to arise from a combination of genetic vulnerability, temperament, traumatic or observational learning experiences, and evolved preparedness to fear certain stimuli such as snakes, spiders, heights and blood. Classical conditioning and information transmission (warnings, media) also contribute, particularly in childhood.

Treatment

The most effective treatment is exposure-based cognitive behavioural therapy, in which the person gradually and repeatedly confronts the feared stimulus in a safe way (exposure therapy, including systematic desensitisation), weakening the fear response. Virtual-reality exposure and one-session intensive treatments are used for some phobias. Medication such as benzodiazepines or antidepressants is sometimes used adjunctively, particularly short term, though effects are generally less durable than exposure therapy.

Common phobias

  • Arachnophobia — spiders
  • Acrophobia — heights
  • Claustrophobia — enclosed spaces
  • Aviophobia — flying
  • Trypanophobia — injections and needles
  • Dentophobia — dentists and dental procedures
