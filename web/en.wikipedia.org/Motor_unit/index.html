Motor unit

A motor unit is made up of a motor neuron and all of the skeletal muscle fibers it innervates. Groups of motor units often work together as a motor pool to coordinate the contractions of a single muscle. The concept was proposed by Charles Scott Sherrington.

Recruitment

When a motor unit is activated, all of its fibres contract. The force of a muscle contraction is graded by recruiting more motor units and by increasing their firing rate. According to Henneman's size principle, motor units are recruited from smallest to largest as the required force increases: small, fatigue-resistant units maintain posture, while large, powerful but fatigable units are reserved for brief, forceful efforts.

Types

Motor units are commonly classed as slow (S), fast fatigue-resistant (FR), fast fatigable (FF), and fast intermediate (FI), corresponding to the fibre types they innervate. Muscles controlling fine movements, such as those of the eye and hand, have small motor units with few fibres per neuron; large postural muscles may have thousands of fibres per unit.

Clinical significance

Diseases of motor neurons, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and poliomyelitis, destroy motor units, and surviving units may enlarge by reinnervating orphaned fibres — a process visible on electromyography.
