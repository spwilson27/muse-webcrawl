Miosis

Miosis is excessive constriction of the pupil of the eye. It is the opposite of mydriasis (dilation of the pupil) and can be a normal physiological response to bright light, near focus or sleep, or a sign of disease or drug effect. Extremely small pupils are often described as "pinpoint pupils".

Physiology

Pupil size is controlled by two muscles of the iris: the sphincter pupillae, driven by the parasympathetic nervous system via the oculomotor nerve, constricts the pupil, while the dilator pupillae, driven by the sympathetic system, dilates it. Miosis results from sphincter contraction or from loss of dilator tone.

Causes

Physiological causes include the pupillary light reflex, the near reflex during accommodation, and drowsiness. Pathological and pharmacological causes include Horner's syndrome, in which interruption of sympathetic supply produces a small pupil with ptosis and anhidrosis; neurosyphilis, which can produce Argyll Robertson pupils; iritis and other eye injuries; and drugs — above all opioids such as morphine and heroin, and cholinergic agents including organophosphate insecticides and nerve agents, whose poisoning triad includes miosis, salivation and bronchospasm. Pilocarpine drops are used deliberately to constrict the pupil in the treatment of glaucoma.

Diagnosis and treatment

Comparing the pupils in bright and dim light helps distinguish sympathetic from parasympathetic lesions, and pharmacological testing with agents such as apraclonidine or cocaine drops can confirm Horner's syndrome. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause.
