SpecialtyGastroenterology, surgery, pulmonology
InstrumentEndoscope (rigid or flexible, with camera and light)
Common typesGastroscopy, colonoscopy, bronchoscopy, cystoscopy, laparoscopy

Endoscopy is a procedure used in medicine to look inside the body. An endoscope — a long, thin, flexible (or rigid) tube with a light and camera — is inserted through a natural opening such as the mouth or anus, or through a small surgical incision, to examine internal organs and cavities directly. Images are displayed on a monitor, and instruments can be passed through a working channel to take biopsies, remove polyps, stop bleeding or perform other interventions.

Types

  • Gastroscopy (upper GI endoscopy) — oesophagus, stomach and duodenum
  • Colonoscopy and sigmoidoscopy — colon and rectum
  • Bronchoscopy — airways and lungs
  • Cystoscopy — bladder and urethra
  • Laparoscopy — abdominal cavity through small incisions
  • Arthroscopy — interior of joints
  • ERCP and endoscopic ultrasound — bile and pancreatic ducts and deeper imaging
  • Capsule endoscopy — a swallowed camera pill that images the small intestine

History

The first practical endoscope was developed by Philipp Bozzini in 1806 using candlelight and mirrors. Antoine Jean Desormeaux introduced an improved instrument in the 1850s, and in 1868 Adolf Kussmaul performed early gastroscopy. Flexible fibre-optic endoscopes, developed in the 1950s–60s following work by Basil Hirschowitz, transformed the field; video endoscopes with CCD sensors followed in the 1980s.

Risks

Endoscopy is generally safe but carries small risks including bleeding, perforation of the organ wall, infection, and reactions to sedation.
