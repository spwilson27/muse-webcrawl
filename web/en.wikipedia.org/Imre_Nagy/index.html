Imre Nagy (Hungarian: [ˈimrɛ ˈnɒɟ]; 7 June 1896 – 16 June 1958) was a Hungarian communist politician who served as Prime Minister and Chairman of the Council of Ministers of the Hungarian People's Republic from 1953 to 1955 and again in 1956. Nagy became leader of the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 against the Soviet-backed government, for which he was sentenced to death and executed two years later.

Born to a peasant family, Nagy was apprenticed as a locksmith before being drafted into the Austro-Hungarian Army during the First World War. Captured by the Russians, he joined the Bolsheviks and fought in the Russian Civil War. Returning to Hungary in 1921, he worked as an underground communist organiser and spent much of the interwar period in Moscow, where he studied agriculture. After the Second World War he served as Minister of Agriculture in Hungary's post-war government, overseeing the land reform of 1945.

First premiership and the New Course

Following the death of Joseph Stalin in March 1953, the Soviet leadership pressed the Hungarian party leader Mátyás Rákosi to yield the premiership. Nagy became prime minister on 4 July 1953 and announced a "New Course" (Új szakasz): he slowed forced industrialisation and collectivisation, raised living standards, released many political prisoners and closed internment camps. Rákosi, who remained party first secretary, undermined the programme, and in April 1955 Nagy was removed and expelled from the party, accused of "rightist deviation".

Role in the 1956 Revolution

As discontent grew in the autumn of 1956, reformist students and intellectuals demanded Nagy's return. On 23 October 1956, demonstrations in Budapest escalated into a nationwide uprising after security police fired on crowds. Nagy was reappointed prime minister on 24 October. Over the following days he formed a coalition government including non-communists, abolished the one-party system, announced a ceasefire and the withdrawal of Soviet troops from Budapest, and on 1 November declared Hungary's neutrality and withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact, appealing to the United Nations for help.

On 4 November 1956 Soviet forces launched Operation Whirlwind and crushed the revolution. Nagy took refuge in the Yugoslav embassy; on 22 November he was seized by Soviet agents despite a written safe-conduct guarantee and deported to Romania.

Trial, execution and rehabilitation

Nagy was returned to Hungary and tried in secret in June 1958. Refusing to plead for mercy, he was convicted of treason and hanged on 16 June 1958; his body was buried face-down in an unmarked grave. The writer and minister Géza Losonczy and journalist Miklós Gimes were executed with him, and Defence Minister Pál Maléter was executed the same day.

On 16 June 1989, the 31st anniversary of his execution, Nagy and his associates were ceremonially reburied in Budapest before a crowd estimated at a quarter of a million people, in one of the symbolic milestones of the end of communist rule in Hungary. Hungary's Supreme Court formally annulled his conviction in July 1989.
