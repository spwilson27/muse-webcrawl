Drainage divide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A drainage divide, water divide, ridgeline, watershed, water parting or height of land is elevated terrain that separates neighbouring drainage basins. On rugged topography, the divide lies along topographical ridges and may form a single range of hills or mountains, known as a dividing range.

Types and examples

Continental divides are the most significant: the Continental Divide of the Americas runs along the Rockies and Andes, separating rivers flowing to the Pacific from those flowing to the Atlantic and Arctic oceans. A triple divide is a point where three basins meet — Triple Divide Peak in Montana sheds water to the Pacific, Atlantic and Hudson Bay. In flat terrain such as deltas and marshes, divides can be indistinct, and streams may bifurcate, flowing to two basins at once.

Significance

Because ridges have historically formed defensible boundaries and obstacles to transport, drainage divides often coincide with political borders — a principle embodied in 19th-century "ridgeline" boundary treaties in the Himalayas and elsewhere. Divides matter for water management, since they define the catchment from which a river system draws its water, and crossing them has driven major engineering works such as canals and tunnels (for example the trans-Alpine tunnels and the Panama Canal's crossing of the continental divide).
