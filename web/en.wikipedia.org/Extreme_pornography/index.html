Extreme pornography

Extreme pornography is a legal term introduced in the United Kingdom by sections 63–67 of the Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008, which made it an offence in England and Wales to possess an "extreme pornographic image". Scotland adopted a similar offence in the Criminal Justice and Licensing (Scotland) Act 2010.

The offence

An image qualifies if it is pornographic — produced solely or principally for sexual arousal — and portrays, in an explicit and realistic way, acts that threaten a person's life, acts likely to result in serious injury to certain body parts, sexual interference with a human corpse, or intercourse with an animal, and a reasonable person would think the persons or animals shown were real. Defences include legitimate reasons for possession, not having seen the image or known its nature, unsolicited receipt, and participation in the events depicted. The maximum sentence in England and Wales is three years' imprisonment (five years for the most serious categories), and offenders may be placed on the sex offenders register.

Background and debate

The law followed a government consultation prompted in part by campaigning after the murder of Jane Longhurst by Graham Coutts, who was said to have consumed violent pornography. Civil liberties groups and parts of the adult industry criticised the law as vague and liable to criminalise depictions of consensual activity and material from horror films; early prosecutions included cases involving staged images and computer-generated content, one of which — the 2012 trial involving a joke image of a tiger — collapsed amid controversy. Similar definitional questions arise in other jurisdictions' obscenity law, though few countries have adopted a possession offence framed in these terms.
