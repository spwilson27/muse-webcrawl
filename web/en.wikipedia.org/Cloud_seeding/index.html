Cloud seeding

Cloud seeding is a type of weather modification that aims to change the amount or type of precipitation that falls from clouds by dispersing substances into the air that serve as cloud condensation or ice nuclei. The most common chemicals used are silver iodide, potassium iodide, dry ice (solid carbon dioxide), and hygroscopic salts such as calcium chloride. Seeding is carried out from aircraft or from ground-based generators and rocket launchers.

History

In 1946 Vincent Schaefer of the General Electric Research Laboratory found that dropping dry ice into a supercooled cloud produced snow, and his colleague Bernard Vonnegut identified silver iodide, whose crystalline structure resembles ice, as an effective ice-nucleating agent. The first field experiments, including Project Cirrus (1947), prompted widespread commercial and government programmes in the following decades, as well as military operations such as Operation Popeye during the Vietnam War, which sought to extend the monsoon over supply routes and helped spur the 1977 ENMOD convention banning hostile environmental modification.

Methods

Glaciogenic seeding introduces ice nuclei into supercooled clouds so that ice crystals grow at the expense of liquid droplets and fall as snow or rain. Hygroscopic seeding, used in warm clouds, disperses salt particles that attract water and accelerate droplet coalescence. Delivery is by burn-in-place or ejectable flares on aircraft, ground generators in mountainous terrain, or anti-aircraft artillery and rockets in some national programmes.

Uses and programmes

Cloud seeding is practised in dozens of countries to augment rainfall and snowpack — notably winter orographic programmes in the western United States that feed reservoirs — to suppress damaging hail in agricultural regions, to disperse fog at airports, and, in China, Russia, and the United Arab Emirates, as part of large state weather-modification efforts. China's weather modification office is among the world's largest such organisations.

Effectiveness and criticism

The effectiveness of seeding has long been debated because natural precipitation is highly variable and difficult to attribute. A 2017–era series of experiments in Idaho (SNOWIE) produced the first unambiguous radar and gauge evidence that seeding can generate precipitation that would not otherwise have fallen, though gains are generally estimated in the modest range of a few percent to about 15% under favourable conditions. Environmental assessments generally find the silver iodide quantities involved to be small relative to natural background levels, but critics continue to question cost-effectiveness, downwind effects, and governance.
