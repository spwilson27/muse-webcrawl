Carbide lamp

A carbide lamp, or acetylene gas lamp, is a simple lamp that produces and burns acetylene (C2H2) created by the reaction of calcium carbide (CaC2) with water. It was widely used by miners, cavers, cyclists, and motorists in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

Operation

The lamp has two chambers: water in the upper chamber drips onto calcium carbide in the lower chamber, generating acetylene gas, which is burned at a jet, often in front of a polished reflector. The drip rate — and thus the flame size — is adjusted by a valve. The flame is remarkably bright and wind-resistant, but the lamps require frequent cleaning, produce soot, and can leak flammable gas.

History

Commercial calcium carbide production began in the 1890s, and carbide lamps spread rapidly through mining, where their flame also warned of gas conditions. Electric cap lamps largely replaced them in mining by the mid-twentieth century, but cavers continued using carbide lamps into the 1990s for their warmth, reliability, and long burn time before LED lights took over.
