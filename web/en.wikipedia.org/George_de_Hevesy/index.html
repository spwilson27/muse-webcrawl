George Charles de Hevesy (August 1, 1885 – July 5, 1966) was a Hungarian radiochemist and Nobel laureate, recognised in 1943 for his key role in developing radioactive tracers to study chemical processes such as in the metabolism of animals.

Career

Hevesy studied chemistry in Budapest and Freiburg and worked with Ernest Rutherford in Manchester. With Friedrich Paneth he developed the radioactive tracer method, first applied in 1913. In Copenhagen, working with Dirk Coster, he discovered the element hafnium in 1923, named for the Latin name of Copenhagen.

War years

When German troops occupied Denmark in 1940, Hevesy famously dissolved the gold Nobel Prize medals of Max von Laue and James Franck in aqua regia to prevent their confiscation, storing the solution on a shelf at the Niels Bohr Institute; the gold was later recovered and the medals recast. He fled to Sweden in 1943 and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry that year.

Later life

Hevesy continued research in Stockholm on tracer applications in biology and medicine before retiring to Freiburg, where he died in 1966.
