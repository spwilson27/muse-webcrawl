Speed

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In everyday use and in kinematics, the speed of an object is the magnitude of the change of its position over time; it is thus a scalar quantity. The average speed of an object in an interval of time is the distance travelled by the object divided by the duration of the interval; the instantaneous speed is the limit of the average speed as the duration of the time interval approaches zero. Speed is the magnitude of velocity, which additionally specifies direction.

v = d / t

Units

The SI unit of speed is the metre per second (m/s). Other common units include kilometres per hour (km/h), miles per hour (mph), and the knot for maritime and aviation use. The speed of light in vacuum, c = 299,792,458 m/s, is a fundamental constant and the upper limit for the speed at which information and matter can travel, according to special relativity.

Examples

A walking human moves at roughly 1.4 m/s (5 km/h); the fastest sprinters reach about 12 m/s over short bursts. A cheetah can reach speeds near 29 m/s (104 km/h). Passenger aircraft cruise around 250 m/s, and the speed of sound in air at sea level is about 343 m/s, with speeds relative to it expressed in Mach numbers. Earth orbits the Sun at about 29.8 km/s.

Speed and velocity

Unlike velocity, which is a vector, speed ignores direction: a car travelling around a circular track at constant speed nevertheless has a changing velocity and hence an acceleration. Tangential speed in rotational motion equals the angular speed multiplied by the radius.
