Maglev

Maglev (short for magnetic levitation) is a system of train transportation in which a vehicle levitates above a guideway using the repulsive and attractive forces of magnets, eliminating rolling friction. Propelled by linear motors, maglev trains can reach very high speeds and accelerate quickly, though they require dedicated infrastructure incompatible with conventional rail.

Technology

Two main approaches are used. Electromagnetic suspension (EMS), developed in Germany as Transrapid, uses electromagnets on the train that are attracted upward toward a ferromagnetic rail. Electrodynamic suspension (EDS), used in Japan's SCMaglev, uses superconducting magnets and induced currents in the guideway to produce repulsive levitation at speed. Levitation is closely related to the physics of the magnet and the fields it produces.

Commercial systems

The Shanghai maglev train, opened in 2004 using Transrapid technology, connects Pudong International Airport with the city and remains the fastest commercial electric train service, reaching 431 km/h (268 mph) in normal operation, though its operating speed has at times been reduced. Shorter commercial lines operate in Japan (Linimo), South Korea (Incheon Airport), and China (including the Beijing S1 and Changsha lines).

Speed records and future projects

Japan's SCMaglev holds the world speed record for trains at 603 km/h (375 mph), set in 2015, and is being built for the Chūō Shinkansen line between Tokyo and Nagoya. Proposed systems elsewhere have repeatedly stalled over cost, which is typically far higher per kilometre than conventional high-speed rail.
