Pangu

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pangu (Chinese: 盤古; pinyin: Pángǔ) is a primordial being and creation figure in Chinese mythology and Taoism. According to the myth, Pangu emerged from a cosmic egg and separated heaven from earth, establishing the ordered world; when he died, parts of his body transformed into the features of the world and, in some versions, into humankind.

The myth

In the beginning, the universe was a formless chaos contained within an egg. Inside slept Pangu. After 18,000 years he awoke and stretched, breaking the egg: its lighter parts rose to become the heavens (yang) and its heavier parts sank to form the earth (yin). Fearing that heaven and earth might reunite, Pangu stood between them, pushing the sky upward. Each day the sky rose ten feet, the earth sank ten feet, and Pangu grew ten feet; this labour continued for another 18,000 years until heaven and earth were fixed far apart.

When Pangu died, his body became the world: his breath became the wind and clouds, his voice the thunder, his left eye the sun and his right eye the moon, his limbs the mountains at the four quarters, his blood the rivers, his hair the stars and vegetation, his flesh the soil, his bones and teeth the rocks and metals, and the parasites on his body — blown by the wind — became human beings.

Sources

The myth is first recorded in texts of the Three Kingdoms period, notably the Sanwu Liji ("Historical Records of the Three Sovereign Divinities and the Five Gods") attributed to Xu Zheng in the third century CE. Later works, including the Wuyun Linian Ji, expanded the story. The tale is not found in the earliest Chinese classics, and scholars debate whether it developed within China or was influenced by southern minority peoples or foreign traditions; Pangu remains venerated in some regions, with temples dedicated to him, and the story is a staple of Chinese creation mythology.
