Hypercalcaemia

Calcium.

Hypercalcaemia, also spelled hypercalcemia, is a high calcium (Ca2+) level in the blood serum. The normal range is 2.1–2.6 mmol/L (8.8–10.7 mg/dL, 4.3–5.2 mEq/L), with levels greater than 2.6 mmol/L defined as hypercalcaemia. Those with a mild increase that has developed slowly typically have no symptoms. In those with greater levels or a rapid onset, symptoms may include abdominal pain, bone pain, confusion, depression, weakness, kidney stones, or an abnormal heart rhythm including cardiac arrest.

Causes

Most outpatient cases are due to primary hyperparathyroidism, in which excess parathyroid hormone causes calcium release from bone, increased intestinal absorption, and reduced renal excretion. In hospitalised patients, malignancy is the most common cause, through secretion of parathyroid hormone-related protein (humoral hypercalcaemia of malignancy), local osteolysis from metastases or myeloma, or production of calcitriol. Other causes include vitamin D excess, granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis, thyrotoxicosis, immobilisation, thiazide diuretics, lithium, and milk-alkali syndrome.

Signs and symptoms

The classic mnemonic is "stones, bones, abdominal groans, thrones, and psychiatric overtones": nephrolithiasis and polyuria; bone pain and osteoporosis; constipation, nausea, and peptic symptoms; frequent urination ("thrones"); and depression, confusion, or fatigue. Severe hypercalcaemia is a medical emergency because of the risk of arrhythmia and coma.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis involves a corrected (albumin-adjusted) or ionised calcium level and a parathyroid hormone level, which separates PTH-mediated from non-PTH-mediated causes. Treatment of acute hypercalcaemia begins with aggressive intravenous saline rehydration, followed by bisphosphonates (such as zoledronic acid) and calcitonin; glucocorticoids help in vitamin D-mediated cases, and cinacalcet or parathyroid surgery address hyperparathyroidism.
