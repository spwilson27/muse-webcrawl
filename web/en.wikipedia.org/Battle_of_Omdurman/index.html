The Battle of Omdurman was fought on 2 September 1898 outside Omdurman, the capital of Mahdist Sudan, between an Anglo-Egyptian army commanded by Major-General Herbert Kitchener and the forces of the Khalifa Abdallahi ibn Muhammad, successor to the Mahdi, Muhammad Ahmad. The battle ended the Mahdist War: the Khalifa's army of some 50,000 men was destroyed by the firepower of Kitchener's modern army — magazine rifles, Maxim guns, artillery and gunboats — at astonishingly unequal cost. The victory avenged the death of General Gordon at Khartoum in 1885 and opened the way to the Anglo-Egyptian condominium over Sudan. A young cavalry officer present, Winston Churchill, later wrote a famous account in The River War.

Background

Kitchener, Sirdar (commander) of the Egyptian army, had advanced methodically up the Nile since 1896, building a military railway across the Nubian desert to supply his force and defeating the Mahdists at Atbara in April 1898. In late August his army of 25,800, supported by a flotilla of gunboats, encamped at Egeiga near Omdurman, behind a thorn-fence zariba.

The battle

At dawn on 2 September the Mahdist army advanced in five divisions against the zariba. Massed rifle and Maxim-gun fire, with artillery from the gunboats on the Nile, shattered the attacks before they closed; few attackers reached within a few hundred metres of the line. Kitchener then advanced on Omdurman — prematurely, as it proved: a large uncommitted Mahdist force under Osman Azraq and the Khalifa's reserve attacked the advancing troops, and the 21st Lancers, charging what appeared to be a small party of spearmen, rode into a concealed depression holding some 2,000 warriors, losing a quarter of their number in minutes before dismounting and fighting clear. A brigade under Hector MacDonald, isolated on the flank, beat off a desperate attack by the Khalifa's Black Flag division, calmly changing front under fire in one of the most admired feats of arms of the colonial era. By midday the Mahdist army was in full retreat; the Khalifa fled south, to be run to ground and killed at Umm Diwaykarat in November 1899.

Aftermath

Mahdist casualties were around 11,000–12,000 killed; Anglo-Egyptian losses were under 50 killed. The lopsided result, summarised bitterly by Hilaire Belloc's contemporary couplet about the Maxim gun, became a symbol of the technological gulf of late nineteenth-century colonial warfare, and the treatment of Mahdist wounded and the desecration of the Mahdi's tomb provoked controversy in Britain. Sudan was reconquered and administered from 1899 as an Anglo-Egyptian condominium. Among the British dead was none better known at the time than the battle's fame made its chronicler: Churchill's The River War (1899) remains a classic.
