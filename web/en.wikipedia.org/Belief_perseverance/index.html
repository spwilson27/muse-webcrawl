Belief perseverance

Belief perseverance (also known as conceptual conservatism) is maintaining a belief despite new information that firmly contradicts it. Such beliefs may even be strengthened when others attempt to present evidence debunking them, a phenomenon known as the backfire effect (also called the boomerang effect). For example, journalist Dan Gardner wrote on the effect in relation to beliefs concerning 9/11 conspiracy theories. Because the beliefs in question typically do not have a meaningful impact on most people's immediate lives, they are "fairly harmless", and all people naturally display some degree of belief perseverance, as well as the ability to change beliefs given sound reasoning.

Experimental evidence

In a classic study by Ross, Lepper and Hubbard (1975), participants formed impressions of their own and others' ability to distinguish authentic suicide notes from fakes, based on feedback that was later revealed to be entirely fabricated. Even after full debriefing, participants' self-impressions and impressions of others persisted. In Lord, Ross and Lepper's 1979 study, supporters and opponents of capital punishment both rated studies confirming their views as more convincing than studies disconfirming them, and exposure to mixed evidence polarised their attitudes further.

Mechanisms

Belief perseverance is thought to arise because people generate causal explanations that link evidence to their beliefs; once the explanation exists, it can stand even when the original evidence is withdrawn. Related processes include confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, and the reluctance to construct a new mental model of a topic.

Consequences and remedies

The effect underlies resistance to corrective information in politics, health, and science communication. Techniques such as "consider the opposite", perspective-taking, and explaining alternative outcomes have been shown to reduce perseverance in experimental settings, although large-scale replication work on the backfire effect suggests corrections usually do move beliefs, even if imperfectly.
