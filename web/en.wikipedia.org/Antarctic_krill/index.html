OrderEuphausiacea

Antarctic krill (Euphausia superba) is a species of krill — a small, shrimp-like crustacean — found in the cold waters of the Southern Ocean around Antarctica. It is one of the most abundant animal species on Earth by biomass and a keystone species of the Antarctic ecosystem: huge swarms of krill feed on phytoplankton and in turn sustain most of the region's whales, seals, penguins, seabirds, squid and fish.

Description and life cycle

Adults reach about 6 cm in length. Like other krill they are largely transparent, with large black eyes and a reddish shell, and possess light-producing organs (photophores) whose function may include camouflage by counter-illumination. Females spawn thousands of eggs in summer; the eggs sink to deep water before the larvae hatch and rise through several developmental stages to the surface. Larvae depend on algae growing on the underside of winter sea ice, so sea-ice extent strongly influences recruitment. Krill can shrink in size and survive long periods without food in winter.

Ecology

Krill gather in swarms that may extend for kilometres and contain millions of individuals, making them accessible to baleen whales — including blue whales, which consume several tonnes of krill per day — and to crabeater seals, the most numerous large mammal dependent on them. By grazing phytoplankton and producing fast-sinking faecal pellets, krill also play a major role in the biological carbon pump of the Southern Ocean, whose upwelling is driven by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current.

Fishery and conservation

A commercial fishery harvests Antarctic krill for aquaculture feed, omega-3 oil supplements and other products; catches are regulated by the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR), which sets precautionary limits of a few hundred thousand tonnes per year. Concerns focus on the concentration of fishing near predator colonies and on the effects of ocean warming and sea-ice loss on krill populations, with wide-ranging consequences for the whole Antarctic food web.
