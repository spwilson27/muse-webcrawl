Deluge (history)

The Deluge (Polish: Potop szwedzki) was a series of mid-17th-century campaigns in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, principally the Swedish invasion of 1655–1660 during the Second Northern War, overlapping with a Russian invasion (1654–1667) and the Khmelnytsky Uprising.

Course

Charles X Gustav invaded in 1655; much of the nobility defected at Ujście and Kėdainiai, and Warsaw and Kraków fell. King John II Casimir fled to Silesia. Resistance rallied after the successful defence of Jasna Góra monastery (1655) and the Lwów Oaths (1656); Stefan Czarniecki waged guerrilla war. The Confederation of Tyszowce and a Polish–Tatar alliance recovered most territory.

Consequences

Population losses from war, plague and famine are estimated in the millions — perhaps a third of the population. Towns, castles and cultural treasures were looted and taken to Sweden. By the Treaty of Oliva (1660) John II Casimir renounced claims to the Swedish throne; the Treaty of Wehlau (1657) freed Prussia from Polish suzerainty in exchange for Brandenburg's alliance — a long-term strategic loss. The Deluge marks the Commonwealth's turning from great power to decline.
