Early music revival
Period: 19th century – present
Key figures: Arnold Dolmetsch, Wanda Landowska, Nikolaus Harnoncourt, Gustav Leonhardt
Focus: Historically informed performance of medieval, Renaissance and Baroque music

The early music revival is the renewed interest, beginning in the 19th century and greatly expanding in the 20th, in performing music of the medieval, Renaissance and Baroque eras, typically in accordance with historically informed performance practice — using period instruments (or copies), original performance conventions, and research into historical sources.

Early music ensembles perform on period instruments such as the harpsichord.

Origins

Felix Mendelssohn's 1829 revival of Bach's St Matthew Passion, a century after its composition, is often taken as a landmark in the rediscovery of earlier music. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Arnold Dolmetsch in England built and played period instruments and promoted domestic performance of old music, while harpsichordist Wanda Landowska championed the instrument in recitals and teaching.

Post-war expansion

After the Second World War the movement flourished. Noah Greenberg's New York Pro Musica (founded 1952), Nikolaus Harnoncourt's Concentus Musicus Wien (1953), and ensembles led by Gustav Leonhardt, Frans Brüggen, and later John Eliot Gardiner, Christopher Hogwood and Trevor Pinnock brought period-instrument performances of Baroque and Classical repertoire into the mainstream of recording and concert life. Specialist labels, festivals and university early music programmes spread the approach.

Debate

The quest for "authenticity" has been debated by scholars and critics, who question how fully historical practices can be recovered and whether period performance reflects modern tastes as much as historical ones. Nevertheless, historically informed approaches have profoundly influenced the performance of music from Bach and Handel to Mozart and Beethoven on modern instruments as well.
