A cytotoxic T cell (also known as a Tc cell, killer T cell, or CD8+ T cell) is a T lymphocyte that kills cancer cells, cells infected with intracellular pathogens (such as viruses or bacteria), and cells damaged in other ways. Most cytotoxic T cells express T-cell receptors (TCRs) that recognise a specific antigen presented by MHC class I molecules, which are found on nearly all nucleated cells, together with the CD8 glycoprotein that binds to a conserved portion of the MHC molecule.

Development

Cytotoxic T cells develop in the thymus, where they undergo positive and negative selection. Mature naïve CD8+ T cells circulate in the blood and lymphoid organs until they encounter their cognate antigen presented by a dendritic cell in a secondary lymphoid organ. Activation, which usually requires co-stimulation and cytokines such as interleukin-2, drives clonal expansion and differentiation into effector cells and long-lived memory cells.

Mechanisms of killing

Effector cytotoxic T cells kill targets mainly by releasing cytotoxic granules containing perforin, which forms pores in the target cell membrane, and granzymes, serine proteases that enter through the pores and trigger apoptosis. They can also kill via the Fas–FasL pathway, in which Fas ligand on the T cell engages Fas on the target and activates caspases. Cytotoxic T cells additionally secrete cytokines such as interferon-γ and tumour necrosis factor that inhibit viral replication and activate macrophages.

Clinical relevance

Cytotoxic T cells are central to control of viral infections and to tumour immunosurveillance. Their activity is exploited by cancer immunotherapies, including checkpoint inhibitors and adoptive cell therapies using tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes or CAR T cells (though the latter are often CD4+ or mixed populations). Conversely, inappropriate cytotoxic responses contribute to some autoimmune diseases and to transplant rejection.
