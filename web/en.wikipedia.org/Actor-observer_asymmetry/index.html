Actor–observer asymmetry

Actor–observer asymmetry (also called actor–observer bias or the actor–observer difference) is a bias in social psychology concerning how people explain behaviour. Actors tend to attribute their own actions to situational factors, whereas observers tend to attribute the same actions to the actor's stable disposition or personality. The asymmetry was proposed by Edward E. Jones and Richard Nisbett in 1971.

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. Actors have access to information about how their own behaviour varies across situations, while observers see only a single episode. Perceptually, actors focus on the situation they confront, whereas observers focus on the actor, who is the salient figure in the scene. Linguistic and cultural factors also play a role; the asymmetry has been found to be weaker or absent in some collectivist cultures.

Relation to other biases

The actor–observer asymmetry is related to the fundamental attribution error — the tendency to overemphasise dispositional explanations for others' behaviour — and to the self-serving bias. Meta-analyses have found that the classic asymmetry is strongest for negative events and is moderated by how well the observer knows the actor.
