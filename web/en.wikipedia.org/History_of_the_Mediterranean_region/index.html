History of the Mediterranean region

The history of the Mediterranean region and of the cultures and people of the Mediterranean Basin is the history of the interaction of the cultures around the Mediterranean Sea — the cradle of many of the world's earliest civilisations. Fernand Braudel's classic study The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949) treated the sea as a unifying historical stage shaped by geography, trade and climate.

Earliest civilisations

The eastern Mediterranean saw the first agricultural societies and cities of the Fertile Crescent. Egypt along the Nile, the Phoenicians of the Levantine coast, the Minoans of Crete and the Mycenaeans of Greece traded metals, grain, wine and oil across the sea. Around 1200 BC the Late Bronze Age collapse destroyed many palace economies. The Phoenicians then spread alphabetic writing and colonies — Carthage, Cádiz, and outposts in Sicily and Sardinia — across the basin.

Classical antiquity

From the 8th century BC Greek city-states founded colonies from Spain to the Black Sea. The Greco-Persian Wars (499–449 BC) checked Persian expansion; Athens built a maritime empire, and Alexander the Great's conquests made Greek the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean. Hellenistic kingdoms — Ptolemaic Egypt, the Seleucid Empire — patronised great cities such as Alexandria and Antioch.

The Roman lake

Rome's victories over Carthage in the Punic Wars (264–146 BC) and its conquests of the Hellenistic east left the entire sea under one power by the 1st century BC: the Romans called it mare nostrum, "our sea". Roman rule brought centuries of internal peace, suppression of piracy, common currency and law, and bulk shipping of grain from Egypt and North Africa to the capital. Christianity, born in Roman Judaea, spread along sea routes and became the empire's official religion in the 4th century AD.

The medieval Mediterranean

The empire's division and the fall of the West (5th century) fractured Mediterranean unity. The Byzantine Empire held the east; the Arab conquests of the 7th–8th centuries brought Islam to the Levant, Egypt, North Africa and Iberia, dividing the sea between Christian and Muslim shores, though trade and translation of Greek learning continued across it. The Crusades (1096–1291) briefly planted Latin states in the Levant. Meanwhile Venice, Genoa and later Pisa built maritime empires on trade in spices, silk and slaves, and the Italian Renaissance grew from their wealth.

Ottoman ascendancy and the Atlantic turn

The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople (1453) and of Egypt and the Levant (1516–17) made the eastern Mediterranean an Ottoman domain; the Battle of Lepanto (1571) checked Ottoman sea power but did not reverse it. Meanwhile, the voyages of Columbus (1492) and da Gama (1498) shifted the centre of world trade from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic, beginning a long relative decline of the region.

Modern era

Napoleon's Egyptian expedition (1798) opened a century of European penetration. The opening of the Suez Canal (1869) restored the Mediterranean's role as the artery between Europe and Asia. European empires took control of North Africa and the Levant; after the Ottoman collapse in 1918, Britain and France held mandates there. The Second World War was fought across the sea (Malta, North Africa, Italy). Decolonisation, the Arab–Israeli conflict, the Suez Crisis (1956), and later mass tourism, migration across the sea and the European Union's Mediterranean policies have shaped the region to the present day.
