Schlieffen Plan

The Schlieffen Plan (German: Schlieffen-Plan, pronounced [ʃliːfən plaːn]) is a name given to German war plans at the beginning of the First World War. It is named after its principal architect, Field Marshal Count Alfred von Schlieffen, chief of the German General Staff from 1891 to 1906. The plan called for a rapid, overwhelming attack on France through the Low Countries, enveloping Paris and destroying the French army within about six weeks, before turning east to face the slower-mobilizing Russian Empire.

Background

Following the Franco-Russian Alliance of 1892, Germany faced the prospect of a two-front war. Schlieffen reasoned that Germany had to defeat one opponent quickly before the other could fully mobilize. Because Russia's vast size and slower mobilization made a quick victory in the east impractical, the plan concentrated overwhelming strength in the west while a small force held East Prussia.

The plan

The essence of the plan was a gigantic wheeling movement: the powerful German right wing would advance through neutral Belgium (and, in Schlieffen's original conception, the Dutch province of Limburg), sweep west and south of Paris, and crush the French armies against the German left wing in Lorraine and the Swiss border. The left wing was to be deliberately weak, even yielding ground if necessary, to draw French forces away from the decisive sector. Schlieffen's 1905 memorandum envisaged victory in France within roughly 39–42 days of mobilization.

Execution in 1914

Schlieffen's successor, Helmuth von Moltke the Younger, modified the plan: he strengthened the left wing at the expense of the right, dropped the advance through the Netherlands, and sent additional corps east after early reports of Russian advances. Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August 1914, bringing Britain into the war. The advance nevertheless failed at the First Battle of the Marne in September 1914, after which both sides dug in, beginning four years of trench warfare on the Western Front.

Historiography

Historians have long debated whether a single coherent "Schlieffen Plan" existed as later described. The American historian Terence Zuber argued that the canonical plan is a postwar invention; other historians, working from surviving memoranda, maintain that a Schlieffen-style deployment plan genuinely formed the basis of German operations in 1914, while disputing details.
