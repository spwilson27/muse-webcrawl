Père Lachaise Cemetery

An avenue in Père Lachaise

Père Lachaise Cemetery (French: Cimetière du Père-Lachaise) is the largest cemetery in Paris, France, covering 44 hectares (110 acres) in the 20th arrondissement. It is estimated to contain around one million interments and receives more than 3.5 million visitors a year, making it the most visited necropolis in the world.

History

The cemetery takes its name from Père François de la Chaise (1624–1709), confessor to Louis XIV, who lived in the Jesuit house on the site. Established by Napoleon in 1804, it was initially unpopular — it lay far from the city and had few burials. To raise its prestige, the authorities transferred the remains of the playwright Molière and the poet La Fontaine, and later of the medieval lovers Héloïse and Abélard, after which Parisians clamoured to be buried among the famous.

Famous burials

Among the many notable people interred at Père Lachaise are the composers Frédéric Chopin and Gioachino Rossini, the writers Oscar Wilde, Marcel Proust, Honoré de Balzac and Colette, the singers Édith Piaf and Maria Callas, the painter Eugène Delacroix, and Jim Morrison of The Doors, whose grave is one of the most visited. The Mur des Fédérés (Communards' Wall) in the cemetery marks the spot where 147 fédérés, defenders of the Paris Commune, were shot on 28 May 1871.

Description

Laid out as an English-style garden cemetery with winding paths, some 5,000 trees, and an extraordinary variety of sepulchral architecture — from simple headstones to elaborate miniature chapels — Père Lachaise is also credited as the first garden cemetery and the first municipal cemetery in Paris.
