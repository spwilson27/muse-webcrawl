Bilateral contract

A bilateral contract is a contract in which both parties exchange promises to perform. Under a bilateral contract, each party is both an obligor (a person bound to another) and an obligee (a person to whom another is bound), because the promise of each party is supported by the promise of the other. Most contracts are bilateral; the sale of goods, for example, involves a promise by the seller to deliver the goods and a promise by the buyer to pay.

Contrast with unilateral contracts

A unilateral contract, by contrast, involves a promise made by only one party in exchange for the performance of an act by the other party. The classic example is a reward contract: a person who promises a reward for the return of a lost dog is bound only once someone actually performs the act of returning the dog. In a bilateral contract, both parties are bound from the moment the promises are exchanged.

Formation

Bilateral contracts are formed through offer and acceptance: an offeror makes an offer, and the offeree accepts by making a return promise. Once acceptance is communicated, the contract is binding on both parties even though neither has yet performed. This distinguishes bilateral contracts from unilateral ones, where acceptance occurs only through complete performance.

Examples

Common examples of bilateral contracts include sales agreements, employment contracts, leases, and most commercial transactions, in which each side undertakes obligations enforceable by the other.
