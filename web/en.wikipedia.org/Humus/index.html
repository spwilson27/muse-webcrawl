Humus
The dark organic component of soil formed by the decomposition of plant and animal matter.

In soil science, humus is the dark organic matter in soil that is formed by the decomposition of plant and animal matter. It is a kind of soil organic matter that has reached a point of stability, resisting further decomposition, and it is crucial for soil fertility and structure. The word is Latin for "earth" or "ground" — the same root as in human.

Formation

Humus forms through humification: microorganisms and soil fauna such as earthworms break down litter, roots and other organic residues. Fungi and bacteria enzymatically transform the material into complex, amorphous organic compounds — humic substances, including humic acids, fulvic acids and humin. The process is slower in cold or waterlogged soils, which is why peat accumulates in bogs.

Importance

Humus improves soil by increasing water-holding capacity, cation exchange (and thus nutrient retention), aggregation and aeration. Mature compost and well-rotted manure are largely humus. Because humus stores large amounts of carbon for decades to centuries, soils are a major terrestrial carbon reservoir, and humus loss through erosion or intensive tillage contributes to declining soil quality.

Types

Forest-floor humus forms are classified as mor (acidic, distinct layers, common under conifers), moder, and mull (well mixed by fauna, typical of fertile deciduous woodland soils).
