A steam turbine is a machine that extracts thermal energy from pressurised steam and uses it to do mechanical work on a rotating output shaft. Its modern manifestation was invented by Charles Parsons in 1884. Fabrication of a modern steam turbine involves advanced metalwork using high-quality stainless steels and specialist forgings.

Principle of operation

Steam is expanded through a series of stages, each consisting of a ring of fixed guide vanes (nozzles) and a ring of blades on the rotor. In an impulse stage (de Laval, Curtis), the pressure drop occurs in the nozzles and the high-velocity jets drive the blades; in a reaction stage (Parsons), expansion continues across the moving blades, which act as nozzles themselves, with roughly half the pressure drop taken in the rotor. Most large turbines combine both types across many stages.

Applications

Steam turbines drive most of the world's electricity generation: steam raised in coal, gas (combined cycle), nuclear, geothermal and solar thermal plants turns turbine-generator sets, with single units exceeding 1,000 MW. Parsons' turbine also revolutionised marine propulsion, dramatically demonstrated when his vessel Turbinia upstaged the Royal Navy fleet review at Spithead in 1897; turbines powered generations of warships and fast liners. Smaller turbines drive compressors, pumps and other industrial machinery.

Efficiency

Large modern condensing turbines achieve thermal efficiencies around 40–45% in combined-cycle or advanced steam plants, limited by steam temperatures and materials.
