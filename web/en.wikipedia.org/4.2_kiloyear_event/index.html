4.2 kiloyear event

The 4.2 kiloyear event (also called the 4.2 ka event) was an aridification event that occurred around 2200 BCE, lasting through much of the 22nd century BCE. It is hypothesized to have caused the collapse of several ancient civilizations, including the Old Kingdom of Egypt, the Akkadian Empire in Mesopotamia, and the Liangzhu culture in China.

Evidence

Paleoclimate records from ice cores, cave speleothems, lake sediments, and dust deposits in marine cores indicate a period of severe drought and cooling across North Africa, the Middle East, the Indus Valley, and parts of China and North America. A dust spike recorded in a stalagmite from Gol-e-Zard cave in Iran has been dated precisely to around 2260–2140 BCE.

Societal effects

In Egypt, low Nile floods coincided with the end of the Old Kingdom and the fragmentation of the First Intermediate Period. In Mesopotamia, the Akkadian Empire disintegrated within a few generations, an event lamented in the text known as the Curse of Akkad. Archaeological evidence from Tell Leilan in Syria shows abandonment of the site at this time.

Geological significance

In 2018, the International Chronostratigraphic Chart formally adopted the event as the boundary between the Northgrippian and Meghalayan ages of the Holocene epoch, making the Meghalayan the youngest formally recognized age of the geological time scale.
