Metabolic theory of ecology

The metabolic theory of ecology (MTE) is an ecological theory that seeks to explain patterns in the structure and dynamics of organisms, populations, communities and ecosystems from the principles of individual metabolism. It was developed principally by James H. Brown, Geoffrey B. West, James F. Gillooly and colleagues, building on earlier work on allometric scaling.

Foundations

The theory rests on two pillars. First, Kleiber's law: the metabolic rate of an organism scales with its body mass to approximately the three-quarter power, a pattern attributed by West, Brown and Enquist (1997) to the fractal-like geometry of resource distribution networks such as circulatory systems. Second, the temperature dependence of biochemical kinetics, described by the Boltzmann–Arrhenius relation, means metabolic rates rise exponentially with temperature.

Predictions

Combining these, MTE predicts how body size and temperature jointly govern rates of growth, reproduction, mortality, population growth, species interactions, biodiversity gradients and ecosystem fluxes of energy and materials.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether scaling exponents are truly universal, whether network models apply across all taxa, and how well the theory handles variation among individuals and environments. The theory nevertheless remains one of the most ambitious attempts at a unified, mechanistic ecology.
