In Greek mythology, Medea (Greek: Μήδεια) is a princess and sorceress from Colchis on the Black Sea, the daughter of King Aeëtes, niece of Circe and granddaughter of the sun god Helios. She is best known for aiding Jason in winning the Golden Fleece and, after he betrayed her, for her terrible revenge — the central subject of Euripides' tragedy Medea (431 BC).

Mythology

Struck by love for Jason through the machinations of Aphrodite and Hera, Medea used her magic to help him perform the impossible tasks set by her father: she gave him an ointment against the fire-breathing bulls, told him how to defeat the earthborn warriors, and charmed the sleepless dragon guarding the Fleece. She fled with the Argonauts, killing her own brother Absyrtus to delay pursuit, and lived with Jason at Iolcus and then Corinth. In Euripides' play, Jason abandons Medea to marry the Corinthian princess Glauce; Medea sends Glauce a poisoned robe and crown, then murders her own two sons by Jason and escapes to Athens in a chariot drawn by dragons, sent by her grandfather Helios.

Later traditions

Medea married Aegeus, king of Athens, and bore Medus; she fled after attempting to poison the young Theseus. Seneca's Roman tragedy intensified her portrayal as a figure of furious vengeance, and she has been endlessly reinterpreted in opera, drama, film and feminist criticism — variously as monstrous child-killer, wronged foreigner, and archetype of the powerful woman feared by the male order.
