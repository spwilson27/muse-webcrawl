Newborn screening
SpecimenDried blood spot from a heel prick
TimingUsually 24–72 hours after birth
First screened conditionPhenylketonuria (PKU), 1960s
Key technologyTandem mass spectrometry
PurposeEarly detection of treatable congenital disorders

Newborn screening is a public health programme in which infants are tested shortly after birth for a panel of congenital conditions that are treatable but not clinically evident in the newborn period. A few drops of blood, collected from a heel prick onto filter paper (a "Guthrie card"), are analysed for metabolic, endocrine, haemoglobin, and genetic disorders. Early detection allows treatment to begin before irreversible damage—such as intellectual disability, severe illness, or death—occurs.

History

The field began with Robert Guthrie's bacterial inhibition assay for phenylketonuria (PKU) in the early 1960s, which made mass screening feasible for the first time. Screening for congenital hypothyroidism followed in the 1970s. The introduction of tandem mass spectrometry in the 1990s allowed dozens of metabolic disorders to be detected from a single blood spot, greatly expanding panels. In the United States, the Recommended Uniform Screening Panel now lists more than 35 core conditions, including cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, severe combined immunodeficiency, and spinal muscular atrophy; screening also commonly includes hearing tests and pulse oximetry for critical congenital heart disease.

Process and follow-up

Screening is not diagnosis: a positive result triggers confirmatory testing and referral to specialists. Programmes must balance sensitivity against false positives, which cause parental anxiety and follow-up costs. Criteria for adding conditions derive from the classic Wilson and Jungner principles—a known natural history, an available treatment, and a suitable, acceptable test—adapted as therapies such as gene therapy emerge for ever rarer disorders.

Issues

Debates surround the retention and secondary use of dried blood spots, consent models (screening is mandatory or opt-out in many jurisdictions), the expansion of panels to conditions without proven treatments, and the prospect of genomic newborn screening, which could detect hundreds of conditions but raises questions about incidental findings and equity.
