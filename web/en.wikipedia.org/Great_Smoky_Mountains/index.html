Great Smoky Mountains

The Great Smoky Mountains are a mountain range rising along the Tennessee–North Carolina border in the southeastern United States. They are a subrange of the Appalachian Mountains and form the core of Great Smoky Mountains National Park, the most visited national park in the United States. The range takes its name from the natural blue haze — caused by airborne hydrocarbons released by the dense vegetation — that often hangs over its ridges.

Geography

The Smokies stretch for roughly 70 miles from the Pigeon River in the northeast to the Little Tennessee River in the southwest. Sixteen peaks rise above 6,000 feet (1,829 m); the highest is Clingmans Dome at 6,643 feet (2,025 m), the highest point in Tennessee and the third-highest mountain east of the Mississippi River. Other prominent summits include Mount Guyot and Mount Le Conte.

The range is among the oldest in the world, with rocks of Precambrian age. Heavy rainfall — up to 85 inches a year in places — sustains temperate rainforest conditions.

Ecology

The Smokies are an International Biosphere Reserve and are noted for exceptional biodiversity: over 19,000 species have been documented, including more than 100 native tree species, an unusually rich salamander fauna (earning the area the nickname "Salamander Capital of the World"), and some 1,500 black bears. The synchronous fireflies Photinus carolinus draw crowds each June. Much of the range was logged before the park's establishment in 1934, but about 35 percent of the park remains old-growth forest.

History

The Cherokee inhabited the region for centuries before European settlement; the name Shaconage — "place of the blue smoke" — is attributed to them. Most Cherokee were forcibly removed west on the Trail of Tears in 1838, though the Eastern Band remained. The Appalachian Trail crosses the range along the state line, passing over Clingmans Dome. Historic settlements such as Cades Cove preserve the log cabins and mills of 19th-century mountain communities.
