Special Higher Police

The Special Higher Police (特別高等警察, Tokubetsu Kōtō Keisatsu), often abbreviated Tokkō, was a police force established in 1911 in the Empire of Japan, specifically charged with investigating and controlling political groups and ideologies seen as a threat to public order. It is often referred to in English as the "Thought Police," by analogy with the force in George Orwell's novel Nineteen Eighty-Four.

History

The Tokkō was created in the wake of the High Treason Incident of 1910, in which socialists and anarchists were accused of plotting to assassinate the Emperor. Its powers were greatly expanded under the Peace Preservation Law of 1925, which criminalised advocacy of changes to the kokutai (national polity) or to the system of private property. The Tokkō monitored socialists, communists, labour unions, tenant farmers' movements, religious groups such as Ōmoto, and Koreans and other minorities.

Methods

The force maintained extensive surveillance networks, conducted arrests and interrogations, and pressured suspects to undergo tenkō — ideological conversion and public recantation. Its sections covered areas including censorship, labour, arbitration, and the monitoring of foreigners.

Abolition

The Tokkō was abolished in October 1945 by order of the Allied occupation authorities (GHQ), and the Peace Preservation Law was repealed as part of the democratisation of Japan.
