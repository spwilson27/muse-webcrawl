Brake

FunctionSlow or stop motion by converting kinetic energy
Common typesDisc, drum, regenerative, air, electromagnetic

A brake is a mechanical device that inhibits motion by absorbing energy from a moving system, most commonly converting kinetic energy into heat through friction. Brakes are essential components of vehicles, trains, aircraft, elevators, and industrial machinery.

Types

Friction brakes include disc brakes, in which pads clamp a rotating disc, and drum brakes, in which shoes press outward against a drum. Large trucks and trains use compressed-air brakes. Electric and hybrid vehicles employ regenerative braking, running the motor as a generator to recover energy into the battery. Other forms include electromagnetic brakes, aerodynamic brakes (air brakes and spoilers), and water brakes used in dynamometers.

Principles and safety

Brake performance is limited by available friction and by heat dissipation; repeated hard use can cause brake fade. Modern road vehicles add anti-lock braking systems (ABS), which modulate pressure to prevent wheel lock, and electronic brake-force distribution. Brakes are safety-critical and are designed with redundant circuits so a single hydraulic failure does not remove all braking.
