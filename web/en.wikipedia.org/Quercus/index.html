Quercus is a genus of about 430 extant species of trees and shrubs in the beech family, Fagaceae. Its members are collectively known as oaks. The genus is native to the Northern Hemisphere, ranging from cool temperate to tropical latitudes in North and South America, Europe, Asia, and North Africa, with the greatest diversity in Mexico, China, and the eastern United States.

Oaks are hardwoods with spirally arranged leaves, often with lobed margins, and fruit in the form of acorns — nuts held in a cup-like cupule. Every oak species is pollinated by wind and hosts hundreds of other species of insects, birds, and mammals. Oak wood is strong, hard, and resistant to decay, and has been prized since antiquity for construction, shipbuilding, barrels, and fuel.

English oak (Quercus robur), the type species of the genus

Classification

The genus is divided into two subgenera: Quercus, containing the white, red, and intermediate oaks of the Americas, Europe, and Asia, and Cerris, containing the cerris, holm, and cork oaks and their relatives. Traditional groupings recognize the white oak group (acorns maturing in one season, sweet-tasting), the red oak group (acorns maturing in two seasons, bitter-tasting), and the live oaks, which remain evergreen in warm climates. Hybridization between related species is frequent, making species boundaries difficult to draw.

Ecology

Oaks are keystone species in many forests and savannas. In mast years they produce enormous crops of acorns that feed deer, bears, pigs, pigeons, jays, and squirrels, which in turn disperse the seeds. Old oaks support an exceptional diversity of lichens, fungi, and invertebrates; a single English oak may host over 2,000 species. Oak trees can live for many centuries — some famous specimens are believed to be more than a thousand years old.

Uses

Oak timber is used for furniture, flooring, panelling, and historically for warships such as HMS Victory. Oak barrels impart flavour to wine, sherry, whisky, and brandy during aging; the bark of the cork oak (Quercus suber) is harvested for cork stoppers. Tannins from oak bark were long essential to leather tanning. Acorns, though bitter, have been ground into flour in many cultures and remain an important wildlife and livestock food in Mediterranean dehesa landscapes.

Cultural significance

The oak symbolizes strength and endurance in many cultures. It was sacred to Zeus, Thor, and the Celts; it appears on coats of arms and coins, serves as the national tree of numerous countries (including England, Germany, Poland, and the United States), and gives its name to countless places.

Threats

Oaks face pests and diseases including oak processionary moth, acute oak decline, and sudden oak death caused by Phytophthora ramorum, as well as habitat loss and climate change.
