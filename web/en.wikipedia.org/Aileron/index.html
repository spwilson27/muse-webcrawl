Aileron

An aileron (French for "little wing" or "fin") is a hinged flight control surface, usually on the trailing edge of each wing of a fixed-wing aircraft. Ailerons are used in pairs to control the aircraft in roll (rotation around the longitudinal axis): one aileron deflects upward while the other deflects downward, increasing lift on one wing and decreasing it on the other, so the aircraft banks.

Operation

The pilot commands roll with the control stick or yoke, which moves the ailerons differentially through cables, pushrods or hydraulic actuators. Because the down-deflected aileron also increases drag, it tends to yaw the nose away from the turn — an effect known as adverse yaw. Designers counter it with differential ailerons (which deflect up more than down), Frise-type ailerons, or automatic coordination with the rudder.

History

The principle of lateral control by movable wing surfaces was described by Matthew Piers Watt Boulton in an 1868 patent. The Wright brothers instead used wing-warping on their early aircraft, but ailerons — championed by Glenn Curtiss and the Aerial Experiment Association, and independently developed in Europe — became standard by the First World War.

Variants

Related devices include flaperons (combining aileron and flap functions), spoilerons (spoilers used for roll control), and elevons (combining aileron and elevator functions on tailless and delta-wing aircraft).
