Pranayama (Sanskrit: प्राणायाम, prāṇāyāma) is the yogic practice of focusing on breath. In Sanskrit, prana means "vital life force", and yama means "to gain control". In yoga, breath is associated with prana; thus, pranayama is a means to elevate the prana-shakti, or life energies. Pranayama is described in Hindu texts such as the Bhagavad Gita and the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali. Later, in hatha yoga texts, it came to mean the complete suspension of breathing.

Etymology

The word is a compound of prāṇa (प्राण, "life force" or "vital principle") and āyāma (आयाम, "extension" or "expansion"). Some scholars instead parse the second element as yama (यम, "restraint" or "control"), giving the meaning "breath control" or "breath restraint". The practice is ancient, with references in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (c. 700 BCE) and the Bhagavad Gita.

In the Yoga Sutras

Pranayama is the fourth "limb" of the eight limbs of yoga, as set out by Patanjali in the Yoga Sutras. Patanjali describes pranayama as the regulation of the incoming and outgoing flow of breath with retention, undertaken after attaining stability in posture (asana). He distinguishes three components: inhalation (puraka), retention (kumbhaka), and exhalation (rechaka). Its purpose is to reduce the veil over the inner light and to make the mind fit for concentration (dharana) and meditation.

Types of pranayama

The Hatha Yoga Pradipika (15th century) describes eight classical kumbhakas, the foundational repertoire of hatha pranayama:

  • Ujjayi — "victorious" breath, with slight constriction of the glottis, producing a soft oceanic sound.
  • Surya Bhedana — "sun-piercing" breath, inhaling through the right nostril and exhaling through the left.
  • Bhastrika — "bellows" breath, rapid forceful inhalations and exhalations.
  • Bhramari — "bee" breath, exhaling with a humming sound.
  • Kapalabhati — "skull-shining" breath, a series of sharp exhalations driven by the abdominal muscles.
  • Nadi Shodhana — alternate-nostril breathing, said to purify the subtle channels (nadis).
  • Shitali and Sitkari — cooling breaths taken through the curled tongue or teeth.

Physiological effects

Slow pranayama breathing has been shown in clinical studies to influence autonomic function, increasing vagal tone and heart-rate variability, and to reduce blood pressure and perceived stress. Practised at around six breaths per minute, it can resonate with the baroreflex. However, forceful techniques such as bhastrika and kapalabhati carry risks for people with hypertension, cardiovascular disease, or glaucoma, and traditional texts warn that improper practice can be harmful, recommending instruction from an experienced teacher.
