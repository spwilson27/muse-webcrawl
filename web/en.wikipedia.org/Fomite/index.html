Fomite

A fomite is any inanimate object that, when contaminated with or exposed to infectious agents such as pathogenic bacteria, viruses or fungi, can transfer disease to a new host. Common fomites include door handles, handrails, elevator buttons, shared utensils, towels, clothing, bedding and medical instruments. The risk posed by a fomite depends on the pathogen, how long it survives on the surface, and the frequency with which the object is touched.

Transmission

Fomite transmission typically occurs when a person touches a contaminated surface and then touches their mouth, nose or eyes, allowing the pathogen to reach mucous membranes. Respiratory viruses such as rhinoviruses and norovirus are known to spread this way; norovirus in particular can persist on surfaces for days and requires only a tiny infectious dose. Bacteria such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) can also survive on hospital surfaces, making environmental cleaning an important infection-control measure.

Prevention

Hand hygiene is the principal defence against fomite transmission, alongside regular disinfection of high-touch surfaces and sterilisation of medical equipment. During the COVID-19 pandemic, attention to fomites was high early on, though later evidence indicated airborne transmission of SARS-CoV-2 was the dominant route; public-health guidance came to emphasise ventilation while still recommending routine surface cleaning.

History

The term derives from the Latin fomes, meaning tinder or touchwood — material that readily catches fire — a metaphor for objects that readily spread infection. The plural fomites became standard in English medical usage.
