Diamond Head, Hawaii

Diamond Head is a volcanic tuff cone on the Hawaiian island of Oʻahu and known to Hawaiians as Lēʻahi. Its English name was given by British sailors in the 19th century, who mistook the calcite crystals on the adjacent beach for diamonds. It is located on the coast east of Waikīkī in Honolulu.

Geology

Diamond Head is part of the Honolulu Volcanic Series, a series of eruptions of the Koʻolau Volcano that took place long after the main shield-building stage ended. The cone is estimated to be about 300,000 years old and was formed in a single explosive eruption lasting only a few days, when magma met shallow seawater and fragmented into ash that cemented into tuff. The crater is about 1.1 km (3,520 ft) in diameter and the summit rises 232 m (761 ft) above sea level.

History

The Hawaiian name Lēʻahi combines lē (browridge) and ʻahi (tuna), because the ridgeline resembles the dorsal fin of a tuna. In 1904 the federal government acquired the crater for military purposes, and Fort Ruger was established within it; tunnels, batteries, and the Fire Control Station at the summit were built for coastal defense. The summit trail, constructed in 1908, includes switchbacks, a tunnel through the crater wall, and a spiral staircase.

Tourism

Diamond Head State Monument is one of Hawaiʻi's most famous landmarks and a popular hiking destination. The 1.3 km (0.8 mi) trail to the summit offers panoramic views of Waikīkī and the Pacific Ocean. It was designated a National Natural Landmark in 1968.
