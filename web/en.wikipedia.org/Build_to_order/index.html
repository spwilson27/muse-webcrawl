Build to order

Build to order (BTO), also called make to order (MTO), is a production approach in which products are not built until a confirmed order for the product is received. It is one of several production strategies alongside build to stock, assemble to order, and engineer to order. The approach is often used for highly configured or customized products and for expensive goods where holding inventory is costly.

Advantages

Build to order reduces inventory carrying costs and the risk of unsold stock, eliminates the waste of building unwanted configurations, and lets customers receive products tailored to their specifications. Dell became the best-known example in the 1990s and 2000s by building personal computers only after customers configured and ordered them, giving the company a cost advantage over rivals holding finished-goods inventory.

Disadvantages

Customers must wait for production after ordering, so lead times are longer than for stocked goods. The approach also requires flexible manufacturing, reliable supply chains, and accurate demand information, and unit costs may be higher because production cannot be smoothed over time.

Applications

Build to order is common in the automobile industry, for industrial machinery and ships, in personal computing, and in niche or luxury markets where customization is valued. Variants include configure to order, in which standard modules are combined to a customer specification, and delayed differentiation, in which customization is postponed to the latest possible stage.
