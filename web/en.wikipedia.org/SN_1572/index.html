SN 1572, also known as Tycho's Supernova or Tycho's Nova (B Cassiopeiae), was a Type Ia supernova in the constellation Cassiopeia, one of eight supernovae visible to the naked eye in historical records. It appeared in early November 1572 and was observed by many astronomers across Europe and Asia, most famously by the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe, whose detailed treatise De nova stella (1573) made it famous.

The remnant of Tycho's supernova in X-rays, imaged by the Chandra observatory.

Historical significance

At its peak the supernova rivalled Venus in brightness, reaching an apparent magnitude of about −4, and it remained visible to the naked eye for some sixteen months. Tycho's careful measurements showed that the new star exhibited no measurable diurnal parallax, meaning it lay far beyond the Moon. This contradicted the Aristotelian doctrine of the unchanging heavens — new stars were supposed to be atmospheric phenomena — and helped undermine the Ptolemaic cosmology, paving the way for the astronomical revolution of the following century.

The remnant

The supernova remnant, designated 3C 10, was identified in the radio in the 1950s and is now studied across the electromagnetic spectrum. Observations of the light echo of the original explosion confirmed its classification as a normal Type Ia supernova, the thermonuclear destruction of a white dwarf. Studies of the remnant continue to inform models of cosmic-ray acceleration and supernova ejecta dynamics.
