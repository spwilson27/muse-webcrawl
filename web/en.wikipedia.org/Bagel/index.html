Bagel


Sesame and poppy-seed bagels.

A bagel (Yiddish: בײגל, beygl; Polish: bajgiel) is a bread roll traditionally made from yeasted wheat dough, shaped by hand into a ring, briefly boiled in water, and then baked. The result is a dense, chewy interior with a browned, sometimes crisp exterior. Bagels are often topped with seeds — most commonly poppy or sesame — baked onto the outer crust, and are eaten plain, toasted, or as sandwiches, famously with cream cheese and lox (cured salmon).

History

The bagel originated in the Jewish communities of Poland. A popular legend connects it with the 1683 Battle of Vienna and a bread shaped like a stirrup (Bügel in Austrian German) in honour of King John III Sobieski's cavalry, but ring-shaped breads in Kraków are documented earlier: community regulations of the Kraków Jewish community mention bajgiel as early as 1610, given to women in childbirth. Boiling before baking — the bagel's defining technique — may have been encouraged by restrictions on Jewish baking in some places, and the method also gave the bread a longer keeping quality.

Jewish immigrants brought the bagel to North America in the late nineteenth century, especially to New York City, where bagel bakeries flourished on the Lower East Side. Bagel bakers organised in a powerful craft union, Bagel Bakers' Local 338, which controlled production in the city for decades; bagels were rolled by hand in small shops until the invention of bagel-making machines in the 1960s, notably by Daniel Thompson and the Lender family, which allowed mass production and freezing. Murray Lender's bagel bakery in New Haven and later national distribution turned the bagel from an ethnic speciality into a mainstream American breakfast food.

Preparation and varieties

Traditional bagel dough is high in gluten, low in fat and sugar, and relatively stiff. After shaping — classically by rolling a rope of dough around the hand and sealing the ends — the rings are proofed, often retarded overnight in the cold, then boiled briefly in water that may contain malt or a little alkali. Boiling gelatinises the surface starch, producing the characteristic chewy crust and shine, before the bagels are baked at high temperature.

Regional styles differ notably. The New York bagel is large, malty and chewy, traditionally credited to the city's water. The Montreal bagel, introduced by Jewish bakers from Poland, is smaller, denser and sweeter — boiled in honey-sweetened water and baked in a wood-fired oven. Common varieties include plain, sesame, poppy seed, "everything" (a mixture of seeds, salt, garlic and onion), pumpernickel, cinnamon-raisin and egg bagels. In London's East End, the similar "beigel" — as sold on Brick Lane — accompanies salt beef.

Cultural significance

The bagel became a symbol of Jewish-American food culture and of New York City itself. The classic combination of bagel, cream cheese, lox, capers, tomato and red onion is a fixture of delicatessens and "appetizing" stores. Bagels are now produced worldwide, and chains such as Einstein Bros. and Bruegger's, along with supermarket brands, have made them a standard breakfast item far beyond their original communities, though purists continue to debate the merits of steamed, mass-produced versions versus traditionally boiled ones.
