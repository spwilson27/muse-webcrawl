Bialy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A bialy (Yiddish: ביאלי), short for bialystoker kuchen (from Białystok, a city in Poland), is a chewy yeast roll associated with Jewish cuisine. Unlike a bagel, which is boiled before baking, a bialy is simply baked, and instead of a hole in the middle it has a depression. The depression is traditionally filled with diced onions and often poppy seeds before baking. The bialy is also not as smooth or shiny on the outside as a bagel, being dusted with flour.

History

The bialy originated among the Jewish bakers of Białystok, then part of the Russian Empire and later Poland. Jewish immigrants brought the roll to the United States in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, and it became a staple of Jewish bakeries in New York City, particularly on the Lower East Side. The roll's stronghold remains New York, where bakeries such as Kossar's Bialys (founded 1936) keep the tradition alive, though bialys can now be found in many American cities and, rarely, elsewhere.

Preparation

Bialy dough is made with high-gluten flour, water, yeast, and salt. After proofing, rounds of dough are stretched by hand to form a wide rim around a thin center, which is pricked and filled with a mixture of minced onion and poppy seeds (sometimes with breadcrumbs or garlic). The rolls are baked at high temperature until crisp and golden at the edges. Because they are not boiled, bialys have a matte, flour-dusted crust and a softer, more bread-like interior than bagels, and they stale quickly — traditionally they are eaten the day they are baked.

Variations and serving

Bialys are commonly split and filled with butter, cream cheese, or lox and other sandwich fillings. Modern variations include garlic bialys, sesame, and "bialy" flatbread-style pizza toppings. The onion-filled center is considered the classic form.
