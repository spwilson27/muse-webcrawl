Jewish cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jewish cuisine refers to the worldwide culinary traditions of the Jewish people. It has evolved over many centuries, shaped by Jewish dietary laws (kashrut), Jewish festivals, and Shabbat (Sabbath) traditions, and by the local ingredients and cuisines of the many countries where Jewish communities have lived. Because of this diaspora, "Jewish food" encompasses several distinct regional styles, notably Ashkenazi, Sephardi, and Mizrahi cuisines, as well as more recent Israeli cuisine.

Influences and rules

Kashrut prohibits, among other things, pork and shellfish, and forbids mixing meat and dairy in the same meal; these rules strongly shape traditional dishes and their adaptations. Sabbath laws prohibiting cooking from Friday sundown to Saturday night produced slow-cooked stews — the Ashkenazi cholent and Sephardi hamin — that cook overnight. Holiday foods carry symbolic weight: apples and honey at Rosh Hashanah, matzo at Passover, fried foods such as latkes and sufganiyot at Hanukkah, and triangular hamantaschen at Purim.

Ashkenazi cuisine

The food of the Jews of Central and Eastern Europe includes bagels, bialys, challah, gefilte fish, chopped liver, chicken soup with matzo balls, kugel (noodle or potato pudding), knishes, pastrami and corned beef, rugelach, and borscht adaptations. In America this tradition produced the Jewish delicatessen, with its oversized sandwiches and pickles.

Sephardi and Mizrahi cuisines

Sephardi cuisine, from Iberia and the Mediterranean diaspora, features olive oil, rice dishes, stuffed vegetables, borekas, and egg-and-vegetable dishes such as the ancestors of shakshuka. Mizrahi cuisine, from the Middle East and North Africa, includes hummus, falafel, kebabs, couscous, and richly spiced stews. These traditions, together with Ashkenazi contributions, formed the basis of modern Israeli cuisine.
