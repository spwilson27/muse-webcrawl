Lox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lox is a fillet of brined salmon, which may or may not be smoked. Lox is frequently served on a bagel with cream cheese, and often garnished with tomato, red onion, and capers. The word derives from the Yiddish laks (לאַקס), meaning salmon, cognate with German Lachs and Scandinavian lax/laks.

Types

In the strict sense, lox is salmon cured only in a salt brine — also called "belly lox" in New York delicatessen usage, for the fatty cut from the belly of the fish, which is saltier and oilier. Nova or Nova Scotia salmon, often what American menus actually serve as "lox," is cured with a milder brine and then cold-smoked, giving it a smoother texture and gentler flavor. Gravlax is the Scandinavian preparation cured in salt, sugar, and dill and not smoked. Other variants include pastrami-cured salmon and Western or "Scottish-style" smoked salmon.

History and use

Salt-cured and smoked fish were preservation staples for Eastern European Jewish immigrants, who sold salmon from pushcarts and appetizing stores in New York. The bagel with lox and cream cheese became an iconic American Jewish dish in the early 20th century, when refrigeration and transcontinental rail made salmon widely available, and Jewish dietary law conveniently permitted fish to be eaten with dairy. Appetizing stores — such as Russ & Daughters (founded 1914) — specialized in lox and other cured fish alongside dairy goods, complementing the meat-only delicatessen under kashrut separation.
