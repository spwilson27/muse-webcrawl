A refracting telescope (also called a refractor) is a type of optical telescope that uses a lens as its objective to form an image. Light passing through the convex objective lens is refracted (bent) to a focus, where an eyepiece magnifies the image. The refracting telescope was the earliest type of optical telescope, invented in the Netherlands in 1608.

A Keplerian refractor uses a convex eyepiece; a Galilean uses a concave one.

History

The first known refracting telescopes were Hans Lippershey's spyglasses of 1608. Galileo Galilei improved the design in 1609 and turned it on the heavens, discovering Jupiter's moons, the phases of Venus, and sunspots. Johannes Kepler proposed the improved design with two convex lenses in 1611. Early refractors suffered from chromatic aberration — coloured fringes caused by the lens dispersing light — which opticians fought with extremely long focal lengths (the "aerial telescopes" of the 17th century) until the achromatic doublet, developed by Chester Moore Hall and commercialised by John Dollond in the 1750s, greatly reduced it; apochromatic designs reduced it further.

Design and limits

A lens can only be supported at its edges, so large objective lenses sag under their own weight, and suitable glass blanks become prohibitively difficult to cast. The largest practical refractor ever built is the 40-inch (1.02 m) telescope at Yerkes Observatory (1897), still the largest refractor used for astronomical research. Because of these limits, professional astronomy moved to reflecting telescopes, while refractors remain popular for amateur observing, where their sealed tubes, sharp images, and low maintenance are advantages.
