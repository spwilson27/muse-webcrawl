Tunnel warfare

Underground tunnels have been used in sieges since antiquity

Tunnel warfare is warfare conducted in tunnels and other underground cavities, often as part of siege warfare. It involves digging tunnels under enemy fortifications to undermine them, placing explosives (mining), and counter-mining by defenders to intercept the attackers' tunnels.

Ancient and medieval warfare

In ancient sieges, attackers dug tunnels to undermine city walls, propping the excavations with timber that was then burned so the wall above collapsed. Defenders dug counter-mines, and underground fights with smoke and hand weapons could result. At the siege of Dura-Europos (256 CE), Persian sappers and Roman counter-miners fought underground; the remains of Roman soldiers were found in a collapsed tunnel.

Gunpowder era and the First World War

With gunpowder, mines became explosive charges placed beneath strongpoints. Mining was a feature of the Crimean War, the American Civil War (famously at the Battle of the Crater, 1864) and especially the static fronts of the First World War. Specialist tunnelling companies dug deep mines under German lines on the Western Front; at the Battle of Messines in 1917, nineteen huge mines were detonated simultaneously, killing an estimated 10,000 German soldiers.

Modern tunnel warfare

Extensive tunnel networks were used by the Viet Cong in the Vietnam War, notably at Củ Chi, and tunnel systems have featured in the Korean Demilitarized Zone and in the Gaza Strip in the 21st century. Modern militaries employ ground-penetrating radar, seismic sensors, drills and specialised units to detect and destroy military tunnels.
