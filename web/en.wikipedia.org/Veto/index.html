Veto

A veto is a legal power to unilaterally stop an official action. In the most typical example, the president of a republic or the governor of a state vetoes a bill passed by a legislature to prevent it from becoming law, though the legislature may often override the veto by a supermajority vote. The word originates from Latin, meaning "I forbid".

History

The concept originated with the Roman offices of consul and tribune of the plebs. A tribune could block legislation or actions of the Roman Senate with an intercessio, physically interposing his person — sacrosanct under Roman law — between the Senate and its decree. The veto power of the United Nations Security Council's permanent members, the British monarch's historical reserve power, and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth's liberum veto are among the institution's notable historical forms.

Types

Vetoes may be absolute, where the blocked measure cannot proceed at all, or qualified, where a legislature may override. Executives may wield a package veto over an entire bill or a line-item veto over particular provisions. A pocket veto occurs when an executive simply declines to sign a bill within the allotted period and the legislature has adjourned, making override impossible.
