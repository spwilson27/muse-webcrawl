History of writing

The history of writing traces the development of the expression of language by systems of marks, and how these systems have in turn shaped — and been shaped by — human society. Writing is generally agreed to have emerged independently in at least three places: Mesopotamia (c. 3400–3300 BC), China (c. 1250 BC, with possible earlier antecedents), and Mesoamerica (c. 900–600 BC). In Egypt, hieroglyphic writing appeared around 3200 BC, possibly inspired by contact with Mesopotamia.

Proto-writing

Before full writing, human beings used systems of symbols to record information: tally sticks, painted signs, the Vinča symbols of Neolithic Europe, and the clay tokens used in the Near East to count goods from about 8000 BC. A "full" writing system, by contrast, encodes language itself, so that anything spoken can in principle be written down.

Mesopotamia: cuneiform

The Sumerians of southern Iraq developed cuneiform ("wedge-shaped") writing by pressing a reed stylus into clay tablets. The earliest tablets (Uruk period, c. 3400–3300 BC) record accounts of grain, beer and livestock. Over centuries the signs evolved from pictographs into a mixed logographic-syllabic system used for Sumerian, Akkadian and many other languages. Scribes wrote literature such as the Epic of Gilgamesh, laws, letters and scientific records. Cuneiform was last used in the 1st century AD.

Egyptian hieroglyphs

Egyptian writing combined logograms, phonetic signs and determinatives in a formal system (hieroglyphs) with cursive counterparts (hieratic, then demotic). Inscriptions from Abydos dated to about 3250–3200 BC are among the earliest. Hieroglyphs were carved on temple walls and written on papyrus for three and a half millennia. Knowledge of the script was lost until the Rosetta Stone — a decree in hieroglyphic, demotic and Greek — enabled Jean-François Champollion's decipherment in 1822.

Chinese characters

The earliest undisputed Chinese writing is the oracle bone script of the late Shang dynasty (c. 1250 BC), used for divination inscriptions on bones and shells. Chinese characters are logographic: each graph represents a morpheme, and tens of thousands of characters exist, though a few thousand suffice for literacy. The script was standardised under the Qin dynasty (221 BC) and spread to Korea, Japan and Vietnam, where it was adapted to local languages.

The alphabet

Semitic-speaking workers in Egypt and Sinai adapted hieroglyphic signs around 1850–1700 BC into the first consonantal alphabet (abjad), Proto-Sinaitic. The Phoenicians' 22-sign script spread through trade; the Greeks adopted it (c. 800 BC) and added vowel letters, creating the first true alphabet. From the Greek alphabet descend the Latin and Cyrillic scripts; from the Phoenicians' Aramaic relatives descend Hebrew, Arabic, and — through Brahmi — the scripts of India and Southeast Asia. Nearly all the world's alphabets trace back to this single invention.

Mesoamerican scripts

In the Americas, Zapotec and Olmec-related peoples developed writing by the first millennium BC. The Maya script, a logosyllabic system of hundreds of glyphs, reached its greatest elaboration in the Classic period (AD 250–900) and was used in stone inscriptions, painted ceramics and folded bark-paper books. Most Maya codices were destroyed in the Spanish conquest; the script was deciphered only in the second half of the 20th century.

Writing materials and printing

Clay, stone, papyrus, parchment and — after its invention in China (traditionally AD 105) — paper successively lowered the cost of writing. Woodblock printing flourished in Tang-dynasty China; Bi Sheng invented movable type around 1040, and metal type was used in Korea before Gutenberg's printing press (c. 1440) transformed Europe, enabling mass-produced books, newspapers and the spread of literacy.

Modern era

The telegraph, typewriter, and finally digital computing changed writing again: text became electronic, searchable and instantaneous. Unicode (from 1991) encodes the world's scripts in a single standard. Despite predictions of its decline, writing in digital form — messages, posts, code — is now produced in greater volume than ever before.
