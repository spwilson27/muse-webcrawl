A Southern blot is a method used for the detection of a specific DNA sequence in DNA samples. Southern blotting combines transfer of electrophoresis-separated DNA fragments to a filter membrane and subsequent fragment detection by probe hybridisation. The method is named after the British biologist Edwin Southern, who first published it in 1975. Other blotting methods (northern, western) that employ similar principles took their names from the "directional" pun on Southern's surname.

Method

Genomic DNA is first cut with one or more restriction enzymes, and the resulting fragments are separated by size on an agarose gel. Because genomic digests contain far too many fragments to see individually, the gel shows a continuous smear; the pattern of interest is invisible until revealed by the probe. The DNA in the gel is denatured with alkali and transferred upward by capillary action (or vacuum or electroblotting) onto a nitrocellulose or nylon membrane, preserving the fragment pattern. After fixing the DNA to the membrane, it is incubated with a labelled single-stranded probe — historically radioactive phosphorus-32 — complementary to the target sequence. Stringent washing removes mismatched probe, and the bound probe is detected by autoradiography or chemiluminescence.

Applications

Southern blotting established many foundational practices of molecular genetics: it was used to map genes, detect mutations and gene rearrangements (for example in leukaemias and in the diagnosis of inherited diseases such as sickle-cell anaemia and Huntington's disease by restriction fragment length polymorphism, RFLP), confirm the integration of transgenes in genetically modified organisms, measure telomere length, and, in its forensic form (DNA fingerprinting, developed by Alec Jeffreys in 1984), to identify individuals. Although PCR-based methods have replaced it for many routine purposes, Southern blotting remains a reference standard for analysing large structural changes and copy number that amplification methods can miss.

Legacy

The 1975 paper describing the technique became one of the most cited in biology, and Southern went on to found Oxford Gene Technology. The compass-point naming joke spawned an entire family of terms, including the northern blot for RNA, the western blot for protein, and tongue-in-cheek coinages such as the "southwestern" blot for DNA-binding proteins.
