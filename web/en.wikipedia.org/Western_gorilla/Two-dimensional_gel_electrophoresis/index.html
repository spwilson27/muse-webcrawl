Two-dimensional gel electrophoresis

A 2D gel: proteins are separated by charge in one dimension and by mass in the other

Two-dimensional gel electrophoresis, abbreviated 2-DE or 2-D electrophoresis, is a form of gel electrophoresis commonly used to analyse proteins by separating them according to two independent properties in two steps. The technique was introduced independently by Patrick O'Farrell and by Joachim Klose in 1975, and for decades it was the central technology of proteomics, capable of resolving several thousand proteins from a cell or tissue sample on a single gel.

Method

In the first dimension, proteins are separated by isoelectric focusing (IEF): they migrate through a pH gradient until each reaches the point (its isoelectric point, pI) where its net charge is zero. In the second dimension, the focused proteins are separated by SDS-PAGE according to molecular mass, with the detergent SDS conferring a uniform negative charge. After electrophoresis, proteins are visualised by staining with Coomassie blue or silver stain, or by fluorescence in the DIGE (difference gel electrophoresis) variant, which allows multiplexed comparison of samples labelled with different fluorescent dyes.

Analysis

The resulting pattern of spots can be digitised and compared between samples to detect changes in protein abundance, for example between healthy and diseased tissue. Spots of interest are excised from the gel, digested with trypsin, and identified by mass spectrometry through peptide mass fingerprinting.

Limitations

Two-dimensional electrophoresis has limited dynamic range, struggles with very hydrophobic membrane proteins and with proteins of extreme size or pI, and is labour-intensive with imperfect reproducibility. It has therefore been largely complemented or replaced in proteomics by liquid-chromatography–mass-spectrometry (shotgun proteomics) methods, although it remains valuable for resolving protein isoforms and post-translational modifications.
