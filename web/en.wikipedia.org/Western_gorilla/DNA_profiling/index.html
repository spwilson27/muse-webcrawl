DNA profiling

An electropherogram of STR alleles used in DNA profiling

DNA profiling (also called DNA fingerprinting or DNA typing) is the process of determining an individual's DNA characteristics in order to distinguish one individual from another. The technique was developed in 1984 by Sir Alec Jeffreys at the University of Leicester, who recognised that certain highly variable regions of DNA — minisatellites — could produce patterns unique to each person. Modern profiling focuses on short tandem repeats (STRs), short sequences repeated a variable number of times at specific loci, amplified by the polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Methods

Early DNA fingerprinting used restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis of variable number tandem repeats (VNTRs) and required relatively large, high-quality samples. Contemporary forensic profiling uses PCR amplification of a standard panel of autosomal STR markers (for example the FBI's CODIS core loci in the United States, expanded to 20 loci in 2017), separated by capillary electrophoresis. Y-chromosome STRs, mitochondrial DNA sequencing and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) panels are used for lineage testing, degraded samples and forensic genetic genealogy.

Applications

DNA profiling is widely used in criminal investigations to link suspects or victims to biological evidence, in paternity and immigration disputes, in identifying human remains after disasters, and in historical and genealogical research — for example, in the identification of the remains of Richard III of England. National DNA databases, such as the UK National DNA Database and CODIS, store profiles from offenders and crime scenes for intelligence-led matching.

Because only a small fraction of the genome is examined, profiles are normally highly discriminating: a full STR profile is expected to be shared by unrelated individuals with a probability far below one in a billion, although identical twins share the same profile.

Issues

Concerns surrounding DNA profiling include contamination and sample mix-ups, the interpretation of mixed or low-template samples, familial searching, database retention of profiles from unconvicted persons, and the privacy implications of forensic genetic genealogy, which was used to identify the Golden State Killer in 2018.
