Monoclonal antibody

Monoclonal antibodies are identical antibodies that bind a single epitope

A monoclonal antibody (mAb, or moAb) is an antibody produced by a single clone of B cells, so that every molecule is identical and binds to the same epitope of an antigen. This contrasts with polyclonal antibodies, which are mixtures produced by many B-cell clones. The technique for producing monoclonal antibodies — the hybridoma method — was developed by Georges Köhler and César Milstein in 1975, earning them a share of the 1984 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Production

In the classical hybridoma method, an animal (usually a mouse) is immunised with the target antigen, and its antibody-producing spleen cells are fused with immortal myeloma cells. The resulting hybridomas are screened and cloned so that each line secretes a single antibody of the desired specificity, which can then be produced in large quantities in culture or in bioreactors. Modern methods also include phage display, single B-cell cloning, and recombinant production of chimeric, humanised and fully human antibodies, whose names carry the substems -ximab, -zumab and -umab respectively.

Applications

Monoclonal antibodies are essential tools in research and diagnostics — used in Western blotting, ELISA, flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry — and form one of the largest classes of modern medicines. Therapeutic examples include trastuzumab (Herceptin) against HER2-positive breast cancer, rituximab against CD20 in B-cell lymphomas, adalimumab (Humira) against TNF in autoimmune disease, checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab in cancer immunotherapy, and antiviral antibodies against Ebola and SARS-CoV-2.

Therapeutic antibodies act by blocking signalling pathways, recruiting immune effector mechanisms such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, or delivering cytotoxic payloads as antibody–drug conjugates. Their specificity generally limits off-target toxicity, although immune reactions and high production costs remain challenges.
