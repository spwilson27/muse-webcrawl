Gene expression

The central dogma: DNA is transcribed to RNA, which is translated into protein

Gene expression is the process by which information encoded in a gene is used to produce a functional product, typically a protein, or a functional RNA molecule such as transfer RNA or ribosomal RNA. It is the means by which the genotype of an organism gives rise to its phenotype. The flow of information from DNA to RNA to protein is often summarised as the central dogma of molecular biology, first framed by Francis Crick.

Stages

In all organisms, expression proceeds through transcription, in which RNA polymerase synthesises an RNA copy of the gene's coding sequence. In eukaryotes, the primary transcript is processed by capping, splicing and polyadenylation to form mature messenger RNA, which is exported from the nucleus. In translation, ribosomes read the mRNA in codons of three nucleotides, assembling amino acids delivered by transfer RNAs into a polypeptide, which then folds into a functional protein, sometimes with post-translational modifications.

Regulation

Gene expression is tightly regulated at every stage, allowing cells to respond to their environment and to differentiate into specialised types despite sharing the same genome. Regulatory mechanisms include transcription factors binding promoters and enhancers, operons in bacteria (such as the lac operon), chromatin modification and DNA methylation (epigenetic regulation), alternative splicing, RNA interference by microRNAs, and control of translation and protein degradation.

Measurement

Expression levels can be measured by Northern blotting, quantitative PCR, DNA microarrays and, most commonly today, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), which profiles the whole transcriptome. Systems biology studies expression patterns across tissues, developmental stages and disease states; misregulation of gene expression underlies many cancers and genetic disorders.
