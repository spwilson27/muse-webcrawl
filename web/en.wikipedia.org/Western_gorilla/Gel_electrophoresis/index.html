Gel electrophoresis

DNA fragments separated by agarose gel electrophoresis, visualised under UV light

Gel electrophoresis is a method for separation and analysis of biomacromolecules (DNA, RNA, proteins, etc.) and their fragments, based on their size and charge. It is used in clinical chemistry to separate proteins by size or charge, in biochemistry and molecular biology to separate DNA and RNA fragments by length and proteins by charge, and in genetics for DNA fingerprinting and sequencing work.

Principle

Biomolecules are placed in wells at one end of a gel — typically agarose for nucleic acids or polyacrylamide for proteins — submerged in a buffer solution. An electric field is applied; because DNA and RNA carry a uniform negative charge from their phosphate backbones, they migrate toward the positive electrode (anode) at rates inversely related to their size, with smaller fragments moving faster and farther through the gel matrix.

Types and visualisation

Agarose gel electrophoresis is the standard method for separating DNA fragments of roughly 100 base pairs to 25 kilobases and is used to check PCR products, restriction digests, and plasmids. Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) resolves smaller fragments and proteins; SDS-PAGE, in which the detergent sodium dodecyl sulfate denatures proteins and gives them a uniform charge, separates proteins by molecular weight. After separation, nucleic acids are stained with fluorescent dyes such as ethidium bromide or safer alternatives and viewed under ultraviolet light; proteins are stained with Coomassie blue or silver stain.

Applications

Gel electrophoresis is fundamental to molecular cloning, genotyping, forensic DNA analysis, paternity testing, diagnosis of genetic disorders, and quality control in biotechnology.
