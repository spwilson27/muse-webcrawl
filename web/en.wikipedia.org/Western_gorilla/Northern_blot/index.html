The northern blot, or RNA blot, is a technique used in molecular biology research to study gene expression by detection of RNA (or isolated mRNA) in a sample. With northern blotting it is possible to observe cellular control over structure and function by determining the particular gene expression rates during differentiation and morphogenesis, as well as in abnormal or diseased conditions. The technique was developed in 1977 by James Alwine, David Kemp and George Stark at Stanford University, and named in playful reference to the Southern blot, its DNA-detecting predecessor.

Procedure

Total RNA is extracted from a tissue or cell sample and separated by size using gel electrophoresis under denaturing conditions (typically an agarose gel containing formaldehyde), so that the RNA migrates according to its length. The separated RNA is then transferred ("blotted") onto a nylon or nitrocellulose membrane and immobilised by baking or ultraviolet crosslinking. The membrane is incubated with a labelled single-stranded DNA or RNA probe complementary to the sequence of interest; after washing away unbound probe, the signal is detected — historically by autoradiography with radioactive labels, today often by chemiluminescence or fluorescence. A ladder of RNA standards and a "housekeeping" gene band (such as actin) serve as controls.

Applications

Northern blotting measures both the abundance and the size of a specific transcript, and can reveal alternative splicing, processing intermediates and degradation products that amplification-based methods may miss. It has been used to compare gene expression between tissues and developmental stages, to verify results from microarrays and RNA sequencing, and to study viral RNAs and non-coding RNAs such as microRNA precursors.

Advantages and disadvantages

Compared with RT-PCR and RNA-seq, northern blotting is labour-intensive, requires relatively large amounts of RNA and is less sensitive and less quantitative; it also involves hazardous reagents. Its enduring advantages are that it reports the actual size of the RNA and requires no amplification, avoiding amplification artefacts. Variants include the reverse northern blot, in which the labelled material is the RNA sample itself and the targets are fixed to the membrane.
