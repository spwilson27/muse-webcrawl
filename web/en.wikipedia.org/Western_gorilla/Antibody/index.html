An antibody (Ab), also known as an immunoglobulin (Ig), is a large, Y-shaped protein used by the immune system to identify and neutralise foreign objects such as pathogenic bacteria and viruses. The antibody recognises a unique molecule of the pathogen, called an antigen. Each tip of the "Y" of an antibody contains a paratope (analogous to a lock) that is specific for one particular epitope (analogous to a key) on an antigen, allowing these two structures to bind together with precision. Antibodies are produced by specialised white blood cells called plasma cells, which develop from B cells.

Structure

An antibody consists of four polypeptide chains — two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains — joined by disulfide bonds into a Y shape. The arms of the Y (the Fab regions) end in variable domains whose hypervariable loops form the antigen-binding site; the stem (the Fc region) is constant within a class and interacts with cell receptors and complement. In humans there are five heavy-chain classes, giving the isotypes IgG (about 75% of serum antibody), IgA, IgM, IgD and IgE, each suited to a different role and location in the body — IgA guards mucous surfaces, IgM is the first antibody of a new infection, and IgE mediates responses to parasites and allergens.

Diversity and production

The immune system can generate an astronomically large repertoire of antibodies by V(D)J recombination, which assembles variable gene segments in developing B cells, followed by somatic hypermutation during an immune response, which fine-tunes binding affinity. When a B cell's surface antibody binds its antigen, the cell proliferates and differentiates into plasma cells that secrete the same antibody in soluble form, and into long-lived memory cells.

Functions

Antibodies protect the body by neutralising viruses and toxins directly; by opsonisation, coating pathogens so phagocytes ingest them more readily; by agglutination, clumping microbes together; and by activating the complement system, which punches holes in microbial membranes. Maternal IgG crosses the placenta, giving newborns temporary passive immunity.

Medical and scientific uses

Antibodies are central tools of modern medicine and research. Monoclonal antibodies — identical antibodies produced by cloned hybridoma cells (technique of Köhler and Milstein, 1975) — are used as drugs against cancer, autoimmune disease and infection (their names end in -mab, as in trastuzumab and adalimumab), and in diagnostics such as the ELISA test, pregnancy tests and rapid antigen tests. Antibodies are also used in laboratory techniques including western blotting, immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry.
