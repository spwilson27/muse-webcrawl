B cell

A B cell recognises antigen through its surface immunoglobulin (the B-cell receptor) and can develop into an antibody-secreting plasma cell.

TypeLymphocyte (white blood cell)
SystemAdaptive immune system
Site of maturationBone marrow (mammals); bursa of Fabricius (birds)
Principal functionAntibody production (humoral immunity); antigen presentation; cytokine secretion
Effector formsPlasma cells; memory B cells

B cells, also known as B lymphocytes, are a type of white blood cell of the lymphocyte subtype. They function in the humoral immunity component of the adaptive immune system. B cells produce antibody molecules, which circulate in the blood and lymph and neutralise pathogens and mark them for destruction; unlike T cells, B cells can recognise free antigen directly through their B-cell receptors.

Development

B cells develop from haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow (in birds, in the bursa of Fabricius — the source of the letter "B"). During development each B cell assembles a unique antigen receptor by V(D)J recombination of immunoglobulin genes; cells that react strongly to self antigens are eliminated or inactivated to maintain self-tolerance.

Activation and function

When a naive B cell encounters its specific antigen — usually with help from a T helper cell in a lymph node germinal centre — it proliferates and differentiates. In germinal centres, B cells undergo somatic hypermutation and affinity maturation, improving antibody quality, and class-switch recombination, changing the antibody's effector class (IgM, IgG, IgA, IgE). The result is long-lived plasma cells that secrete large quantities of antibody and memory B cells that respond rapidly on re-exposure — the cellular basis of vaccine-induced immunity.

Clinical significance

Defects in B cells cause antibody deficiencies such as X-linked agammaglobulinaemia and common variable immunodeficiency. Malignancies of B cells include most non-Hodgkin lymphomas, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and multiple myeloma, while autoantibodies produced by B cells drive diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus. Therapies such as rituximab deliberately deplete B cells.
