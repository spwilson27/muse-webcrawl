Western blot

Western blot
Type: Analytical technique (immunoblot)
Purpose: Detect specific proteins in a sample
Introduced: 1979 (Towbin et al.; Burnette)

The western blot (sometimes called the protein immunoblot) is a widely used analytical technique in molecular biology, immunogenetics and biochemistry to detect specific proteins in a sample of tissue homogenate or extract. It uses gel electrophoresis to separate native or denatured proteins by length or three-dimensional structure, which are then transferred to a membrane where they are probed with antibodies specific to the target protein.

Procedure

Proteins in a sample are first denatured and separated by size using SDS-PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis). The separated proteins are transferred ("blotted") onto a nitrocellulose or PVDF membrane, preserving their pattern. The membrane is blocked to prevent non-specific binding, incubated with a primary antibody against the protein of interest, then with a secondary antibody carrying an enzyme or fluorescent label. Substrate reactions produce a signal at the position of the target protein, revealing its presence and approximate size and quantity.

Name and uses

The name is a play on the Southern blot, the earlier technique for DNA named after Edwin Southern; the RNA version was jokingly dubbed the northern blot, and the protein technique the western blot. The method was described in 1979 by Harry Towbin and colleagues and by W. Neal Burnette. Western blotting has been used in research and diagnostics — famously as a confirmatory test for HIV antibodies and for prion diseases such as BSE — though its diagnostic use has declined with newer methods.
