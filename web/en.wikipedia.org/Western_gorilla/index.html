The western gorilla (Gorilla gorilla) is a great ape found in the rainforests of west-central Africa, including Cameroon, the Central African Republic, the Republic of the Congo, Gabon, Equatorial Guinea and Nigeria. It is the most numerous and widespread of the two gorilla species (the other being the eastern gorilla, Gorilla beringei), and nearly all gorillas in zoos are western lowland gorillas. Two subspecies are recognised: the western lowland gorilla (G. g. gorilla) and the Cross River gorilla (G. g. diehli), the latter confined to the Nigeria–Cameroon border region and numbering only a few hundred individuals.

Description and behaviour

Western gorillas are slightly smaller and lighter-haired than eastern gorillas, with brown-grey coats and, in mature males, a silver-grey saddle ("silverbacks") and a distinctive reddish-brown crest. Adults are powerful: males may stand 1.7 m and weigh 140–180 kg in the wild. They live in stable family groups, typically of a dominant silverback, several females and their offspring, travelling a few kilometres a day as they forage on fruit, leaves, stems and insects. They build fresh nests of vegetation each night, on the ground or in trees. Communication includes chest-beating displays, vocalisations and gestures; tool use — such as using sticks to gauge water depth — has been observed in the wild.

Threats and conservation

The species is listed as Critically Endangered. Populations fell sharply from the 1980s through hunting for bushmeat, habitat loss from logging, mining and agriculture, and catastrophic Ebola virus outbreaks that killed large proportions of some populations — declines that led to the upgraded listing despite the species' relative abundance. Conservation measures include protected areas such as Nouabalé-Ndoki and Odzala-Kokoua national parks in the Congo, anti-poaching patrols, and great-ape tourism under strict health rules.
