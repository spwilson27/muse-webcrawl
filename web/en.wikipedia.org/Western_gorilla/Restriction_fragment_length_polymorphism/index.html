Restriction fragment length polymorphism

RFLP banding patterns separated by gel electrophoresis

Restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) is a technique and a form of genetic variation in which differences in DNA sequences between individuals produce fragments of different lengths when the DNA is cut with restriction enzymes. Because restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific recognition sites, a mutation that creates or destroys such a site, or an insertion or deletion between sites, changes the sizes of the resulting fragments, which can be detected by gel electrophoresis and Southern blotting with a labelled probe.

Technique

In classical RFLP analysis, genomic DNA is digested with one or more restriction endonucleases, the fragments are separated by size on an agarose gel, transferred to a membrane, and hybridised with a radioactive or fluorescent probe complementary to the locus of interest. Variation at variable number tandem repeat (VNTR) loci produces large fragment-length differences between individuals — the basis of the original "DNA fingerprinting" method developed by Alec Jeffreys in 1984 (see DNA profiling).

Applications

RFLPs were among the first widely used genetic markers. They were used to construct the first genetic linkage maps of the human genome, to track the inheritance of disease genes such as those for Huntington's disease and cystic fibrosis in families, in paternity testing, and in forensic identification before PCR-based methods took over. In plant and animal breeding, RFLP markers assisted in selecting for desirable traits.

Because the method requires relatively large amounts of intact DNA and is slow, it has largely been superseded by PCR-based techniques, including PCR-RFLP (in which a small region is amplified first, then digested), short tandem repeat profiling, and single-nucleotide polymorphism analysis. The first human genetic map, published in 1987 by Helen Donis-Keller and colleagues, was based on RFLP markers.
