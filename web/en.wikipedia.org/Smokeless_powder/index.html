Smokeless powder is a type of propellant used in firearms and artillery that produces very little visible smoke when fired, unlike the older black powder it replaced. It is based on nitrocellulose, sometimes combined with nitroglycerin or nitroguanidine, and burns with far greater energy per unit mass than black powder, enabling smaller cartridges and higher muzzle velocities.

History

Nitrocellulose ("guncotton") was discovered in 1846 but was too violent and unstable for direct use as a propellant. In 1884 the French chemist Paul Vieille gelatinized nitrocellulose with alcohol and ether to produce Poudre B, the first practical smokeless powder, adopted with the Lebel Model 1886 rifle. Alfred Nobel's ballistite (1887) and the British cordite (1889) added nitroglycerin to the formulation. By the First World War smokeless powder had replaced black powder in virtually all military ammunition.

Composition and forms

Single-base powders consist mainly of nitrocellulose. Double-base powders add nitroglycerin for higher energy. Triple-base powders add nitroguanidine, which reduces flame temperature and muzzle flash in large guns. The powder is manufactured as flakes, balls, or extruded sticks and cylinders; grain shape and coatings control the burning rate so pressure builds progressively as the projectile travels down the barrel.

Advantages

Compared with black powder, smokeless powder produces little obscuring smoke, far less corrosive fouling, and roughly three times the energy, allowing flatter trajectories and longer range. Its relative insensitivity to moisture also improved reliability of ammunition in storage and field use.

Safety and stability

Stabilizers such as diphenylamine are added to absorb decomposition products that would otherwise cause dangerous autocatalytic breakdown. Properly stored smokeless powder remains usable for decades; degraded powder is a fire and explosion hazard and must be disposed of.
