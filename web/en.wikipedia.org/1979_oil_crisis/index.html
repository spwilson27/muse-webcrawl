1979 oil crisis

Period1979 – early 1980s
TriggerIranian Revolution; Iran–Iraq War
Price peak~US$39.50 per barrel (1979–80)
Preceded by1973 oil crisis

The 1979 oil crisis, also known as the second oil crisis or the 1979 oil shock, occurred in the wake of the Iranian Revolution. Disruption to Iranian oil production, compounded by the outbreak of the Iran–Iraq War in 1980, caused world oil prices to more than double, producing fuel shortages, long queues at filling stations, and a major shock to the global economy.

Causes

As strikes gripped Iran's oilfields in late 1978, the country's production fell sharply, removing roughly 4–5 million barrels per day — around 7 percent of world supply — at the start of 1979. Although other producers partly compensated, panic buying and hoarding by refiners magnified the shortfall. Iran's exports resumed under the new Islamic Republic but at a reduced level, and the September 1980 Iraqi invasion halted much of both countries' exports.

Effects

In the United States, the Carter administration imposed price controls and odd–even rationing was adopted in several states; the crisis damaged President Jimmy Carter politically and prompted his "crisis of confidence" address of July 1979. Prices peaked near $39.50 a barrel before demand destruction, conservation, new non-OPEC supply from the North Sea, Alaska, and Mexico, and recession in the industrialized world produced a long decline — the 1980s oil glut.

The crisis accelerated interest in fuel-efficient vehicles, benefiting Japanese automakers, and in energy efficiency and alternative energy more generally.
