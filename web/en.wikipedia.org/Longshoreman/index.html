Longshoreman

A longshoreman (also known as a stevedore or docker) is a waterfront worker responsible for loading and unloading cargo from ships in ports and harbors. The term originates from "along the shore" — men who worked along the shore handling freight. Longshoremen historically performed physically demanding manual labor, moving cargo with hooks, ropes, and cranes; modern containerization has transformed the job toward operating heavy machinery such as gantry cranes, straddle carriers, and forklifts.

History

Before containerization, cargo was shipped "break bulk" — in barrels, crates, sacks, and bales — and gangs of longshoremen loaded each item by hand, often hired casually through the "shape-up" system in which workers gathered at the dock each morning hoping to be chosen. The introduction of the shipping container in the 1950s and 1960s dramatically reduced the need for manual labor but increased productivity and safety, leading to negotiated agreements between unions and employers over job guarantees.

Union organization

Longshoremen are among the most strongly unionized workers in many countries. In the United States, the International Longshoremen's Association (ILA) represents workers on the East and Gulf coasts, while the International Longshore and Warehouse Union (ILWU) represents West Coast workers. The 1934 West Coast Waterfront Strike, led by Harry Bridges, was a landmark in American labor history and established the union hiring hall.

Modern work

Today's longshoremen operate container cranes, lash and unlash containers, drive terminal tractors, and handle specialized cargo such as automobiles and refrigerated goods. The work remains hazardous, and licensing and safety training are standard in major ports.
