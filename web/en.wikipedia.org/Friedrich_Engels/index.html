Friedrich Engels

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Friedrich Engels (28 November 1820 – 5 August 1895) was a German philosopher, political theoretician, historian, and businessman. Together with Karl Marx, he developed the theory now known as Marxism. In 1848 the two co-authored The Communist Manifesto, and Engels later edited and published the second and third volumes of Marx's Das Kapital from Marx's notes after his death.

Early life and Manchester

Engels was born in Barmen (now part of Wuppertal) into a wealthy family of textile manufacturers. Sent to Manchester in 1842 to work in the family's cotton firm, Ermen & Engels, he observed industrial working-class life at first hand. The result was his first major book, The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845), a pioneering work of urban social investigation.

Collaboration with Marx

Engels met Marx in Paris in 1844, beginning a lifelong intellectual partnership. They wrote The Holy Family and The German Ideology together before producing The Communist Manifesto in 1848. Engels's income from the family business in Manchester financially supported Marx's research in London for decades. After Marx's death in 1883, Engels became the leading authority on Marx's thought, preparing volumes II and III of Capital for publication (1885 and 1894).

Later works

In Anti-Dühring (1878) and its popular abridgement Socialism: Utopian and Scientific (1880), Engels set out a systematic account of Marxist philosophy, political economy, and socialism. The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884) applied historical materialism to anthropology and the position of women. His unfinished manuscripts on the dialectics of nature influenced later debates about Marxism and science.

Death and legacy

Engels died of cancer in London in 1895; his ashes were scattered off Beachy Head. His systematisation of Marx's ideas shaped the socialist and communist movements of the twentieth century, and his Manchester writings remain landmarks in social history.
