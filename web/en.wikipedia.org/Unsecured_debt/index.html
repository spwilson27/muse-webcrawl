Unsecured debt

In finance, unsecured debt refers to any type of debt or general obligation that is not protected by a guarantor, or collateralised by a lien on specific assets of the borrower in the case of a bankruptcy or liquidation or failure to meet the terms for repayment. Unsecured debts are sometimes called signature debt because the borrower's signature on the loan agreement is the only security offered.

Characteristics

Because unsecured creditors have no claim on a specific asset, unsecured lending carries greater risk for the lender than secured debt. Lenders compensate by charging higher interest rates, screening borrowers using credit scores and credit history, and limiting loan amounts. If the borrower defaults, the lender's remedies are generally limited to suing for repayment or reporting the default to credit bureaus, unless the debt is written off or discharged.

Examples

Common forms of unsecured debt include credit card debt, personal loans and signature loans, student loans, medical bills, most corporate bonds (debentures), and unsecured promissory notes. By contrast, mortgages and car loans are typically secured by the property or vehicle purchased.

Treatment in bankruptcy

In bankruptcy proceedings, unsecured creditors rank behind secured creditors in the order of repayment. Claims are generally divided into priority unsecured claims — such as certain taxes, child support and administrative expenses — which are paid first, and general unsecured claims, which are paid only from whatever assets remain, often receiving only a fraction of the amount owed. In consumer bankruptcy, many unsecured debts such as credit card balances may be discharged entirely.

Risks

For borrowers, the accessibility of unsecured credit can lead to unsustainable debt burdens because of high interest rates and compounding. For lenders and investors, unsecured debt carries higher expected losses, which is reflected in credit ratings and the yields demanded on unsecured corporate bonds.
