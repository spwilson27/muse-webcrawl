Cavalieri's principle

In geometry, Cavalieri's principle, a modern application of the method of indivisibles, is a principle formulated by the Italian mathematician Bonaventura Cavalieri in the 17th century. It states that two solids have equal volumes if, for every plane parallel to a given plane, the cross-sections of the two solids have equal areas. A two-dimensional analogue states that two planar regions have equal areas if every line parallel to a given line cuts them in chords of equal length.

History

Cavalieri developed the idea in his Geometria indivisibilibus (1635), building on earlier work by Kepler and, independently, on ideas known in China through Zu Gengzhi in the 5th century — where it is often called Zu's principle. The principle was an important precursor of integral calculus.

Applications

Cavalieri's principle gives simple derivations of the volumes of spheres, cones, and other solids: a hemisphere, for example, has at every height the same cross-sectional area as a cylinder with a cone removed, so its volume follows from those familiar volumes. In modern terms, the principle is a special case of Fubini's theorem in measure theory.
