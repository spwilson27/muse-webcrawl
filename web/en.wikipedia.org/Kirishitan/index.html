Kirishitan

Kirishitan (Japanese: キリシタン) was the Japanese term for Christians in early modern Japan, from the Portuguese cristão. It refers to the Japanese Catholics converted by Jesuit, Franciscan, and Dominican missionaries from 1549 onward, and to their descendants who kept the faith secretly during two centuries of prohibition.

Mission era

St Francis Xavier arrived at Kagoshima in 1549. Missionaries gained converts especially in Kyushu, where daimyo such as Ōmura Sumitada (baptised 1563) adopted Christianity, and by 1600 there may have been 300,000 Japanese Christians. A Tenshō embassy of four Japanese youths toured Europe in 1582–1590, and Kirishitan culture produced churches, seminaries, printing presses, and the first Japanese dictionaries and grammars.

Persecution

Toyotomi Hideyoshi issued anti-Christian edicts from 1587 and crucified the Twenty-six Martyrs at Nagasaki in 1597. The Tokugawa shogunate banned Christianity outright in 1614, expelled missionaries, and enforced apostasy through the fumi-e (trampling on sacred images) and temple-registration systems. The Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–38, largely a Kirishitan uprising, was crushed, after which Japan closed itself to the West. Survivors — the Kakure Kirishitan ("hidden Christians") — preserved baptisms, prayers (orasho), and a syncretic faith in secret around Nagasaki and the Gotō Islands.

Rediscovery and heritage

When missionaries returned in 1865, thousands of hidden Christians revealed themselves at Ōura Cathedral in Nagasaki. The Hidden Christian Sites in the Nagasaki Region were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018.
