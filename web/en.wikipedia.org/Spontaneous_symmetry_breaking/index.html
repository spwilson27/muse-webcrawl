Spontaneous symmetry breaking

Spontaneous symmetry breaking is a spontaneous process of symmetry breaking that occurs in a physical system when its ground state breaks a symmetry of the system's equations, while the equations themselves remain symmetric. The system settles into one of several equally valid lowest-energy states, and the choice among them — made by chance or by tiny perturbations — breaks the symmetry in practice.

Examples

A classic illustration is a ferromagnet: above the Curie temperature the atomic spins point randomly and the material is rotationally symmetric, but below it the spins align in one particular direction, breaking the symmetry. A mechanical analogue is a ball balanced on the central peak of a "Mexican hat" potential: it must roll down to one point on the circular rim, though every point on the rim is equivalent.

Goldstone's theorem and the Higgs mechanism

Goldstone's theorem states that breaking a continuous global symmetry produces massless bosons — Nambu–Goldstone bosons. When the symmetry is a local gauge symmetry, the would-be Goldstone bosons are instead absorbed by the gauge fields, which acquire mass: this is the Higgs mechanism. In the Standard Model, spontaneous breaking of the electroweak symmetry by the Higgs field gives mass to the W and Z bosons and to the elementary fermions, while leaving the photon massless. The Higgs boson, predicted as the excitation of this field, was discovered at CERN in 2012.

History

The idea was developed in condensed-matter physics and imported into particle physics by Yoichiro Nambu in 1960, drawing on the theory of superconductivity. Jeffrey Goldstone, Abdus Salam and Steven Weinberg formalised the theorem for relativistic fields in 1962, and in 1964 Peter Higgs, François Englert and Robert Brout, and Gerald Guralnik, C. R. Hagen and Tom Kibble independently showed how gauge bosons could gain mass through the mechanism that now bears Higgs's name.
