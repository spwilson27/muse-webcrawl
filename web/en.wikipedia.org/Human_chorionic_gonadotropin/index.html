Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) is a hormone produced mainly by the syncytiotrophoblast of the placenta during pregnancy. It is a glycoprotein composed of alpha and beta subunits; the beta subunit (β-hCG) is unique to the hormone and is what pregnancy tests detect in blood and urine.

Function

hCG rescues the corpus luteum, sustaining progesterone production until the placenta takes over steroid synthesis around weeks 8–10. Levels rise rapidly in early pregnancy, roughly doubling every 48–72 hours, peak near the end of the first trimester and then decline. hCG also weakly stimulates the thyroid and is implicated in nausea of pregnancy.

Clinical uses

Quantitative β-hCG measurement helps assess viability and location of pregnancy (including ectopic pregnancy) and monitors gestational trophoblastic disease, where very high levels occur in molar pregnancies and choriocarcinoma. hCG is also a tumour marker for some germ-cell cancers. Pharmaceutical hCG is used in fertility treatment to trigger ovulation and, controversially and prohibited in sport, to stimulate testosterone production.
