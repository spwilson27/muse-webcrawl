In rail transport, track gauge is the distance between the two rails of a railway track, measured between the inner faces of the load-bearing rails. All vehicles on a network must have running gear compatible with the gauge, and it is one of the chief barriers to interoperability between systems: where networks of different gauges meet, a "break of gauge" forces passengers and freight to change trains, or requires bogie exchange or variable-gauge axles.

Standard gauge

The most common gauge in the world, used by roughly 55–60% of railways, is 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in), called standard gauge. It descends from the early English colliery wagonways and George Stephenson's railways, was fixed for Britain by the Gauge Act of 1846 after defeating Brunel's 7 ft ¼ in broad gauge, and spread worldwide with British and later American engineering. It is used across North America, most of Europe, China, and nearly all high-speed rail.

Broad and narrow gauges

Broad gauges include Russian gauge (1,520 mm) across the former Soviet Union and Finland, Iberian gauge (1,668 mm) in Spain and Portugal, and Indian gauge (1,676 mm), the widest in regular use. Narrow gauges — such as Cape gauge (1,067 mm) in southern Africa, Japan, and Queensland, and metre gauge in Southeast Asia and parts of Europe — were cheaper to build in difficult terrain and remain extensive.

Break of gauge

Breaks of gauge persist at frontiers such as Spain–France, Poland–Ukraine, and China–Mongolia. Solutions have included transshipment, dual-gauge track, bogie exchange, and variable-gauge trainsets such as Spain's Talgo and CAF systems, which pass through gauge changers without stopping for long.
