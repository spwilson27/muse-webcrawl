Economic indicator

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An economic indicator is a statistic about an economic activity. Economic indicators allow analysis of economic performance and predictions of future performance. One application of economic indicators is the study of business cycles. Economic indicators include various indices, earnings reports, and economic summaries: for example, the unemployment rate, quits rate, housing starts, consumer price index (a measure for inflation), inverted yield curve, consumer leverage ratio, industrial production, bankruptcies, gross domestic product, broadband internet penetration, retail sales, price index, and money supply changes.

Classification by timing

Economic indicators are usually classified according to their timing relative to the business cycle:

  • Leading indicators change before the economy as a whole changes, and are therefore useful as short-term predictors. Examples include stock market returns, building permits, initial unemployment claims, manufacturing new orders, and the yield curve.
  • Coincident indicators change at approximately the same time as the whole economy, thereby providing information about the current state of the economy. Examples include GDP, industrial production, personal income, and employment.
  • Lagging indicators change after the whole economy changes. Examples include the unemployment rate, corporate profits, and the consumer price index.

Composite indices

Statistical agencies and private organisations combine multiple indicators into composite indices. In the United States, The Conference Board publishes the Leading Economic Index (LEI), a composite of ten leading indicators designed to signal peaks and troughs in the business cycle. Many indicators are published by government agencies such as the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the Bureau of Economic Analysis.

Accuracy

Economic indicators are frequently subject to revision as more complete data become available, and their predictive record is imperfect: leading indicators have on occasion signalled recessions that did not occur. Analysts therefore typically consult a broad range of indicators rather than relying on any single series.
