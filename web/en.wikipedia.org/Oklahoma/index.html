Oklahoma is a state in the South Central region of the United States. It borders Texas to the south and west, Kansas to the north, Missouri to the northeast, Arkansas to the east, New Mexico to the west, and Colorado to the northwest. Its name derives from the Choctaw words okla ("people") and humma ("red"), and it has one of the largest Native American populations of any state; 39 federally recognised tribal nations are headquartered there.

History

In the 1830s the area was designated Indian Territory, the destination of the forced removals of the "Five Civilized Tribes" along the Trail of Tears. After the Civil War, parts of the territory were opened to non-Native settlement in famous Land Runs beginning in 1889; settlers who jumped the gun early were dubbed "Sooners". Oklahoma Territory and Indian Territory were combined into the state of Oklahoma in 1907. Oil booms enriched Tulsa ("Oil Capital of the World") and Oklahoma City, while the Dust Bowl of the 1930s drove mass migration to California—the "Okies" of Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath. In 1995, the Oklahoma City bombing killed 168 people, then the deadliest act of domestic terrorism in US history.

Geography and climate

Oklahoma spans ten distinct ecological regions, from the Ozark and Ouachita highlands in the east to the High Plains of the panhandle, with more man-made lakes than any other state. It lies in Tornado Alley and experiences frequent severe thunderstorms; the 3 May 1999 Bridge Creek–Moore tornado and the May 2013 El Reno tornado (the widest ever recorded) are among the most powerful measured.

Economy and culture

Energy (oil and natural gas), agriculture, aviation, and biotechnology anchor the economy. The state is a centre of Western swing (Bob Wills) and country music, and its cultural heritage includes large Native American, cowboy, and African American traditions—Tulsa's Greenwood district, site of the 1921 race massacre, is now a nationally significant historic site.
