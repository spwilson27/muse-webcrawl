Dispersion (optics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In optics and in wave propagation in general, dispersion is the phenomenon in which the phase velocity of a wave depends on its frequency; sometimes the term chromatic dispersion is used for specificity to optics. A medium having this common property may be termed a dispersive medium.

Although the term is used in the field of optics to describe light and other electromagnetic waves, dispersion in the same sense can apply to any sort of wave motion such as acoustic waves and seismic waves, and in telecommunications signals along transmission lines.

Examples

The most familiar example of dispersion is a rainbow, in which dispersion causes the spatial separation of a white light into components of different wavelengths (different colors). In lenses, dispersion causes chromatic aberration, a failure of the lens to focus all colors to the same point.

Material dispersion

Material dispersion arises because the refractive index of a transparent material varies with wavelength. It is commonly quantified by the Abbe number and modeled by the Sellmeier equation. In most transparent media the refractive index decreases with increasing wavelength, so blue light is refracted more strongly than red light; this is called normal dispersion.

Group-velocity dispersion

In waveguides and optical fibers, group-velocity dispersion causes pulses to broaden as they propagate, limiting the data rate of fiber-optic communication systems. Dispersion compensation techniques and engineered fiber profiles are used to manage this effect.

Applications

Dispersion is exploited in prisms and gratings for spectroscopy, in chirped pulse amplification for ultrashort laser pulses, and in pulse compression. Conversely, it must be corrected in high-quality camera lenses and telescopes using achromatic and apochromatic designs combining glasses of different dispersive powers.
