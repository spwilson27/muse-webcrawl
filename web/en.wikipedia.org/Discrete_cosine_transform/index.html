Discrete cosine transform

A discrete cosine transform (DCT) expresses a finite sequence of data points in terms of a sum of cosine functions oscillating at different frequencies. The DCT, first proposed by Nasir Ahmed in 1972, is a widely used transformation technique in signal processing and data compression. It is used in most digital media, including digital images (such as JPEG and HEIF), digital video (such as MPEG and H.26x), digital audio (such as Dolby Digital, MP3 and AAC), and speech coding.

The use of cosine rather than sine functions is critical for compression, since it turns out (as described below) that fewer cosine functions are needed to approximate a typical signal. Formally, the DCT is a linear, invertible function from RN to RN, equivalently an invertible N × N square matrix.

Definition

The most common variant, the type-II DCT, is defined for a sequence x0, …, xN−1 by

Xk = Σn=0..N−1 xn cos[ π/N (n + ½) k ],  k = 0, …, N−1.

Its inverse is the type-III DCT. There are eight standard variants, differing in boundary conditions; the DCT-II and DCT-III pair is often simply called "the DCT" and "the inverse DCT".

Relation to the Fourier transform

The DCT is closely related to the discrete Fourier transform: it is equivalent to a DFT of roughly twice the length operating on real data with even symmetry. This even extension avoids the artificial discontinuities at the boundaries that make the DFT inefficient at compacting the energy of smooth signals, which is the key to the DCT's success in compression.

Applications

The two-dimensional DCT-II on 8 × 8 blocks is the heart of the JPEG image compression standard. Variants such as the modified discrete cosine transform (MDCT), with overlapping blocks, are used in audio codecs including MP3, AAC and Vorbis.
