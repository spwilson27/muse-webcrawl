Geothermal energy

Geothermal energy is thermal energy extracted from the Earth's crust. It combines energy from the formation of the planet and from radioactive decay. The geothermal gradient — the difference in temperature between the core and the surface — drives continuous conduction of thermal energy, which can be used for electricity generation, direct heating, district heating, and ground-source heat pumps.

Electricity generation

Conventional geothermal power plants are built where hydrothermal resources bring hot water or steam near the surface, typically at tectonic plate boundaries. Dry steam, flash steam, and binary cycle plants convert the heat to electricity; the world's first generator was tested at Larderello, Italy, in 1904, and the largest complex, The Geysers in California, has a capacity of about 1.5 gigawatts. Leading producers include the United States, Indonesia, the Philippines, Turkey, New Zealand, Mexico, Kenya, and Iceland, where geothermal supplies a large share of electricity and most space heating.

Direct use and heat pumps

Lower-temperature resources are used directly for bathing, greenhouses, aquaculture, and district heating; Reykjavík's district heating system is a prominent example. Ground-source heat pumps exploit the near-constant temperature of shallow ground for efficient heating and cooling almost anywhere, and form the fastest-growing segment of geothermal use.

Enhanced geothermal systems

Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) create artificial reservoirs by fracturing hot dry rock and circulating water through it, potentially expanding geothermal power far beyond natural hydrothermal fields. Challenges include drilling costs, water loss, and induced seismicity, which has affected projects such as Basel, Switzerland.
