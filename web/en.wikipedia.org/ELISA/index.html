The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is a commonly used analytical biochemistry assay, first described by Eva Engvall and Peter Perlmann in 1971. The assay is a plate-based technique designed for detecting and quantifying soluble substances such as peptides, proteins, antibodies, and hormones, by using antibodies directed against the analyte and an enzyme label whose reaction with a substrate produces a measurable (usually colour) signal.

Principle

In an ELISA, an antigen or antibody is immobilised on a solid surface, typically the wells of a 96-well polystyrene microplate. After binding of the target from the sample, a detection antibody linked to an enzyme (commonly horseradish peroxidase or alkaline phosphatase) is added; after washing away unbound material, a chromogenic substrate is added and the enzyme converts it into a coloured product. The intensity of colour, read by a spectrophotometer (plate reader), is proportional to the amount of analyte.

Types

  • Direct ELISA — antigen is immobilised and detected by an enzyme-labelled antibody.
  • Indirect ELISA — a labelled secondary antibody detects the primary antibody; used widely for antibody screening, e.g. HIV testing.
  • Sandwich ELISA — the antigen is captured between two antibodies, giving high specificity and sensitivity.
  • Competitive (inhibition) ELISA — sample analyte competes with a reference, so signal is inversely proportional to concentration.

Applications

ELISA is a mainstay of clinical diagnostics (infectious diseases including HIV, hepatitis, dengue; pregnancy tests are related immunochromatographic devices), allergy testing, food-industry allergen and pathogen detection, veterinary medicine, and research quantification of cytokines and other proteins. As a "wet lab" benchmark it complements methods such as Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and immuno-PCR.

History

ELISA grew out of radioimmunoassay (RIA), developed by Rosalyn Yalow and Solomon Berson in the 1960s; replacing radioactive labels with enzymes removed hazards and enabled automation. Engvall and Perlmann, and independently Anton Schuurs and Bauke van Weemen, published the enzyme immunoassay in 1971. The technique was adapted for the first licensed HIV blood-screening tests in 1985, transforming blood-supply safety.
