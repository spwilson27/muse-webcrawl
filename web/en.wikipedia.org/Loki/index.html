Loki is a god in Norse mythology. The son of the jötunn Fárbauti and Laufey, and the blood-brother of Odin, Loki is a shape-shifter who both helps and hinders the gods, and whose nature has been debated by scholars since the earliest studies of the myths. He is attested in the Poetic Edda and Prose Edda, compiled in 13th-century Iceland from earlier traditional material.

Mythology

Loki's schemes frequently put the gods in danger, which he then resolves through cunning: he is responsible for the gods acquiring Odin's spear Gungnir, Thor's hammer Mjölnir, and the ship Skíðblaðnir. Having turned into a mare to distract a stallion, he gave birth to Odin's eight-legged horse Sleipnir. By the giantess Angrboða he fathered the wolf Fenrir, the world-serpent Jörmungandr, and Hel, ruler of the dead.

Loki's role darkens as the myths approach Ragnarök. He engineered the death of Baldr by guiding the blind god Höðr to throw a dart of mistletoe, and taunted the gods at Ægir's feast in the poem Lokasenna. As punishment he was bound in a cave with the entrails of his son, a serpent dripping venom onto his face; his faithful wife Sigyn catches the drops in a bowl, and his writhing when she empties it is said to cause earthquakes. At Ragnarök he will break free, steer the ship Naglfar carrying the dead, and kill and be killed by the god Heimdall.

Interpretation and legacy

Scholars have variously interpreted Loki as a trickster, a fire spirit (a theory due to a mistaken association with the god Logi), and a figure of necessary chaos. Whether he was ever worshipped is doubtful — no place names or cult sites are clearly associated with him — but he remains the most psychologically vivid character of Norse myth, and his modern popularity has been amplified by his portrayal in Marvel comics and films.
