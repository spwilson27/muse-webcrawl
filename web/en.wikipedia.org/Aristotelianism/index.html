Aristotelianism

Aristotelianism is a philosophical tradition inspired by the work of Aristotle, usually characterised by deductive logic and an analytic inductive method in the study of natural philosophy and metaphysics. It covers the treatment of the social sciences under a system of natural law.

It answers the question raised by Plato's Theory of Forms with the hypothesis of the world as composed of substances, each with matter and form entwined. It uses the concept of final causality (purpose) as an explanation of natural phenomena, and the concept of the unmoved mover as the first cause of the world.

History

Ancient and medieval periods

After Aristotle's death, his school, the Peripatetics, preserved and developed his thought in the Hellenistic world. Aristotle's works were later transmitted through the Islamic world, where philosophers such as Al-Farabi, Avicenna, and Averroes wrote extensive commentaries; Averroes became known in the Latin West simply as "the Commentator". In medieval Christian Europe, Aristotelianism became the foundation of scholastic philosophy, reaching its synthesis in the work of Thomas Aquinas, who reconciled Aristotle's metaphysics with Christian theology.

Renaissance and decline

During the Renaissance, Aristotelianism remained dominant in the universities, though humanists such as Petrarch criticised its scholastic forms. The scientific revolution of the seventeenth century, led by figures such as Galileo and Descartes, rejected Aristotelian physics and cosmology, though Aristotelian logic and ethics continued to be influential.

Modern revivals

In the twentieth century, virtue ethicists such as Alasdair MacIntyre and Philippa Foot revived Aristotelian approaches to morality, and Aristotle's biological and metaphysical concepts have drawn renewed attention in philosophy of science.

Core doctrines

  • Four causes: material, formal, efficient, and final causes as modes of explanation.
  • Substance and accident: reality is made up of substances bearing properties.
  • Potentiality and actuality: change is the actualisation of what exists potentially.
  • Hylomorphism: natural things are compounds of matter (hyle) and form (morphe).
  • Virtue ethics: the good life consists in exercising the virtues in accordance with reason.
