French Forces of the Interior

The French Forces of the Interior (French: Forces françaises de l'Intérieur, FFI) were the formal name given by General Charles de Gaulle in 1944 to the combined French Resistance fighters operating against the German occupation of France during the Second World War.

Formation

The FFI was created in February–March 1944 by uniting the military wings of the main resistance movements — the Gaullist Armée secrète, the communist Francs-Tireurs et Partisans (FTP), the Organisation de résistance de l'armée (ORA), and others — under a single command. General Marie-Pierre Kœnig was appointed commander in June 1944. Estimates of FFI strength range from about 100,000 in June 1944 to some 400,000 by October, as volunteers flocked to the colours after the Normandy landings.

Operations

The FFI sabotaged railways and communications (notably under the Plan Vert), harassed German forces moving toward the Normandy front, gathered intelligence, and liberated many towns ahead of or alongside Allied forces. FFI units were prominent in the liberation of Paris in August 1944 and in the uprising that preceded the Allied arrival, and they tied down German garrisons in the Atlantic pockets of Brittany and the south-west.

Integration

After the liberation, de Gaulle's provisional government dissolved the FFI as a separate force; around 140,000 of its members were integrated ("amalgamated") into the regular French Army, forming the nucleus of units that fought in the final campaigns of 1944–45 and the occupation of Germany. FFI veterans were recognised as soldiers, though the contribution of the resistance long remained politically contested in postwar France.
