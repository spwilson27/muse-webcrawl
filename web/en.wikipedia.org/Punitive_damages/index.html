Punitive damages

Punitive damages, or exemplary damages, are damages assessed in a legal proceeding not to compensate the plaintiff for losses suffered but to punish the defendant for egregious misconduct and to deter similar conduct by others. They are awarded in addition to compensatory damages when the defendant's conduct is found to be especially harmful, malicious, fraudulent, oppressive, or reckless.

United States

Punitive damages are available in most U.S. states for tort claims and, in some jurisdictions, for certain contract and statutory claims. The U.S. Supreme Court has held that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment limits punitive awards that are "grossly excessive." In BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore (1996), the Court set out guideposts for review, including the ratio between punitive and compensatory damages, and in State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. v. Campbell (2003) it suggested that ratios exceeding single digits will rarely satisfy due process. Several states cap punitive damages by statute.

Well-known cases include Liebeck v. McDonald's Restaurants (1994), in which a jury awarded $2.7 million in punitive damages (later reduced) to a woman scalded by coffee, and the Exxon Valdez litigation, in which the Supreme Court reduced a punitive award to a 1:1 ratio with compensatory damages (Exxon Shipping Co. v. Baker, 2008).

Other jurisdictions

Exemplary damages are available in England and Wales only in limited categories following Rookes v Barnard (1964), such as oppressive conduct by government servants or conduct calculated to make a profit exceeding compensation. Canada permits punitive damages for "malicious, oppressive and high-handed" misconduct, as in Whiten v Pilot Insurance Co. (2002). Most civil-law countries, including Germany and Japan, do not award punitive damages in private litigation, regarding punishment as a function of criminal law, and some refuse to enforce foreign punitive judgments.
