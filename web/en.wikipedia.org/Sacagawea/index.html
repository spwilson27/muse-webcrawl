Sacagawea (c. 1788 – December 20, 1812) was a Lemhi Shoshone woman who, in her teens, was the only woman to accompany the Lewis and Clark Expedition as an interpreter and guide during their exploration of the Western United States. She traveled thousands of miles from North Dakota to the Pacific Ocean, establishing cultural contacts with Native American people and contributing to the expedition's knowledge of natural history in the regions it crossed.

Born into the Agaidika ('Salmon Eater') tribe near present-day Salmon, Idaho, Sacagawea was captured by Hidatsa raiders at about age twelve and later sold to the French-Canadian trapper Toussaint Charbonneau, who took her as one of his wives. When the Corps of Discovery wintered at Fort Mandan in 1804–05, Lewis and Clark hired Charbonneau as an interpreter, largely because Sacagawea spoke Shoshone.

Role in the expedition

Sacagawea gave birth to a son, Jean Baptiste, in February 1805, and carried him on the journey. Her presence signaled to tribes that the party was peaceful — a woman and infant did not accompany a war party. She recognized landmarks in her homeland, helped negotiate for horses with her brother, the Shoshone chief Cameahwait, identified edible plants, and once rescued important instruments and journals from a capsizing boat. Clark nicknamed her "Janey" and her son "Pompy."

Later life and legacy

After the expedition, Sacagawea lived at Fort Lisa in present-day South Dakota, where records indicate she died of a fever in 1812; her children were taken into Clark's care in St. Louis. An oral tradition among the Shoshone holds that she instead lived until 1884 on the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming. Sacagawea became an enduring symbol for the women's suffrage movement and is commemorated by numerous statues, place names, and the United States dollar coin issued in 2000 bearing her likeness.
