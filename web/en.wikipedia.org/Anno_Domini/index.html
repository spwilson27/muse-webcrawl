Anno Domini

The terms anno Domini (AD) and before Christ (BC) are used when designating years in the Gregorian and Julian calendars. Anno Domini is Latin for "in the year of the Lord". The era's epoch is the traditionally reckoned year of the birth of Jesus of Nazareth. The system has no year zero: the year 1 BC is immediately followed by AD 1.

History

The anno Domini system was devised in AD 525 by the monk Dionysius Exiguus, who used it to number the years in his Easter tables in place of the era of Diocletian. It spread through the works of the Venerable Bede, whose Ecclesiastical History of the English People (731) popularised both AD dating and the practice of counting years backwards "before Christ" for earlier events. Dionysius's calculation is now generally considered to be a few years off, since Herod the Great, in whose reign Jesus was born according to the Gospels, died in 4 BC.

Usage

AD is conventionally placed before the year number (AD 2024), while BC follows it (44 BC). The system became the international standard for civil and scholarly dating. In academic and interfaith contexts, the religiously neutral equivalents CE (Common Era) and BCE (Before the Common Era) are widely used and are numerically identical to AD and BC.

Related eras

Other epoch-based systems include Anno Mundi (years since the creation of the world), the Islamic Hijri era, and regnal-year dating used in many historical monarchies.
