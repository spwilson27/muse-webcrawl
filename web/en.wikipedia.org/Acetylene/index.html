Acetylene

Acetylene (systematic name: ethyne) is the chemical compound with the formula C2H2 and structure HC≡CH. It is a hydrocarbon and the simplest alkyne. This colourless gas is widely used as a fuel and a chemical building block. It is unstable in its pure form and thus is usually handled as a solution. Pure acetylene is odourless, but commercial grades usually have a marked odour due to impurities such as divinyl sulfide and phosphine.

As an alkyne, acetylene is unsaturated because its two carbon atoms are bonded together in a triple bond. The carbon–carbon triple bond places all four atoms in the same straight line, with CCH bond angles of 180°.

Production

Acetylene is traditionally produced by the reaction of calcium carbide with water, which generates acetylene and calcium hydroxide. Today it is also made by partial combustion of methane or recovered as a side product of ethylene production by steam cracking of hydrocarbons. In 2016 China accounted for most of world production, largely via the calcium carbide route.

Uses

Burned with oxygen in an oxy-acetylene torch, acetylene produces a flame of over 3,600 K (3,330 °C), releasing 11.8 kJ/g — it is the hottest-burning common fuel gas, and is used for welding and cutting metal. Because free compressed acetylene can decompose explosively, it is stored and shipped dissolved in acetone (or dimethylformamide) within a cylinder filled with a porous material such as agamassan.

As a chemical feedstock, acetylene was historically used to make acetaldehyde, acrylic acid derivatives, vinyl chloride and many other products; much of this chemistry was displaced by ethylene-based routes in the mid-20th century, though carbide-based acetylene chemistry remains important in China. Acetylene is also used in carburising, in atomic absorption spectroscopy calibration, and historically in carbide lamps for miners, cyclists and lighthouses.

Discovery

Acetylene was discovered in 1836 by Edmund Davy, who identified it as a "new carburet of hydrogen". It was rediscovered in 1860 by the French chemist Marcellin Berthelot, who coined the name acétylène and produced it by passing vapours of organic compounds through red-hot tubes.
