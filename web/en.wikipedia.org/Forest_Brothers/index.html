The Forest Brothers (Estonian: metsavennad, Latvian: mežabrāļi, Lithuanian: miško broliai) were Baltic partisans who waged guerrilla warfare against Soviet rule during and after the Second World War. They took refuge in the region's dense forests — hence the name — and fought first during the Soviet occupation of 1940–41, then against the German occupation, and most extensively after the Red Army reoccupied the Baltic states in 1944.

Background

The Soviet Union annexed Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania in 1940 under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact arrangements. Mass deportations in June 1941 drove many men into the forests even before the German invasion. When Soviet forces returned in 1944, tens of thousands refused conscription into the Red Army or expected imminent Western help, and took up arms.

The guerrilla war

At its height around 1945–1949, the resistance fielded organised units with ranks, uniforms and underground administrations, particularly in Lithuania, where the movement formed a unified command. Partisans ambushed Soviet officials, attacked military outposts and resisted collectivisation and deportation. The Soviet response combined NKVD/MGB counterinsurgency battalions, agent infiltration and the mass deportations of March 1949 (Operation Priboi), which broke the partisans' rural support base by removing some 90,000 people.

Decline and legacy

Amnesties, infiltration and exhaustion reduced the movement through the early 1950s; the last known Forest Brother, the Estonian August Sabbe, died evading KGB capture in 1978. After the restoration of independence in 1990–91, the partisans were officially honoured in all three Baltic states as national heroes, with memorials, state funerals and dedicated museum exhibitions.
