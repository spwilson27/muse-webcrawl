Minaret

FunctionCall to prayer (adhan); landmark
ElementsBase, shaft, balcony (şerefe), cap
TallestHassan II Mosque minaret, 210 m

A minaret (from Arabic manāra, "lighthouse" or "place of light") is a tower typically attached to a mosque, from which the muezzin traditionally proclaims the call to prayer (adhan) five times a day. Minarets also serve as visual landmarks announcing a mosque's presence, and have become one of the most recognizable features of Islamic architecture.

The earliest mosques had no minarets; the feature developed by the 8th–9th centuries under the Umayyads and Abbasids, partly influenced by Syrian church towers and ancient lighthouses. Styles vary greatly by region: the spiral Malwiya Minaret of Samarra, the square towers of the Maghreb, the single-balconied "pencil" minarets of Ottoman Turkey, and the paired slender minarets flanking Persian iwans.

Structure

A typical minaret consists of a foundation, a tapering shaft, one or more balconies (şerefe) from which the call is given, and a conical or domed cap, often topped by a finial with crescent. In the modern era loudspeakers usually carry the muezzin's voice, and some minarets — such as that of the Hassan II Mosque in Casablanca, the tallest at 210 metres — incorporate lights or laser beams pointing toward Mecca.
