Godfrey Hounsfield

Sir Godfrey Newbold Hounsfield CBE FRS (28 August 1919 – 12 August 2004) was an English electrical engineer who shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Allan Cormack for his part in developing the diagnostic technique of X-ray computed tomography (CT scanning). The Hounsfield unit, the standard quantitative scale of radiodensity used in CT imaging, is named after him.

Life and career

Hounsfield grew up on a farm in Nottinghamshire and served as a radar instructor in the Royal Air Force during the Second World War. He joined EMI in 1951, working on radar and guided weapons before leading the design of the EMIDEC 1100, one of Britain's first transistorised computers. In the late 1960s, drawing on pattern-recognition ideas, he conceived of reconstructing a three-dimensional image of the body from X-ray measurements taken from many angles.

The CT scanner

Working largely in secret, Hounsfield built a prototype head scanner; the first clinical CT scan of a patient — revealing a cerebral cyst — was performed at Atkinson Morley's Hospital, Wimbledon, on 1 October 1971. The EMI scanner transformed diagnostic medicine by making soft-tissue structures inside the skull visible for the first time. Hounsfield was knighted in 1981. He never married and continued working at EMI's Central Research Laboratories into his later years.
