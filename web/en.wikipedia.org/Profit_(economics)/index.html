Profit (economics)

In economics, profit is the difference between revenue gained from selling output and the full opportunity costs of producing it. Economists distinguish economic profit from accounting profit: accounting profit counts only explicit costs, whereas economic profit also deducts implicit costs, such as the return the firm's resources could earn elsewhere.

Normal and supernormal profit

Normal profit is the level of (accounting) profit at which economic profit is zero — just enough to keep resources in their current use. Anything above this is supernormal (or abnormal) profit. In perfectly competitive markets, free entry and exit drive economic profit to zero in the long run; firms with market power, such as monopolies, may sustain supernormal profit.

Functions and theories

Profit is theorised as a reward for entrepreneurship and risk-bearing. Frank Knight distinguished profit as the return to bearing uninsurable uncertainty; Joseph Schumpeter linked profit to innovation and "creative destruction"; Marxist economics treats profit as appropriated surplus value. Profits also act as a signal, directing resources toward their most valued uses.
