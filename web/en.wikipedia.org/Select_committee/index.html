Select committee

Select committees hold hearings in which ministers, officials and experts give evidence.

A select committee is a committee of a legislature, usually made up of a small number of members, appointed to consider a particular matter, to scrutinise the work of government, or to conduct an investigation. The term is used in several Westminster-system parliaments and in the United States Congress, though its precise meaning differs between them.

United Kingdom

In the UK Parliament, select committees of the House of Commons shadow each government department, examining its expenditure, administration and policy; there are also cross-cutting committees such as the Public Accounts Committee and the Liaison Committee. They take written and oral evidence, can compel the attendance of witnesses and production of papers, and publish reports to which the government must respond. Since 2010 their chairs have been elected by the whole House and their members elected within party groups. The House of Lords also appoints select committees on themes such as the constitution, science and technology, and European affairs. Distinct from select committees, public bill committees examine legislation line by line.

United States

In the United States Congress, a select committee is a committee created by a resolution for a specific purpose, often investigative, and usually temporary, as distinct from a standing committee. Examples include the House Select Committee on Assassinations and the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence — the latter being a permanent select committee — as well as the committee that investigated the January 6 attack on the Capitol.

Other countries

Select committees are also used in the parliaments of Canada, Australia, New Zealand, India and other Commonwealth legislatures, generally for scrutiny and inquiries outside the work of standing committees.
