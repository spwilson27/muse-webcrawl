Structural violence

Structural violence is a form of violence in which social structures and institutions harm people by preventing them from meeting their basic needs — through unequal distribution of resources, power and life chances — without any single actor directly inflicting physical injury. The term was introduced by peace researcher Johan Galtung in his 1969 paper "Violence, Peace, and Peace Research", where he contrasted it with direct (personal) violence and defined peace as the absence of both — "positive peace".

Concept

Galtung's insight was that when the actual realisation of human potential falls below what is possible, and the shortfall is avoidable and attributable to social arrangements, violence is present even if no fist is raised: for example, when people die of preventable disease because healthcare is organised or funded unequally. The anthropologist and physician Paul Farmer later applied the concept to global health, documenting how poverty, racism and political economy structure the risk of diseases such as tuberculosis and HIV/AIDS in Haiti and elsewhere, and arguing that health care is a form of pragmatic solidarity against structural violence.

Criticism

Critics argue the concept stretches "violence" so broadly that it loses analytical precision, conflating injustice, inequality and harm, and that it can smuggle contested political conclusions into a definition. Defenders reply that the term names precisely the mechanism — harm built into ordinary arrangements — that other vocabularies miss, and connects to related ideas such as institutional racism, social determinants of health, and "slow violence".
