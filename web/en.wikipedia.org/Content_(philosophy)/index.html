Content (philosophy)

Mental content is what a thought or belief is about.

In philosophy, content is what a mental state, a linguistic expression, or a representation is about. A belief has content: to believe that snow is white is to stand in the belief relation to the content that snow is white, often identified with a proposition. The notion underlies discussions of intentionality, the "directedness" of mind toward objects and states of affairs, which Franz Brentano revived as the mark of the mental.

Types of content

Philosophers distinguish wide (externalist) content, which depends partly on the subject's environment, from narrow content, which depends only on what is inside the subject's head. Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment, in which two molecule-for-molecule duplicates think about water and a superficially identical substance XYZ respectively, is the classic argument that some content is wide. Tyler Burge extended the argument to socially determined content. Related notions include conceptual and non-conceptual content: whether creatures without language, such as infants and animals, can have states with content that they cannot articulate in concepts remains contested.

Content in language

In the philosophy of language, content is closely related to meaning and reference. Debates over how content is determined, representationalism versus inferentialism, teleosemantics, and two-dimensional semantics all concern how representations come to have the contents they do. The problem of mental content is central to the philosophy of mind, because explaining how physical states of the brain can represent anything at all remains one of its deepest puzzles.
