Satisficing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Satisficing is a decision-making strategy or cognitive heuristic that entails searching through the available alternatives until an acceptability threshold is met. The term is a portmanteau of satisfy and suffice, and was introduced by the American social scientist Herbert A. Simon in 1956, in the context of his work on bounded rationality.

Concept

Simon argued that human decision-makers rarely possess the information, time, and computational capacity required to find the optimal solution assumed by classical economics. Instead, they set an aspiration level—a criterion of "good enough"—and select the first alternative that exceeds it. If no alternative meets the aspiration level, the level may be revised downward; if alternatives are found very easily, it may be raised.

Satisficing appears in many fields. In economics it informs models of firms and consumers under bounded rationality. In psychology it contrasts with maximizing: research by Barry Schwartz and colleagues suggests habitual maximizers experience more regret and lower satisfaction with their choices. In artificial intelligence and operations research, satisficing algorithms return solutions that meet specified constraints rather than guaranteeing optimality, saving computation.

Criticism

Critics note that satisficing can be difficult to distinguish from optimization once search costs are included in the model: an agent who stops searching when the expected cost of continuing exceeds the expected benefit is, formally, optimizing. Defenders reply that the concept remains valuable descriptively, since real aspiration levels are shaped by framing, experience, and social norms rather than by global cost-benefit calculation.
