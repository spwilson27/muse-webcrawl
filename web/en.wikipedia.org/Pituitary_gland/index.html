Pituitary gland

Pituitary gland
LocationSella turcica of the sphenoid bone, at the base of the brain
SizeAbout the size of a pea (roughly 1 cm; ~0.5 g)
PartsAnterior lobe (adenohypophysis); posterior lobe (neurohypophysis); intermediate lobe
SystemEndocrine system

The pituitary gland (or hypophysis) is a small endocrine gland protruding from the bottom of the hypothalamus at the base of the brain. Sitting in a bony hollow called the sella turcica, it is often called the "master gland" because its hormones regulate growth, metabolism, stress responses, reproduction and other glands of the endocrine system.

Structure and hormones

The anterior lobe secretes growth hormone (GH), prolactin, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), under the control of releasing and inhibiting hormones from the hypothalamus delivered through a portal blood system. The posterior lobe stores and releases oxytocin and vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone), which are made in the hypothalamus and transported down nerve fibres.

Clinical significance

Disorders include pituitary adenomas (tumours), which may overproduce hormones — causing acromegaly (excess GH), Cushing's disease (excess ACTH) or hyperprolactinaemia — or underproduction (hypopituitarism). Large tumours can press on the optic chiasm just above the gland, causing characteristic visual-field loss. Diagnosis relies on hormone assays and MRI; many adenomas are treated with medication or transsphenoidal surgery.
