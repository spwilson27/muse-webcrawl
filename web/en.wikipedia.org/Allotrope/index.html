Allotrope

Allotropy is the property of some chemical elements to exist in two or more different structural forms, known as allotropes, in the same physical state. In each allotrope the atoms of the element are bonded together in a different manner, giving forms that can differ strikingly in physical and chemical properties.

Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon, arranged differently.

Examples

The best-known example is carbon, whose allotropes include diamond (a rigid three-dimensional network, extremely hard and transparent), graphite (stacked hexagonal sheets, soft and electrically conductive), graphene, fullerenes such as buckminsterfullerene (C60), and carbon nanotubes. Oxygen occurs as dioxygen (O2) and ozone (O3). Phosphorus has white, red, and black allotropes with very different reactivity; sulphur forms numerous ring and chain structures; and tin undergoes "tin pest," a transformation from white (β) to grey (α) tin at low temperatures.

Terminology

The term was introduced by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1841. Allotropy applies only to elements; analogous variation in compounds is called polymorphism. Transitions between allotropes may be enantiotropic (reversible at a definite temperature) or monotropic (one form metastable at all temperatures).
