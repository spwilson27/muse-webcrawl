Old Comedy

Old Comedy (Latin: comoedia prisca) is the first phase of ancient Greek comedy, flourishing in Athens during the fifth century BC. It is defined above all by the eleven surviving plays of Aristophanes, the only Old Comic author whose works survive complete. Old Comedy is famed for its fantastic plots, topical satire, obscenity, and direct attacks on named politicians, philosophers, and rival poets.

Structure and features

A typical Old Comedy had a formal structure: a prologue establishing a comic scheme, the entry (parodos) of a chorus of 24 in elaborate costume, an agon or contest of argument, the parabasis in which the chorus addressed the audience directly on the poet's behalf, a series of farcical episodes, and a festive exodos. Performances took place at the City Dionysia and Lenaia festivals, where poets competed for prizes.

Aristophanes and decline

Aristophanes' plays — The Acharnians, The Knights, The Clouds (mocking Socrates), Lysistrata, The Frogs — openly savaged the Peloponnesian War and Athenian public figures. By the early fourth century BC, political and economic change produced the transitional Middle Comedy and eventually the domestic New Comedy of Menander, which abandoned personal satire and the chorus's central role.
