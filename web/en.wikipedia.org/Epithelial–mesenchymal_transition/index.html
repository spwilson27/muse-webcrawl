The epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a biological process in which polarised epithelial cells, which normally adhere tightly to one another and to a basement membrane, lose their cell–cell adhesion and apical–basal polarity and acquire the migratory, invasive properties of mesenchymal cells. EMT is essential for embryonic development, contributes to wound healing and tissue fibrosis, and is implicated in cancer invasion and metastasis.

Mechanism

During EMT, epithelial markers such as E-cadherin, claudins, and cytokeratins are repressed, while mesenchymal markers such as N-cadherin, vimentin, and fibronectin are induced. This "cadherin switch" is driven by transcription factors including Snail (SNAI1), Slug (SNAI2), Twist, and ZEB1/2, which are themselves activated by signalling pathways such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), Wnt, Notch, and Hedgehog, and by environmental cues such as hypoxia. Cells undergoing EMT dissolve their adherens junctions, remodel their cytoskeleton, secrete matrix-degrading enzymes, and become capable of moving through extracellular matrix. The reverse process, mesenchymal–epithelial transition (MET), restores the epithelial phenotype. Many cells occupy partial or hybrid states along the EMT spectrum rather than completing a binary switch.

Types and biological roles

EMT is conventionally divided into three types. Type 1 EMT occurs in development: it enables gastrulation and the formation of the mesoderm, delamination of neural crest cells, and fusion of the palatal shelves. Type 2 EMT occurs in wound healing and organ fibrosis, where fibroblasts generated through EMT deposit excess matrix. Type 3 EMT occurs in cancer, where carcinoma cells at the invasive front of a tumour acquire the ability to enter blood vessels and seed metastases; EMT programmes are also associated with resistance to chemotherapy and with cancer stem-cell properties.

History

The process was recognised as the "epithelial–mesenchymal transformation" by developmental biologists, notably through Elizabeth Hay's work on chick embryos in the 1960s–1980s; the term "transition" was later preferred because the change is reversible. Research linking EMT to cancer progression expanded rapidly from the early 2000s.
