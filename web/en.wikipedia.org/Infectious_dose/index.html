Infectious dose

FieldMicrobiology, epidemiology, biosafety
Key measureID50 — dose infecting 50% of hosts
Related conceptsMinimal infective dose, virulence, LD50

The infectious dose is the amount of a pathogen — typically expressed as a number of organisms or infectious particles — required to establish an infection in a host. Because infection is a probabilistic process, it is commonly quantified by the ID50, the dose that infects 50 percent of a population of hosts under specified conditions.

Variation among pathogens

Infectious doses vary enormously. Some agents, such as norovirus and Shigella, can infect with as few as tens of particles or cells, which helps explain their rapid spread. Others, including Vibrio cholerae in water, may require millions of organisms unless the dose is buffered by food or the host has reduced stomach acidity. The route of exposure matters greatly: inhalation, ingestion, and injection can differ by orders of magnitude for the same pathogen.

Host factors

The effective infectious dose also depends on the host. Immune status, age, prior exposure or vaccination, genetic factors, and the condition of barrier defences such as skin, mucus, and gastric acid all shift the dose–response curve. Immunocompromised individuals may be infected by doses harmless to healthy people, including by opportunistic organisms.

Use in risk assessment

Quantitative microbial risk assessment uses dose–response models — such as the exponential and beta-Poisson models — fitted from human feeding studies, animal experiments, and outbreak data to estimate infection probability from measured environmental concentrations. Infectious dose values also inform biosafety classifications and food and water safety standards.
