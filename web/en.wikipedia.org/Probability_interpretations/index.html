Probability interpretations

The mathematical theory of probability is neutral about what probability means: the axioms developed by Andrey Kolmogorov specify how probabilities behave, not what they are. Probability interpretations are the philosophical attempts to answer that question — to say what it is for an event to have a probability, and how probability statements connect to the world. Disagreement among interpreters explains much of the divergence between statistical schools, such as frequentist and Bayesian statistics.

Main interpretations

  • Classical interpretation: probability is determined by symmetry and the principle of indifference — with a fair die, each of six equally possible faces has probability 1/6. Associated with Laplace.
  • Frequentism: the probability of an event is its long-run relative frequency in an indefinitely repeated trial or a reference class. Developed by Venn, Reichenbach, and von Mises.
  • Propensity interpretation: probabilities are objective dispositions or tendencies of physical systems — a die's probability of landing six is a property of the die-and-throwing setup. Proposed notably by Karl Popper, originally to interpret quantum mechanics.
  • Subjective (Bayesian) interpretation: probability expresses a rational agent's degree of belief, constrained by coherence (avoiding Dutch books) and updated by Bayes' theorem as evidence arrives. Associated with Ramsey, de Finetti, and Savage.
  • Logical interpretation: probability is an objective relation of partial entailment between evidence and hypothesis, developed by Keynes and Carnap.

Significance

The interpretations matter practically wherever probabilities are used to guide decisions or claims to knowledge: in the foundations of statistics, in the interpretation of quantum mechanics (where some interpretations of the theory treat probability as fundamental), and in debates about risk and evidence in medicine, law, and public policy. Most working applications remain compatible with several interpretations at once.
