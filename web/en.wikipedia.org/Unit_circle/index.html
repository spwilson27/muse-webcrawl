Unit circle

In mathematics, a unit circle is a circle of unit radius — that is, a radius of 1. Frequently, especially in trigonometry, the unit circle is the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin (0, 0) in the Cartesian coordinate system in the Euclidean plane. It is often denoted S1. If (x, y) is a point on the unit circle, then |x| and |y| are the lengths of the legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse has length 1, so by the Pythagorean theorem:

x² + y² = 1

Trigonometric functions

The unit circle provides the standard geometric definition of the trigonometric functions for all real arguments. For an angle t measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis, the point where the angle's terminal side intersects the circle has coordinates (cos t, sin t). This definition extends sine and cosine beyond acute angles and makes their periodicity transparent: both functions repeat every 2π. The tangent is the ratio sin/cos, and the circle construction yields the fundamental identity cos²t + sin²t = 1 directly.

In complex analysis

In the complex plane, the unit circle is the set of all complex numbers of modulus 1. Under multiplication it forms a group — the circle group — and by Euler's formula every point on it can be written as eit = cos t + i sin t. This ties the circle to harmonic analysis, roots of unity, and the geometry of rotations.

Generalizations

The unit circle generalizes to the unit sphere and unit balls in higher dimensions, and to unit "circles" under other norms — for example, a square under the maximum norm. In topology, S1 is the simplest closed manifold with fundamental group ℤ, making it a foundational example in algebraic topology.
