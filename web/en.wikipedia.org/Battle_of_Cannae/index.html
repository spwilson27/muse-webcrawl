Battle of Cannae

The Battle of Cannae was a decisive engagement of the Second Punic War fought on 2 August 216 BC near the ancient village of Cannae in Apulia, southeast Italy. The Carthaginian army under Hannibal Barca destroyed a larger Roman force led by the consuls Lucius Aemilius Paullus and Gaius Terentius Varro through a double envelopment that has been studied by military theorists ever since. It is regarded as one of the greatest tactical feats in military history and one of the worst defeats in Roman history.

Background

Having crossed the Alps in 218 BC, Hannibal had already destroyed Roman armies at the Trebia and Lake Trasimene. Rome responded with the Fabian strategy of avoidance under Fabius Maximus, but political pressure for battle led to the election of Varro and the assembly of the largest army Rome had yet fielded. The consuls advanced on Hannibal's position near the Aufidus river at Cannae.

Battle

Hannibal arranged his centre — Gauls and Iberians — in a convex crescent projecting toward the Romans, with his veteran African infantry angled back on both flanks and his superior cavalry on the wings. As the massive Roman infantry mass drove the centre backward, the crescent inverted; the African troops turned inward against the Roman flanks while the victorious Carthaginian cavalry closed the rear. Encircled and compressed so tightly that many could not raise their weapons, the Roman army was annihilated. Polybius reports some 70,000 Roman dead; Livy gives around 48,000.

Aftermath

Rome refused to negotiate despite the catastrophe, raised new legions, and returned to Fabian tactics; Hannibal, lacking siege equipment and reinforcements, never took Rome itself. Cannae became the canonical example of a battle of annihilation by envelopment, studied from Schlieffen to modern staff colleges.
