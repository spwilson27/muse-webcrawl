Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) are the stem cells that give rise to other blood cells. This process is called haematopoiesis (or hematopoiesis). In vertebrates, the first definitive HSCs arise from the haemogenic endothelium of the embryonic aorta-gonad-mesonephros region, then expand in the fetal liver before colonising the bone marrow, the primary site of haematopoiesis throughout adult life.

HSCs are multipotent and capable of self-renewal: a small number can replenish the entire blood system of an individual. Through asymmetric division and differentiation, they generate myeloid and lymphoid progenitors that produce erythrocytes (red blood cells), platelets, granulocytes, monocytes, and lymphocytes.

Structure and location

In adults, HSCs are found mainly in the bone marrow of the pelvis, femur, sternum, and cranium. They reside in specialised microenvironments called stem cell niches, where stromal cells, osteoblasts, and endothelial cells regulate their quiescence, proliferation, and differentiation. HSCs can also be mobilised into peripheral blood, a property exploited clinically with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), and are present in umbilical cord blood.

Function

A healthy adult produces roughly 1011–1012 new blood cells daily to maintain steady-state levels in circulation. HSCs sit at the apex of this production hierarchy. Long-term HSCs self-renew for the life of the organism, while short-term HSCs and multipotent progenitors provide waves of differentiation with more limited renewal capacity.

Clinical significance

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is used to treat many cancers and immune system disorders, including leukaemias, lymphomas, multiple myeloma, and primary immunodeficiencies. Grafts may be autologous (from the patient) or allogeneic (from a donor). Donor matching is based on human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing. HSCs are also studied as vehicles for gene therapy of inherited blood disorders such as sickle cell disease and beta-thalassemia.

History

The existence of a common blood-forming stem cell was proposed in the early 20th century by Alexander Maximow. Experimental proof came in 1961, when James Till and Ernest McCulloch demonstrated colony-forming cells in the spleens of irradiated mice. Their work founded modern stem cell biology and led to the identification of HSC markers and purification strategies.
