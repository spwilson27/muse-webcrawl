Baltimore classification
Proposed byDavid Baltimore
Year1971
BasisViral genome type and route to mRNA synthesis
Number of groupsSeven (I–VII)

The Baltimore classification is a system used to classify viruses based on their manner of messenger RNA (mRNA) synthesis. By organising viruses according to how they produce mRNA, it is possible to group together viruses that behave similarly — an approach that complements the ICTV taxonomy based on evolutionary relationships. The system was developed by Nobel laureate virologist David Baltimore and first published in 1971.

Rationale

Every virus must generate mRNA that the host cell's ribosomes can translate into protein. Viral genomes, however, come in many forms: double- or single-stranded, DNA or RNA, positive-sense or negative-sense, and some are copied through reverse transcription. Baltimore placed the production of mRNA at the centre of the scheme, yielding seven groups defined by the pathway from genome to mRNA.

Use and limitations

The Baltimore scheme remains a standard teaching framework in virology because it predicts, from genome structure alone, the enzymes a virus must carry or encode (such as an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase in groups III and V, or reverse transcriptase in groups VI and VII). It does not reflect phylogeny: unrelated viruses can share a group, and it is used alongside, not instead of, formal virus taxonomy.
