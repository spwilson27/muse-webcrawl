The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) is an independent agency of the United States federal government responsible for the civilian space programme, aeronautics, and space research. Established in 1958 in response to the Soviet launch of Sputnik, NASA has led most American space exploration, from the Apollo Moon landings to the Hubble Space Telescope and the Mars rovers.

History

NASA absorbed the earlier National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) and began operations on 1 October 1958. The Mercury and Gemini programmes built the skills for Apollo, which landed Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin on the Moon on 20 July 1969; six crewed landings followed. After Apollo, NASA flew Skylab, the reusable Space Shuttle (1981–2011), and with international partners built the International Space Station, continuously occupied since 2000. The agency's uncrewed programme sent probes to every planet — including the Voyager spacecraft now in interstellar space — and operates observatories such as Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope. Two shuttle disasters, Challenger (1986) and Columbia (2003), each killed seven astronauts and reshaped the agency's culture.

Programmes today

Current priorities include the Artemis programme to return astronauts to the Moon, robotic exploration of Mars (Perseverance and its sample caching), Earth science and climate monitoring, and aeronautics research. Since 2020, SpaceX Crew Dragon vehicles under NASA's Commercial Crew programme have carried astronauts to the ISS. NASA works through ten field centres and employs roughly 18,000 civil servants, with a budget of around $25 billion per year.
