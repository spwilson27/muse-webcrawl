Graph coloring

In graph theory, graph coloring is a special case of graph labelling: an assignment of labels, traditionally called "colors", to elements of a graph subject to certain constraints. In its simplest form, vertex coloring assigns colors to the vertices of a graph so that no two adjacent vertices share the same color. The smallest number of colors needed to color a graph G is called its chromatic number, denoted χ(G).

History

Graph coloring originated with the problem of coloring maps so that neighbouring regions receive different colors. Francis Guthrie conjectured in 1852 that four colors suffice for any map drawn in the plane; the resulting four color theorem resisted proof until Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken produced a computer-assisted proof in 1976. The theory developed through the work of Birkhoff, Whitney and others into a central branch of combinatorics.

Key results

A graph is bipartite if and only if it is 2-colorable. By Brooks' theorem, a connected graph that is neither a complete graph nor an odd cycle can be colored with at most Δ colors, where Δ is its maximum degree. Perfect graphs satisfy χ = ω (the size of the largest clique) for every induced subgraph. Deciding whether a general graph is 3-colorable is NP-complete, one of Karp's original 21 NP-complete problems.

Variants and applications

Variants include edge coloring (governed by Vizing's theorem), list coloring, total coloring and greedy coloring algorithms. Applications of graph coloring include register allocation in compilers, scheduling and timetabling, frequency assignment in mobile networks, and solving Sudoku puzzles, which can be formulated as a graph coloring problem with 81 vertices.
