Underground film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Underground film is a film genre characterised by its opposition to the mainstream commercial film industry, made by independent filmmakers working with minimal budgets, often outside censorship rules and conventional narrative and aesthetic norms. The term came into widespread use in the United States in the late 1950s and 1960s, particularly in association with New York-based filmmakers, critics, and screening cooperatives such as the Film-Makers' Cooperative and the journal Film Culture.

History

Critic Manny Farber used the term "underground" in 1957 to describe anti-artistic genre filmmaking, but it soon came to denote the avant-garde itself. Jonas Mekas and the New American Cinema movement championed personal, poetic filmmaking, and works such as Kenneth Anger's Scorpio Rising (1963), Jack Smith's Flaming Creatures (1963), and Andy Warhol's Chelsea Girls (1966) became emblematic of the underground — frequently confronting both aesthetic conventions and obscenity laws. In Britain, the London Film-Makers' Co-op fostered structural film in the 1970s.

Characteristics

Underground films typically feature some combination of: amateur or deliberately rough technique; shooting on small-gauge film (8mm, Super 8, 16mm); direct manipulation of the film stock (scratching, painting, found footage); improvisation and non-professional performers; taboo or transgressive subject matter; and distribution through artist-run cooperatives, lofts, coffee houses, and midnight screenings rather than commercial theatres.

Later developments

The underground fed into later movements: the New York "No Wave" and Cinema of Transgression scenes of the late 1970s and 1980s, the punk film movement, and independent and experimental cinema generally. The rise of video, then digital media and the internet, progressively dissolved the technological and distribution barriers that had defined the underground, though festivals and microcinemas continue the tradition.
