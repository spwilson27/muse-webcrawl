Endometrium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The endometrium is the inner epithelial layer, along with its mucous membrane, of the mammalian uterus. It has a basal layer and a functional layer: the basal layer contains stem cells which regenerate the functional layer. The functional layer thickens and then is shed during menstruation in humans and some other mammals, including apes, Old World monkeys, some species of bat, the elephant shrew and the Cairo spiny mouse.

In most other mammals, the endometrium is reabsorbed in the estrous cycle. During pregnancy, the glands and blood vessels in the endometrium further increase in size and complexity, and the endometrium becomes the decidua, part of the placenta supplying nutrients to the embryo.

Structure and cycle

The endometrium consists of a single layer of columnar epithelium plus supporting stroma rich in glands and spiral arteries. Under oestrogen in the follicular (proliferative) phase it thickens; after ovulation, progesterone drives secretory changes preparing it for implantation of a blastocyst. If implantation does not occur, falling hormone levels trigger breakdown and shedding — menstruation — and the cycle of roughly 28 days begins again, repeating some 450 times over a reproductive lifetime.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the endometrium include endometriosis (endometrial-like tissue outside the uterus), endometrial hyperplasia, endometritis (inflammation, often infectious), Asherman's syndrome (intrauterine adhesions), and endometrial cancer, the most common gynaecological malignancy in developed countries. Ultrasound measurement of endometrial thickness is used in assessing postmenopausal bleeding and fertility treatment.
