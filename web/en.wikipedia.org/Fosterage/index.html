Fosterage

Fosterage created lifelong bonds of kinship in early Irish and other societies.

Fosterage is the practice of raising a child in the household of a family other than the child's parents, historically an important social institution in many cultures. Unlike modern foster care, which is usually a response to family crisis, historical fosterage was a normal, prestigious arrangement used to cement alliances, educate children, and weave networks of loyalty between families and clans.

Ireland and the Celtic world

Fosterage is best documented in early medieval Ireland, where it was regulated in detail by the Brehon laws. Children could be fostered for affection or for a fee scaled to their social rank. The foster child (Irish: dalta) owed obligations to the foster parents, including supporting them in old age, while the foster father (aite) and foster mother (muime) were responsible for the child's upbringing and education—boys in arms, farming, or crafts, girls in household skills. Foster siblings (comhaltaí) were regarded as close kin, and the bonds of fosterage were often said to be stronger than those of blood. The institution persisted in Gaelic Ireland and Highland Scotland into the 17th century.

Elsewhere

Similar institutions existed across the Norse world, where fostering a child of lower rank could honour the foster family (the Icelandic sagas are full of consequential foster relationships), and among Anglo-Saxon and continental Germanic peoples. In imperial China and Japan, children were sometimes raised in allied or superior households as both honour and hostage. West African and Pacific societies also practised extensive child fostering for education, labour, and alliance.

Functions

Anthropologists note that fosterage served to bind groups together in societies without strong central states, to distribute children among households of differing resources, and to train elites. Its decline in Europe accompanied the growth of boarding schools, apprenticeships, and the ideal of the nuclear family.
