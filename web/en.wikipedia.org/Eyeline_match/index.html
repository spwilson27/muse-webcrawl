Eyeline match

An eyeline match is a film editing technique associated with continuity editing. It is based on the premise that a viewer will want to see what a character on screen is looking at. An eyeline match begins with a shot of a character looking at something off-screen, followed by a cut to the object or person being observed. The second shot is usually understood as the point of view of the character in the first shot.

Principles

For the match to read naturally, the direction of the character's gaze and their position in the frame must be consistent between shots: a character looking to frame right in the first shot should be answered by an object framed so that it appears to be located in that direction. Together with the 180-degree rule and shot/reverse-shot patterns, eyeline matches help maintain a coherent spatial geography for the viewer.

Point-of-view editing

The eyeline match is closely related to the point-of-view (POV) shot. In the classic pattern described by film theorists such as David Bordwell, a "point/glance" shot of the character is followed by a "point/object" shot of what they see, and often a reaction shot completes the sequence. Alfred Hitchcock made extensive use of the pattern to build suspense, as in Rear Window (1954), where the audience sees events largely through the protagonist's gaze.
