The history of Damascus is among the longest of any city on earth: the Syrian capital is often described as the world's oldest continuously inhabited city, an oasis fed by the Barada River at the edge of the Syrian Desert, on the trade routes between Mesopotamia, Arabia and the Mediterranean.

Ancient and classical city

Damascus appears in Egyptian records of the 15th century BCE and was the centre of the Aramaean kingdom of Aram-Damascus, repeatedly at war with Israel in biblical accounts. Conquered by Assyria, Babylon and Persia in turn, it became a Hellenistic city after Alexander, then a prosperous Roman one — the Via Recta, "Straight Street", of Acts. The great temple of Jupiter Damascenus later became the cathedral of St John and then the Umayyad Mosque, layering three faiths on one site.

Islamic golden ages

Muslim forces took Damascus in 635; under the Umayyads (661–750) it was the capital of an empire stretching from Spain to Central Asia. Muawiyah and his successors raised the Umayyad Mosque (706–715), a masterpiece of early Islamic art. After the Abbasids moved the capital to Baghdad, Damascus was a provincial prize contested by Fatimids, Seljuks and Crusaders; Nur al-Din and Saladin made it a centre of the counter-Crusade, and Mamluk Damascus prospered on the pilgrimage and spice routes.

Ottoman to modern

Selim I took the city for the Ottomans in 1516; four centuries of Ottoman rule followed, punctuated by the 1860 massacre of Christians. Faisal's Arab government (1918–20) was ended by French mandate forces at the Battle of Maysalun. Damascus became capital of independent Syria in 1946, seat of the Ba'athist state after 1963, and a contested prize in the civil war that began in 2011; in December 2024 the Assad regime fell and a transitional government took power.
