Pratītyasamutpāda (Sanskrit: प्रतीत्यसमुत्पाद), commonly translated as dependent origination or dependent arising, is a central doctrine of Buddhism. It holds that all phenomena arise in dependence upon other phenomena: "if this exists, that exists; if this ceases to exist, that also ceases to exist." Nothing exists independently or by its own inherent nature.

Overview

The doctrine explains the arising of suffering (duḥkha) and the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra) without recourse to a creator god or an eternal self. In early Buddhist texts the Buddha is said to have realized dependent origination on the night of his awakening. The general principle is expressed in the formula: when this is, that is; from the arising of this comes the arising of that; when this is not, that is not; from the cessation of this comes the cessation of that.

The twelve nidānas

The most famous formulation is a chain of twelve links (nidānas): (1) ignorance (avidyā), (2) volitional formations (saṃskāra), (3) consciousness (vijñāna), (4) name-and-form (nāmarūpa), (5) the six sense bases (ṣaḍāyatana), (6) contact (sparśa), (7) feeling (vedanā), (8) craving (tṛṣṇā), (9) clinging (upādāna), (10) becoming (bhava), (11) birth (jāti), and (12) aging-and-death (jarāmaraṇa). Reversing the chain — ending ignorance — brings the cessation of suffering.

Interpretations

Theravāda commentators traditionally distribute the twelve links across three lifetimes, while many modern and Mahāyāna teachers interpret them as processes operating moment to moment in the mind. Tibetan traditions develop elaborate analyses, and Zen teachers such as Thích Nhất Hạnh popularized the rendering "interbeing".

Relation to emptiness

In Mahāyāna philosophy, especially in Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, pratītyasamutpāda is equated with emptiness (śūnyatā): because things arise dependently, they are empty of intrinsic, independent existence (svabhāva). Nāgārjuna's verse identifies dependent origination, emptiness, the middle way, and dependent designation as the same teaching.
