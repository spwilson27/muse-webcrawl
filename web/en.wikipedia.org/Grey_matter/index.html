Grey matter

SystemCentral nervous system
ComponentsNeuronal cell bodies, dendrites, unmyelinated axons, glial cells, capillaries
LocationCerebral cortex, cerebellar cortex, deep nuclei, spinal cord core
Contrasted withWhite matter

Grey matter (or gray matter) is a major component of the central nervous system, consisting of neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, unmyelinated axons, glial cells, synapses and capillaries. It is distinguished from white matter, which consists mainly of myelinated axon tracts; the colour difference arises because myelin is pale and whitish, while living grey matter has a grey-brown hue from cell bodies and blood vessels (in preserved specimens it appears more distinctly grey).

Distribution

In the brain, grey matter forms the outer cerebral cortex and cerebellar cortex, and deep collections of nuclei such as the thalamus, basal ganglia and brainstem nuclei. In the spinal cord the arrangement is reversed: grey matter forms a butterfly-shaped core surrounded by white matter. Grey matter contains the somata of neurons and is the site of most synaptic processing — perception, voluntary movement, learning, memory and speech are all associated with cortical grey matter.

Development and change

Grey matter volume grows through childhood, peaks in adolescence and declines slowly with ageing, as synaptic pruning and neuronal changes reshape the cortex. Studies using MRI have linked changes in grey matter density to development, training and experience, and to psychiatric and neurodegenerative disease, although such findings require cautious interpretation.

Clinical significance

Loss or damage of grey matter occurs in stroke, trauma, Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, and abnormal grey matter development or connectivity has been studied in schizophrenia and autism. The anterior horn cells of the spinal cord, destroyed in poliomyelitis and motor neurone disease, are grey matter neurons.
