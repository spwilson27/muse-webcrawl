Plant hunting

Plant hunting is the activity of searching for, collecting, and transporting new plant species from their native habitats to botanical gardens, nurseries, and private collections elsewhere. From the 18th to the early 20th century, plant hunters — sponsored by institutions such as the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and by commercial nurseries and wealthy patrons — undertook often-dangerous expeditions that transformed Western gardens and agriculture by introducing thousands of ornamental and economic plants.

History

Plant collecting is ancient: Queen Hatshepsut of Egypt sent an expedition to Punt for incense trees around 1500 BC, and Chinese collectors sought ornamentals for millennia. The modern era of plant hunting began with the global voyages of the 18th century — Joseph Banks collected on Captain Cook's Endeavour voyage — and was enabled by the Wardian case, a sealed glass container invented around 1829 that allowed living plants to survive long sea journeys.

During the Victorian era, collectors introduced many of the staples of temperate gardens: David Douglas brought conifers and other plants from western North America (the Douglas fir bears his name); Robert Fortune smuggled tea plants and the knowledge of their manufacture out of China for the East India Company, and introduced many Chinese garden plants; and Ernest Henry Wilson collected thousands of specimens in China, including the dove tree and the kiwifruit. Frank Kingdon-Ward, George Forrest, and Joseph Rock continued the work in western China and the Himalayas into the 20th century, bringing back rhododendrons, primulas, and the fabled blue poppy (Meconopsis).

Impact and legacy

Plant hunting enriched world agriculture (tea in India, rubber — via seeds collected in Brazil and grown at Kew — in Malaya) and horticulture, but was entangled with colonialism: collectors often relied on unacknowledged local knowledge and removed genetic resources without consent or benefit-sharing. Modern collecting operates under the Convention on Biological Diversity and the Nagoya Protocol, and contemporary expeditions focus on conservation and documenting species threatened by habitat loss and climate change.
