Overview

Charrería is a traditional equestrian practice in Mexico involving working with cattle and horses, and is Mexico's national sport. It evolved from the ranching and horsemanship traditions of colonial New Spain, where Indigenous and mestizo vaqueros developed roping and riding techniques on the haciendas of central Mexico, particularly in the present-day states of Jalisco and Hidalgo.

The charreada

The competitive event, the charreada, takes place in an arena called a lienzo charro. Teams of charros perform a series of nine events, or suertes, including the cala de caballo (a test of rein and obedience), piales (roping a mare by the hind legs), colas (bull-tailing), bull riding, and the dramatic paso de la muerte, in which a rider leaps from his own horse onto a bareback wild horse at a gallop. Women's teams, called escaramuzas, perform precision riding routines in traditional Adelita dress.

Attire and organisation

The charro's ornate outfit — fitted, embroidered suit, wide-brimmed sombrero, boots and spurs — has become an emblem of Mexican identity, closely associated with mariachi music. The sport is governed by the Federación Mexicana de Charrería, founded in 1933. In 2016 UNESCO inscribed charrería on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
