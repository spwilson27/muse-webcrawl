Dark money

Dark money refers to political spending by nonprofit organizations — in particular 501(c)(4) "social welfare" groups, 501(c)(5) labor unions, and 501(c)(6) trade associations — that are not required to disclose the identities of their donors. Such organizations may receive unlimited donations from corporations, individuals, and other groups, and may spend funds to influence elections, provided that political activity is not their primary purpose.

The term is generally used in the context of United States campaign finance. Because the sources of the funding are not publicly disclosed, voters cannot determine who is behind political advertisements run by these groups.

History

The modern dark money era is often traced to the Federal Election Commission's 2007 advisory opinion allowing certain nonprofits to run issue advertisements without disclosing donors, and to the Supreme Court's 2010 decision in Citizens United v. FEC, which struck down restrictions on independent political expenditures by corporations and unions. Spending by groups that do not disclose donors rose sharply in the 2010 and 2012 election cycles, reaching hundreds of millions of dollars.

Regulation and debate

Proposed legislation such as the DISCLOSE Act would require greater transparency of political spending, but such measures have repeatedly failed to pass Congress. Supporters of disclosure argue it is essential to an informed electorate; opponents argue that compelled disclosure can chill free speech and expose donors to harassment. The Internal Revenue Service oversees the tax status of the organizations involved, and the boundary between permissible "social welfare" activity and political campaigning has been a recurring subject of controversy.
