The Great Chicago Fire was a conflagration that burned in the American city of Chicago during October 8–10, 1871. The fire killed approximately 300 people, destroyed roughly 3.3 square miles (9 km²) of the city including over 17,000 structures, and left more than 100,000 residents homeless.

Origin and spread

The fire began on the evening of Sunday, October 8, in or near a small barn belonging to the O'Leary family at 137 DeKoven Street on the city's southwest side. A long-popular legend blamed Catherine O'Leary's cow for kicking over a lantern, a story later admitted to have been fabricated by a reporter. Chicago had endured a dry summer, and the city was built largely of wood — including wooden sidewalks and roads — while a strong southwest wind carried embers across the Chicago River. The fire burned out of control until rain fell late on October 10.

Aftermath and rebuilding

Despite the devastation, much of Chicago's infrastructure — its railroads, stockyards and port — survived, and the city rebuilt rapidly. Donations poured in from around the world. The rebuilding prompted stricter fire codes and stimulated architectural innovation; within two decades Chicago had become a showcase of modern construction, hosting the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893, and the disaster is credited with spurring the development of the skyscraper.

The same night as the Chicago fire, the Peshtigo fire in Wisconsin burned far more land and killed far more people, but was overshadowed in public memory. In 1997 the Chicago City Council formally exonerated Catherine O'Leary and her cow.
