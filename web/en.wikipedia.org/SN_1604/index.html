SN 1604, also known as Kepler's Supernova or Kepler's Star, was a Type Ia supernova that occurred in the Milky Way, in the constellation Ophiuchus. Appearing in October 1604, it is the most recent supernova in our galaxy to have been unambiguously observed by the naked eye, at a distance of roughly 20,000 light-years.

Observation

The new star was first reported in October 1604 and was studied by astronomers across Europe and in China and Korea. Johannes Kepler investigated it from Prague and published his account in De Stella Nova (1606). At its peak it outshone all other stars and exceeded Jupiter in brightness, remaining visible in daylight for over three weeks and to the naked eye at night for about eighteen months.

Significance and remnant

Coming only 32 years after Tycho Brahe's supernova of 1572, SN 1604 further undermined the Aristotelian doctrine of the unchanging heavens. Its expanding remnant, Kepler's Supernova Remnant, is still studied by telescopes such as Chandra and Hubble, and its light echoes and shock dynamics indicate a Type Ia explosion — the thermonuclear disruption of a white dwarf.
