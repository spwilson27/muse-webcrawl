Battle of Sentinum

The valley near Sentinum (present-day Sassoferrato), where Rome broke the grand coalition of 295 BC.

Date295 BC
LocationSentinum, Umbria, Italy
ResultDecisive Roman victory
CommandersFabius Maximus Rullianus and Decius Mus vs. Gellius Egnatius (killed)

The Battle of Sentinum was fought in 295 BC near Sentinum in Umbria during the Third Samnite War. It was the largest and most decisive battle of Rome's conquest of Italy: a Roman army under the consuls Quintus Fabius Maximus Rullianus and Publius Decius Mus defeated a coalition of Samnites and Gauls, whose Etruscan and Umbrian allies had been drawn away before the battle.

Background

By 295 BC the Samnites had assembled the most dangerous coalition Rome had yet faced, uniting Samnite, Gallic (Senones), Etruscan and Umbrian forces to strike from the north while Samnium threatened from the south. Rome responded with a massive mobilisation — our sources speak of four legions at Sentinum with allied contingents, perhaps 36,000–40,000 men — and by raiding Etruria to force the Etruscans and Umbrians to stay home to defend their own territory.

The battle

At Sentinum, Fabius commanded the Roman right against the Samnites under Gellius Egnatius, and Decius the left against the Gauls. The Gallic chariots and cavalry threw Decius's wing into disorder, and Decius — following the example of his father and grandfather — performed the ritual of devotio, dedicating himself and the enemy to the gods of the underworld before charging alone into the Gallic ranks to his death. The sacrifice steadied his troops. On the other wing Fabius ground down the Samnites, then sent cavalry and infantry around to strike the Gauls in flank and rear. The coalition army collapsed; Livy gives enemy losses of 25,000 killed and 8,000 captured, against some 8,700 Roman dead.

Consequences

Sentinum broke the back of the coalition and of Samnite resistance. Within a few years the Samnites submitted, the Senones were crushed, and Rome's supremacy over the Italian peninsula was assured — the platform from which it would soon confront Pyrrhus and then Carthage. The self-sacrifice of Decius Mus became one of the most famous exempla of Roman patriotic legend.
