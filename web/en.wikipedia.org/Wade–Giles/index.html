Wade–Giles

Wade–Giles is a romanisation system for Mandarin Chinese developed from a system created by the British diplomat Thomas Francis Wade in the mid-19th century and completed by Herbert A. Giles in his 1892 Chinese–English Dictionary. For most of the twentieth century it was the dominant system for writing Chinese names and words in English, producing familiar spellings such as Peking (Beijing), Mao Tse-tung (Mao Zedong), and Tao Te Ching.

Features

Wade–Giles distinguishes aspirated and unaspirated consonants with an apostrophe: pinyin b, d, g correspond to Wade–Giles p, t, k, while pinyin p, t, k correspond to p', t', k'. It uses hs for the sound written x in pinyin (e.g., Hsün-tzu for Xunzi) and j for the retroflex sound written r in pinyin. Tones are indicated, when at all, by superscript numbers. Because newspapers and general readers routinely dropped apostrophes and diacritics, distinctions were often lost in popular usage.

Decline

The People's Republic of China developed Hanyu Pinyin, adopted officially in 1958 and by the United Nations and ISO in the late 1970s and 1980s. As English-language media switched to pinyin, Wade–Giles spellings receded, though they persist in Taiwan (where usage is mixed), in older scholarship, in library catalogues, and in entrenched names such as Kung fu, Tai chi (Wade–Giles t'ai chi), and the I Ching.
