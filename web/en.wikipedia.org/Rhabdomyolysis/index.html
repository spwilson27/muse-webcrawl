Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis is a syndrome caused by the rapid breakdown of injured skeletal muscle. Muscle-cell contents—including myoglobin, creatine kinase, potassium, and phosphate—enter the bloodstream and may cause electrolyte disturbances, acute kidney injury, compartment syndrome, and abnormal heart rhythms.

Causes

Crush injury and prolonged compression are classic causes. Other causes include extreme exertion, heat stroke, seizures, infections, metabolic and inherited muscle disorders, electrolyte abnormalities, toxins, alcohol, and medicines including some statins, especially when interacting with other risks. Muscle ischemia and reperfusion can worsen the injury.

Signs and diagnosis

Symptoms may include muscle pain, weakness, swelling, nausea, and dark tea- or cola-coloured urine from myoglobin, but the classic triad is often incomplete. Blood creatine kinase is markedly elevated and is the principal laboratory marker. Testing also assesses kidney function, potassium, calcium, phosphate, uric acid, acid–base state, and urine findings.

Complications

Myoglobin and volume depletion can obstruct and injure renal tubules. Potassium released from muscle may produce dangerous hyperkalaemia. Early hypocalcaemia can occur as calcium enters damaged muscle, sometimes followed by hypercalcaemia during recovery. Swelling within a fascial compartment may require urgent surgery.

Treatment

Treatment is medical and time-sensitive. It generally begins with prompt, carefully monitored intravenous fluids to restore circulating volume and maintain urine output, correction of electrolyte abnormalities, treatment of the underlying cause, and monitoring or support for kidney failure. Severe hyperkalaemia and compartment syndrome require emergency management.

Early recognition after trauma, exertional collapse, or unexplained kidney injury improves outcomes.
