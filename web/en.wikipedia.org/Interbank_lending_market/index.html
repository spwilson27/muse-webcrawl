Interbank lending market

The interbank lending market is the market in which banks lend funds to one another, typically for very short maturities from overnight to a few months. It redistributes reserves and liquidity through the banking system: banks with surplus funds lend to banks needing to meet payment obligations, reserve requirements or unexpected withdrawals. Interest rates in this market — historically summarised by benchmark rates such as LIBOR and EURIBOR, and in the United States by the effective federal funds rate for overnight lending of reserve balances — are a key transmission channel of monetary policy and an indicator of stress in the financial system.

Structure

Loans may be unsecured, based on the credit standing of the borrowing bank, or secured (collateralised), notably in repo-style arrangements against government securities. Terms range from overnight (the largest segment) through one week and one, three and six months. Banks quote bid and offer rates for each tenor, and brokers intermediate a large volume of transactions. Central banks influence the market through open market operations and standing facilities that set a corridor around the policy rate.

Benchmarks

For decades, banks' offered rates were surveyed to produce LIBOR, which became the reference rate for an enormous volume of loans, derivatives and securities. Following the LIBOR manipulation scandal, in which traders at major banks were found to have rigged submissions, and the recognition that underlying unsecured term lending had thinned, regulators phased LIBOR out (most panels ended in 2021–2023). It was replaced mainly by overnight risk-free rates compounded in arrears, such as SOFR (US dollar), €STR (euro) and SONIA (sterling).

2008 financial crisis

During the 2007–2008 financial crisis the interbank market froze: fearing unknown exposure to subprime-related losses, banks hoarded liquidity and unsecured term lending nearly ceased, with spreads such as the LIBOR–OIS spread spiking to unprecedented levels. Central banks responded with massive liquidity provision, term lending facilities, currency swap lines and guarantees of bank debt to restore the market's functioning — an episode that demonstrated how central the interbank market is to the wider credit system.
