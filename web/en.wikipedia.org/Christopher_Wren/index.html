Christopher Wren

Sir Christopher Wren (30 October 1632 – 8 March 1723) was an English architect, astronomer, mathematician and physicist who was one of the most highly acclaimed architects in the history of England. Known for his work in the English Baroque style, he was accorded responsibility for rebuilding 52 churches in the City of London after the Great Fire in 1666, including what is regarded as his masterpiece, St Paul's Cathedral, on Ludgate Hill, completed in 1710.

Life and scientific career

Wren was born in East Knoyle, Wiltshire, the son of a clergyman who became Dean of Windsor. He was educated at Westminster School and Wadham College, Oxford, where he became part of the circle of experimental philosophers that later formed the Royal Society. A child prodigy in mathematics, Wren was appointed Gresham Professor of Astronomy in London at 24 and Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford in 1661. His scientific work ranged over optics, mechanics, microscopy and anatomy — he performed early experiments in intravenous injection and blood transfusion in animals. He was a founder member and later President of the Royal Society.

Architecture

Wren's first architectural works included the Sheldonian Theatre in Oxford (1663–69) and Pembroke College Chapel, Cambridge. After a study trip to Paris in 1665, where he met Bernini, he returned to a London destroyed by the Great Fire of September 1666. His grand plan for rebuilding the City on a baroque street pattern was not adopted, but as Surveyor of the King's Works he directed the reconstruction of 52 parish churches — including St Mary-le-Bow, St Bride's and St Stephen Walbrook — and of St Paul's Cathedral (1675–1710), whose dome, a triple-shell structure of brick between lead-clad timber cones, became an icon of London. Other commissions included the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, Chelsea Hospital, the south and east fronts of Hampton Court, Kensington Palace, and the library of Trinity College, Cambridge.

Death and legacy

Dismissed from the Surveyorship in 1718, Wren died in 1723 and was buried in the crypt of St Paul's. His epitaph reads Si monumentum requiris, circumspice — "Reader, if you seek his monument, look around you." His influence shaped British architecture for a century, and the style of his City church steeples remains a defining feature of London's skyline.
