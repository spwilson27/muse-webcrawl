John Peter Zenger (26 October 1697 – 28 July 1746) was a German printer and journalist in New York City. His acquittal in the seditious libel trial of 1735 — in which his lawyer Andrew Hamilton argued that truth should be a defence against libel — became a landmark precedent for freedom of the press in the American colonies and an early seed of the First Amendment.

Early life

Zenger emigrated from the Palatinate to New York in 1710 as a child, was orphaned on the voyage, and was apprenticed to William Bradford, the colony's first printer. He set up his own print shop in 1726 and in 1733 founded the New-York Weekly Journal, backed by a political faction opposed to the unpopular royal governor, William Cosby.

The trial

The Journal published essays (written anonymously by Zenger's backers, including Lewis Morris and James Alexander) attacking Cosby's administration. In November 1734 Zenger was arrested for seditious libel and spent nearly ten months in jail, after his bail was set beyond his means. When his lawyers were disbarred for challenging the judges' commissions, the celebrated Philadelphia lawyer Andrew Hamilton took the case. At the trial in August 1735 Hamilton conceded that Zenger had printed the papers but urged the jury that truth could not be libel — asking them to judge the law itself. The jury acquitted Zenger within minutes, in one of the most famous instances of jury nullification in Anglo-American legal history.

Legacy

Zenger published an influential account of the trial in 1736 and remained New York's public printer. Though the verdict did not formally change libel law, it decisively shifted public opinion: criticism of government based on truth came to be seen as a right, a principle later embedded in American press freedom.
