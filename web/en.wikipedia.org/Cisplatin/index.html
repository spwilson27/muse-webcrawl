Cisplatin

Structure of cisplatin: a platinum(II) centre with two chloride and two ammine ligands in cis configuration.

Cisplatin is a chemotherapy medication used to treat a number of cancers. These include testicular cancer, ovarian cancer, cervical cancer, breast cancer, bladder cancer, head and neck cancer, esophageal cancer, lung cancer, mesothelioma, brain tumors and neuroblastoma. It is given by injection into a vein. Chemically, it is a coordination complex of platinum — cis-diamminedichloridoplatinum(II), Pt(NH3)2Cl2 — with a square-planar geometry.

History

The compound was first described by Michele Peyrone in 1845 ("Peyrone's salt"). Its biological activity was serendipitously discovered in 1965 by Barnett Rosenberg at Michigan State University, who found that electrolysis products from platinum electrodes inhibited division of Escherichia coli. Clinical trials followed, and the US Food and Drug Administration approved cisplatin in 1978. It transformed testicular cancer from a usually fatal disease into one with cure rates above 90% in many settings.

Mechanism and side effects

Inside the cell, cisplatin loses its chloride ligands and binds DNA, forming intra- and inter-strand crosslinks that distort the double helix, block replication and transcription, and trigger apoptosis. This attack on the DNA built around the atomic nucleus is non-selective, so rapidly dividing normal tissues are also harmed. Common and serious side effects include kidney damage (nephrotoxicity), severe nausea and vomiting (among the most emetogenic agents known), hearing loss (ototoxicity), peripheral neuropathy, and electrolyte disturbances. Hydration and antiemetic regimens mitigate toxicity; related drugs carboplatin and oxaliplatin were developed with different toxicity profiles.
