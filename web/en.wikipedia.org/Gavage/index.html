Gavage

Gavage (from the French gaver, "to gorge") is the practice of feeding an animal or person by means of a tube passed into the stomach, most commonly the force-feeding of domestic ducks and geese to produce foie gras. The term is also used in medicine and laboratory science for administering food, fluids or test substances through a tube.

Foie gras production

In foie gras production, during the final phase of rearing (lasting roughly two to three weeks for ducks and somewhat longer for geese), birds are fed large quantities of grain, usually maize, delivered through a tube inserted down the oesophagus into the crop or stomach, two or three times a day. The high-energy diet causes the liver to accumulate fat and enlarge to several times its normal size — a condition analogous to hepatic steatosis.

The practice is ancient: Egyptian tomb paintings from around 2500 BC depict the force-feeding of geese, and the technique spread through the Roman world, where Apicius describes feeding figs to pigs and geese for enlarged livers.

Animal welfare controversy

Gavage for foie gras is one of the most contested practices in animal agriculture. Critics argue that force-feeding, the enlarged fatty liver and associated mortality constitute cruelty; producers respond that migratory waterfowl naturally gorge before migration and that the process, properly done, does not injure the bird, whose oesophagus lacks a gag reflex. Production has been banned in a number of countries and regions, and bans on sale have been enacted in places including California and New York City (the latter later struck down in litigation). The European Union permits production only where it was traditional, and then under regulation.

Medical and scientific use

In medicine, tube feeding of patients unable to eat is usually called enteral or tube feeding rather than gavage, though the term survives in neonatal care. In pharmacology and toxicology, oral gavage is a standard route for dosing laboratory animals with precise quantities of a substance.
