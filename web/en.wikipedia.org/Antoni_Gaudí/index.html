Antoni Gaudí i Cornet (1852–1926) was a Catalan architect from Spain, known as the greatest exponent of Catalan Modernisme. His highly individual, nature-inspired style—characterised by organic forms, bold colour, and trencadís mosaics of broken tile—produced some of Barcelona's most famous buildings. Seven of his works are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Life and work

Gaudí studied architecture in Barcelona, graduating in 1878; the school's director reportedly remarked that he could not tell whether they had given the degree to a genius or a madman. His patron Eusebi Güell commissioned the Palau Güell, the crypt of Colònia Güell, and Park Güell. Other landmarks include Casa Vicens, Casa Batlló, and Casa Milà (La Pedrera).

Sagrada Família

From 1883 Gaudí took charge of the basilica of the Sagrada Família, devoting the last years of his life entirely to it and developing structural innovations—including catenary arches modelled with hanging chains and tilted, tree-like columns. He died in 1926, three days after being struck by a tram, and was buried in the basilica's crypt; the church, still unfinished, was consecrated by Pope Benedict XVI in 2010. Gaudí's deep Catholic faith earned him the nickname "God's Architect", and a cause for his beatification has been opened.

Style

Gaudí fused Gothic and Art Nouveau elements with forms drawn from nature—bones, trees, shells, and caves—and pioneering geometry (hyperboloids, paraboloids, ruled surfaces) that anticipated later structural design.
