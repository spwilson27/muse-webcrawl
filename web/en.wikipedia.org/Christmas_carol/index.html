Christmas carol

A Christmas carol is a song or hymn on the theme of Christmas, traditionally sung at or just before the holiday. Carols range from medieval sacred hymns such as "O Come, All Ye Faithful" to nineteenth-century compositions like "Silent Night" and secular festive songs. Singing carols — in church services, by choirs, and by groups going door to door — is a central custom of the Christmas season in much of the world.

History

The word carol derives from the Old French carole, a dance accompanied by singing. In the Middle Ages, carols were festive songs for many seasons, often mixing Latin and vernacular words (macaronic verse). St. Francis of Assisi's nativity plays in the 13th century encouraged popular songs about the birth of Christ. Many surviving carols date from the 15th century onward; others, like the "Coventry Carol," come from medieval mystery plays.

Carol singing declined under Puritan rule in 17th-century England but revived in the Victorian era, when collectors published old melodies and composers wrote new ones, including Charles Wesley's "Hark! The Herald Angels Sing," later set to a Mendelssohn tune. The service of Nine Lessons and Carols, developed in 1880 and popularized by King's College, Cambridge from 1918, became a defining broadcast tradition.

Famous carols

  • "Silent Night" — composed in Oberndorf, Austria, in 1818 by Franz Gruber to words by Joseph Mohr.
  • "O Holy Night" — French carol (Cantique de Noël, 1847) by Adolphe Adam.
  • "Good King Wenceslas" — Victorian lyric to a medieval spring melody.
  • "The Twelve Days of Christmas" — cumulative counting song.
  • "Joy to the World" — Isaac Watts, based on Psalm 98.
