Acromegaly

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Acromegaly is a rare hormonal disorder that develops when the pituitary gland produces too much growth hormone (GH) in adulthood, after the growth plates have closed. The excess hormone, acting largely through insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), causes bones and soft tissues—especially of the hands, feet and face—to enlarge gradually. When excess GH occurs in childhood, before the growth plates close, the result is gigantism rather than acromegaly.

Signs and symptoms

Changes develop slowly over years and diagnosis is often delayed. Features include enlargement of the hands and feet (increasing ring, glove and shoe sizes), protrusion of the jaw (prognathism), thickened skin, excessive sweating, joint pains, headaches, and visual field defects from pressure of the tumour on the optic chiasm. Complications include type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiomyopathy, sleep apnoea and an increased risk of colonic polyps and some cancers. Untreated, acromegaly substantially shortens life expectancy, mainly through cardiovascular disease.

Cause and diagnosis

In more than 95% of cases the cause is a benign pituitary tumour (adenoma). Diagnosis is by measuring IGF-1 and confirming that GH fails to suppress during an oral glucose tolerance test, followed by MRI imaging of the pituitary.

Treatment

The first-line treatment is transsphenoidal surgery to remove the adenoma. Where surgery does not control hormone levels, medicines are used: somatostatin analogues (such as octreotide and lanreotide), the growth-hormone receptor antagonist pegvisomant, and dopamine agonists such as cabergoline. Radiotherapy is a third-line option. Normalising IGF-1 levels returns life expectancy close to that of the general population.
