The Ogallala Aquifer is a vast shallow water-table aquifer beneath the Great Plains of the United States, one of the largest aquifers in the world. It underlies parts of South Dakota, Nebraska, Wyoming, Colorado, Kansas, Oklahoma, New Mexico and Texas — some 450,000 km² — and supplies drinking water and irrigation for one of the most productive agricultural regions on Earth.

Geology

The aquifer consists of sand, gravel, clay and silt of the Ogallala Formation, deposited by rivers flowing east from the Rocky Mountains millions of years ago. Saturated thickness ranges from a few metres at the margins to more than 300 metres in north-central Nebraska. Most of its water is "fossil water" left from the last ice age; recharge from precipitation is slow, especially in the arid south.

Use and depletion

Widespread pumping began after World War II with the spread of centre-pivot irrigation. About 90% of withdrawals irrigate crops such as corn, wheat, cotton and sorghum; the aquifer supports roughly a third of US irrigated agriculture. In heavily pumped areas of Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas, water levels have fallen by tens of metres, reducing saturated thickness and forcing some farmland back to dryland farming. Nebraska, where recharge is greater, has fared better. Conservation measures — improved irrigation efficiency, crop switching and groundwater management districts — aim to slow depletion.
