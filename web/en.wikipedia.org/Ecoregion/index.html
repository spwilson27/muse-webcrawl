Ecoregion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Terrestrial ecoregions of the world, grouped by biome.

An ecoregion (ecological region) is an ecologically and geographically defined area that is smaller than a bioregion, which in turn is smaller than a biogeographic realm. Ecoregions cover relatively large areas of land or water and contain characteristic, geographically distinct assemblages of natural communities and species. The boundaries are often drawn along combinations of climate, geology, soils and dominant vegetation.

Classification systems

The World Wildlife Fund (WWF) has identified 867 terrestrial ecoregions, organised into 14 biomes such as tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forests, deserts and xeric shrublands, and tundra. Freshwater and marine ecoregion systems have also been developed. In North America, the US Environmental Protection Agency's system, developed by James Omernik, uses a hierarchical framework of Level I through Level IV ecoregions.

Use in conservation

Ecoregions are a standard unit for conservation planning: the WWF's Global 200 list prioritised 238 ecoregions of exceptional biodiversity value. Because species composition changes between ecoregions, protecting representative habitat in each is treated as necessary to conserve global biodiversity.
