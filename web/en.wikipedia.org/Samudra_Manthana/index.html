Samudra Manthana

The churning of the Ocean of Milk, depicted in a bas-relief at Angkor Wat.

The Samudra Manthana (Sanskrit: समुद्रमन्थन, "churning of the ocean") is a major episode in Hindu mythology, recounted in the Mahabharata, the Ramayana, and the Puranas. In it, the devas (gods) and asuras (demons) cooperate to churn the Ocean of Milk in order to obtain amrita, the nectar of immortality.

The legend

Having lost their strength and fortune through the curse of the sage Durvasa, the devas were advised by Vishnu to churn the cosmic ocean together with the asuras, each side hoping for the amrita. Mount Mandara served as the churning rod and the serpent Vasuki as the rope; Vishnu, in his tortoise avatar Kurma, supported the mountain on his back. The devas held the tail end and the asuras the head end, pulling alternately for a thousand years.

Products of the churning

The churning produced both wonders and dangers. First emerged the deadly poison halahala, which threatened all creation until Shiva drank it, turning his throat blue and earning him the name Nilakantha. Thereafter appeared a series of treasures, varying by text: the goddess Lakshmi; the moon Chandra; the divine cow Kamadhenu; the elephant Airavata; the horse Uchchaihshravas; the gem Kaustubha; the apsaras; and Dhanvantari, the physician of the gods, bearing the pot of amrita.

The amrita

When the amrita appeared, the asuras seized it, but Vishnu took the form of the enchantress Mohini, distracted them, and distributed the nectar to the devas. The asura Svarbhanu, disguised as a deva, managed to drink some and was beheaded, becoming the immortal head Rahu and body Ketu, who periodically "swallow" the sun and moon, causing eclipses.

Cultural significance

The myth is depicted in temple sculpture across South and Southeast Asia, most famously in the great gallery relief at Angkor Wat in Cambodia and in modern monumental sculpture, including at Suvarnabhumi Airport in Bangkok.
