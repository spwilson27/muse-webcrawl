The Kojiki (古事記, "Records of Ancient Matters") is the oldest extant chronicle of Japan, presented to the imperial court in 712 CE. Compiled by the courtier Ō no Yasumaro from material recited by the court attendant Hieda no Are, the work records the myths and legendary history of Japan from the creation of the islands by the gods Izanagi and Izanami down to the reign of Empress Suiko in the seventh century.

Background

According to its preface, Emperor Tenmu ordered that the imperial genealogies and ancient tales be corrected and memorized, fearing that the records kept by the great families were drifting from the truth. Hieda no Are memorized the material, and after Tenmu's death Ō no Yasumaro wrote it down for Empress Genmei, presenting the finished work in 712.

Contents

The Kojiki is divided into three volumes. The first, the "Age of the Gods" (Kamitsumaki), describes the creation of heaven and earth, the deities (kami) of the Shinto pantheon, and the divine descent of the imperial line from the sun goddess Amaterasu. The second and third volumes blend legend with purported history, tracing the emperors from the legendary first emperor Jimmu through early historical rulers, interspersed with songs, poems, and folk tales.

Language

The text is written using Chinese characters in a mixture of classical Chinese and phonetic usage (an early form of man'yōgana) to represent Japanese words and names. Because of this, its precise reading was debated for centuries.

Legacy

Together with the Nihon Shoki (720), the Kojiki forms the mythological basis of Shinto and of the traditional account of the imperial institution. In the eighteenth century the scholar Motoori Norinaga devoted decades to his monumental commentary Kojiki-den, which was central to the kokugaku (national learning) movement.
