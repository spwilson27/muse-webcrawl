Duralumin

TypeAluminium alloy (2xxx series)
Main alloying elementsCopper (~4%), magnesium, manganese
Invented1906–1909, Alfred Wilm, Dürener Metallwerke, Germany
Typical usesAircraft structures, airship frames

Duralumin (also called duraluminum, duraluminium, duralium or dural) is a trade name for one of the earliest types of age-hardenable aluminium alloys. Its principal alloying elements are copper, manganese and magnesium, with a typical composition of about 4% copper, 1% magnesium and 0.5–1% manganese, the remainder aluminium. It is the archetype of the modern 2xxx alloy series, such as alloy 2024, still used in aircraft manufacture.

History

Duralumin was developed by the German metallurgist Alfred Wilm at Dürener Metallwerke between 1903 and 1909. Wilm discovered age hardening: after quenching from a solution heat treatment, the alloy grew markedly stronger over several days at room temperature as fine precipitates formed within the metal. The name derives from the town of Düren and aluminium.

Properties and use

Duralumin combines low density with strength comparable, weight for weight, to mild steel, and can be worked, machined and riveted readily. Its disadvantages are vulnerability to intergranular corrosion — later mitigated by cladding sheet with pure aluminium ("Alclad") — and poor weldability by fusion methods.

The alloy's lightness and strength made it transformative for aviation: it was used in Zeppelin airship frames during the First World War, in the all-metal Junkers aircraft, and extensively in the stressed-skin airframes of the 1930s and Second World War. Modern descendants remain standard for aircraft fuselage skins and wing structures.
