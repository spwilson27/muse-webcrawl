Multics (Multiplexed Information and Computing Service) was an influential early time-sharing operating system developed from 1964 by MIT, Bell Telephone Laboratories, and General Electric. Based on MIT's Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS), Multics was designed as a "computer utility" that would provide computing power as reliably as electricity or telephone service. The last production Multics system was shut down on October 30, 2000, at the Canadian Department of National Defence in Halifax.

Innovations

Multics introduced many concepts now fundamental to computing: a hierarchical file system with access control lists, dynamic linking, segmented virtual memory with paging, ring-based security protection, on-line reconfiguration, and the idea of writing an operating system in a high-level language (PL/I). It also pioneered the relational database model in commercial form (the Multics Relational Data Store) and hosted early electronic mail and the first electronic mailing lists.

Influence on Unix

Bell Labs withdrew from the project in 1969, judging it too ambitious and expensive. Two Bell Labs participants, Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie, drew directly on their Multics experience when creating Unix — the name itself is a pun on Multics ("Uniplexed Information and Computing Service"). Unix deliberately pursued a simpler, smaller design, but adopted Multics ideas such as the hierarchical file system, the shell as a user program, and device-independent I/O. Through Unix and its descendants (including Linux), Multics concepts became ubiquitous.

Security

Multics was evaluated by the U.S. Air Force and received one of the first B2 ratings under the NSA's Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria ("Orange Book"), the highest rating then achieved by a general-purpose system.
