Zaibatsu

Zaibatsu (Japanese: 財閥, literally "financial clique") is a Japanese term referring to industrial and financial vertically integrated business conglomerates in the Empire of Japan, whose influence and size allowed control over significant parts of the Japanese economy from the Meiji period until the end of World War II. A zaibatsu was typically controlled by a single wealthy family through a holding company, with a bank at its core providing finance to subsidiary companies across mining, manufacturing, trading, and shipping.

Structure

The classic zaibatsu was organized as a pyramid: a family-controlled holding company (honsha) owned a bank and a trading company, which in turn controlled operating subsidiaries in many industries. The "Big Four" zaibatsu were Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, and Yasuda; other significant groups included Asano, Fujita, Furukawa, Nissan, Nomura, Okura, and Shibusawa. Unlike Western holding companies, early zaibatsu did not sell shares to the public, keeping control firmly within the founding family.

History

The zaibatsu grew out of merchant houses of the Edo period (most famously Mitsui and Sumitomo) and expanded rapidly during Japan's Meiji-era industrialization, often acquiring state-built enterprises at low prices. They became pillars of the economy and of Japanese imperial expansion, dominating heavy industry and war production in the 1930s. Newer "shinko zaibatsu" such as Nissan, led by professional managers rather than founding families, arose especially in occupied Manchuria.

Dissolution and legacy

After Japan's defeat in 1945, the Allied occupation authorities under SCAP moved to dissolve the zaibatsu as part of democratizing the economy: holding companies were broken up, family shareholdings were seized and sold, and hundreds of executives were purged. The dissolution was only partially completed before the "reverse course" of the early Cold War. In the postwar decades, former zaibatsu companies regrouped as looser, bank-centered networks called keiretsu — notably the Mitsubishi, Mitsui, and Sumitomo groups — which remain prominent in the Japanese economy.
