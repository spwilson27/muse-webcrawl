Greater trochanter

Bone: Femur (proximal end)

Articulation region: Hip

Contrasts with: Lesser trochanter

The greater trochanter of the femur is a large, somewhat quadrilateral bony prominence on the lateral side of the proximal femur, just below the hip joint. It is easily palpable at the side of the hip and serves as the attachment point for several of the muscles that move the thigh, including the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus, the piriformis, the obturator muscles and the gemelli.

Anatomy

The greater trochanter projects upward and laterally from the junction of the neck and shaft of the femur. Its lateral surface gives insertion to the gluteus medius, while the gluteus minimus attaches to its anterior surface. A depression on its medial side, the trochanteric fossa, receives the tendon of the obturator externus. The structure develops from its own ossification centre (a traction epiphysis) appearing around the fourth year of life, and fuses with the femur in late adolescence.

Clinical significance

Pain over the greater trochanter — greater trochanteric pain syndrome — is a common cause of lateral hip pain, most often due to gluteal tendinopathy or inflammation of the surrounding bursae rather than true bursitis. Fractures of the greater trochanter occur in falls and in intertrochanteric hip fractures in older adults. The position of the greater trochanter is also a landmark in orthopaedic examination and in hip replacement surgery, where restoration of its height and offset helps restore normal hip mechanics.
