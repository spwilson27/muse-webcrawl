Sámi peoples

The Sámi (also spelled Sami or Saami) are the traditionally Sámi-speaking Indigenous peoples of Sápmi, a region spanning northern Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia's Kola Peninsula. They are the only Indigenous people officially recognised and protected under international conventions in northern Europe. The Sámi population is estimated at between 80,000 and 100,000 people across the four countries.

Sámi in traditional clothing (gákti) at a gathering in Norway.

History

The Sámi have inhabited northern Fennoscandia for thousands of years. Historically they lived by hunting, fishing, trapping and semi-nomadic reindeer herding. From the Middle Ages onward they came under increasing pressure from the expanding Scandinavian kingdoms, Finland and Russia, which taxed and colonised their lands. In the 19th and 20th centuries, assimilation policies such as Norway's fornorsking ("Norwegianisation") suppressed Sámi languages and culture; boarding schools punished children for speaking Sámi. Since the late 20th century, Sámi political mobilisation — galvanised by conflicts such as the Alta dam dispute in Norway (1979–1981) — has won legal recognition, language rights, and elected Sámi parliaments in Norway (1989), Finland (1996) and Sweden (1993).

Languages

The Sámi languages belong to the Uralic language family and are most closely related to Finnish and Estonian. Of the roughly ten Sámi languages, Northern Sámi is by far the most widely spoken; several others, such as Pite Sámi and Ume Sámi, are critically endangered.

Culture

Distinctive elements of Sámi culture include the gákti (traditional clothing), the joik (a traditional form of song), the duodji handicraft tradition, and the Sámi flag, adopted in 1986. Reindeer herding remains economically and culturally important, though only a minority of Sámi are full-time herders. The Sámi National Day is celebrated on 6 February.
