Principal–agent problem

The principal–agent problem refers to the conflict in interests and priorities that arises when one person or entity (the "agent") is able to make decisions or take actions on behalf of, or that impact, another person or entity (the "principal"). The dilemma exists because the agent is sometimes motivated in their own interests rather than in those of the principal.

The problem arises where the two parties have different interests and asymmetric information, such that the principal cannot directly ensure that the agent is always acting in their (the principal's) best interest, particularly when activities that are useful to the principal are costly to the agent, and where elements of what the agent does are costly for the principal to observe.

Overview

The principal–agent problem is a longstanding issue in economics, political science, and organizational theory. Common examples include the relationship between shareholders (principals) and corporate executives (agents), voters (principals) and politicians (agents), and clients (principals) and lawyers or brokers (agents).

Because the agent has more information about their own actions and intentions than the principal, the principal faces problems of moral hazard and adverse selection. Costs incurred by principals to monitor agents, and by agents to signal trustworthiness, are known as agency costs.

Mechanisms

Principals attempt to align agents' incentives with their own through contracts, performance-based pay, stock options, monitoring, bonding, and the threat of termination. In corporate governance, boards of directors are themselves an intermediate layer of agency between shareholders and managers.

Applications

The framework has been applied to insurance, employment contracts, public bureaucracy, international relations, and healthcare, where doctors act as agents for patients. Critics note that the model can oversimplify human motivation by assuming narrowly self-interested behaviour.
