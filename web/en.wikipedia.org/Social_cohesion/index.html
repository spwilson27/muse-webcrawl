Social cohesion

Social cohesion
FieldSociology; social policy
Related conceptsSocial capital; solidarity; trust
Key thinkerÉmile Durkheim

Social cohesion is the degree of social integration and inclusion in communities and society at large, and the extent to which mutual solidarity finds expression among individuals and communities. A cohesive society is one in which citizens feel a sense of belonging, participate in public life, and trust one another and public institutions.

Origins

The concept descends from Émile Durkheim's work on solidarity — "mechanical" solidarity based on likeness in traditional societies and "organic" solidarity based on interdependence in modern ones. In the late twentieth century, "social cohesion" became a major policy term in Europe and Canada, often defined in contrast to social exclusion.

Dimensions

Analysts typically distinguish several dimensions: shared values and civic culture; social order and control; social networks and social capital; territorial belonging and identity; and equality of opportunity. Measurement commonly draws on survey trust questions, participation rates, and indicators of inequality and segregation.

Policy debates

Governments have pursued cohesion through integration policies, anti-discrimination law, and community programmes. Critics warn the term can be used to pressure minorities to conform, and researchers debate whether ethnic diversity temporarily reduces local trust, as argued in Robert Putnam's "hunkering down" thesis.
