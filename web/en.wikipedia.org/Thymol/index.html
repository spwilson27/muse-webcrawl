Thymol (also known as 2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol, IPMP) is a natural monoterpenoid phenol derivative of p-cymene, C10H14O, found in oil of thyme, and extracted from Thymus vulgaris (common thyme), ajwain, and various other plants as a white crystalline substance of a pleasant aromatic odor and strong antiseptic properties.

History

Thyme was used by the ancient Egyptians in embalming and by the ancient Greeks in baths and as incense. Thymol itself was discovered in 1719 by the German chemist Caspar Neumann, and its pure synthesis and structural characterization followed in the nineteenth century; the chemist Oskar Widmann named it “thymol” in 1882.

Biological activity

Thymol has antimicrobial and antifungal activity and disrupts bacterial cell membranes. It is an active ingredient in some mouthwashes, including the original Listerine formulation, and is used as a preservative in halothane, as a stabilizer in pharmaceutical preparations, and as a pesticide, notably against the Varroa mite in beekeeping.

Chemistry

Thymol is only slightly soluble in water at neutral pH, but is extremely soluble in alcohols and other organic solvents. It is an isomer of carvacrol, differing in the placement of the hydroxyl group on the aromatic ring. Industrially, thymol can be produced by the alkylation of m-cresol with propene.

Uses

Beyond mouthwash and antiseptics, thymol is used to preserve documents and books against mold, in anesthetics, and as a flavoring. Because of its ability to kill microorganisms, it also appears in some hand sanitizers and household disinfectants.
