Intracranial pressure

Intracranial pressure (ICP) is the pressure exerted by fluids, principally cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), inside the skull and on the brain tissue. In a healthy adult at rest it is normally about 7–15 mmHg. Sustained elevations above roughly 20 mmHg constitute intracranial hypertension, a medical emergency that can cause brain herniation and death.

Physiology

According to the Monro–Kellie doctrine, the rigid skull contains a fixed total volume of brain tissue, blood and CSF; an increase in any one component must be offset by decreases in the others, or pressure rises steeply. Compensatory displacement of CSF into the spinal canal and reduced cerebral venous blood volume work only up to a point, after which small volume increases cause large pressure rises.

Causes and symptoms

Raised ICP may result from traumatic brain injury, haemorrhage, tumours, hydrocephalus, meningitis, cerebral oedema or idiopathic intracranial hypertension. Signs include headache, vomiting, altered consciousness, papilloedema, and the Cushing reflex of hypertension, bradycardia and irregular breathing. In infants, whose skull sutures are not fused, it may present with a bulging fontanelle and enlarging head circumference.

Monitoring and treatment

ICP is monitored invasively in severe brain injury, most accurately with an external ventricular drain or an intraparenchymal probe. Management aims to keep ICP below about 22 mmHg and cerebral perfusion pressure adequate, using head elevation, sedation, osmotic agents such as mannitol or hypertonic saline, CSF drainage, and in refractory cases decompressive craniectomy.
