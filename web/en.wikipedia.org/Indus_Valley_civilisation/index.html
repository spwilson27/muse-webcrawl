Indus Valley civilisation

The Indus Valley civilisation (IVC), also known as the Harappan civilisation, was a Bronze Age civilisation in the northwestern regions of South Asia, lasting from 3300 BCE to 1300 BCE, and in its mature form from 2600 BCE to 1900 BCE. Together with ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, it was one of three early civilisations of the Near East and South Asia, and of the three, the most widespread: its sites spanned an area greater than 1.5 million square kilometres, from northeast Afghanistan through much of Pakistan and into western and northwestern India, centred on the Indus River basin.

Cities and society

The civilisation is noted for its planned cities built of fired brick — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Lothal, Kalibangan and Rakhigarhi among them — with grid layouts, sophisticated drainage and water-supply systems, and structures such as Mohenjo-daro's Great Bath. The Harappans developed accurate systems of weights and measures, produced seals bearing signs of the still-undeciphered Indus script, carved stone and bronze (the "Dancing Girl" figurine), and traded with Mesopotamia, where they appear as "Meluhha". Notably, no unambiguous palaces, temples or royal tombs have been identified, leading scholars to debate whether society was relatively egalitarian or ruled by priest-merchant elites.

Decline

From around 1900 BCE the cities were gradually abandoned as the climate dried and river systems shifted (including the weakening of the Ghaggar-Hakra), trade declined and populations moved eastward and southward into smaller settlements during the Late Harappan phase.
