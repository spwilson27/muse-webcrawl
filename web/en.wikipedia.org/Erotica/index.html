Erotica

Erotica is literature or art that deals substantively with subject matter that is erotic, sexually stimulating or sexually arousing, but is not typically considered pornography. Erotic works have existed in virtually every culture and medium, from ancient poetry and sculpture to contemporary fiction and photography.

History

Ancient examples include the love poems of Sappho, the Sanskrit Kama Sutra (a treatise on love and desire), and erotic imagery in Greek vase painting and Roman art such as the frescoes of Pompeii. In early modern Europe, works such as Aretino's sonnets and the clandestine novels of the 18th century circulated despite censorship. Famous literary erotica includes Fanny Hill (1748), the writings of the Marquis de Sade, The Pearl magazine (1879–80), D. H. Lawrence's Lady Chatterley's Lover (1928), Henry Miller's Tropic of Cancer (1934) and Anaïs Nin's Delta of Venus (written 1940s).

Erotica and pornography

Distinctions between erotica and pornography are contested and frequently framed in terms of artistic intent, explicitness and emphasis: erotica is often said to explore sexuality within a narrative or aesthetic context, whereas pornography is said to exist primarily for sexual arousal. Legal frameworks, such as obscenity law in the United States and the UK Obscene Publications Act, have historically treated such works according to prevailing standards, with landmark trials (for example over Lady Chatterley's Lover in 1960) liberalising publication.

Modern erotica

The late 20th and 21st centuries saw growth in erotica written by and for women, LGBTQ+ erotica, and self-published e-book erotica; the commercial success of Fifty Shades of Grey (2011) brought mainstream attention to the genre.
