Edge detection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The output of the Canny edge detector applied to a photograph.

Edge detection includes a variety of mathematical methods that aim at identifying edges — curves in a digital image at which the image brightness changes sharply or, more formally, has discontinuities. The purpose of detecting sharp changes in image brightness is to capture important events and changes in properties of the world, such as object boundaries, surface markings and discontinuities in depth or surface orientation.

Methods

Simple approaches compute the image gradient with small convolution kernels: the Roberts cross, Prewitt and Sobel operators estimate derivatives in two directions, and the resulting gradient magnitude is thresholded to mark edges. The Laplacian of Gaussian responds to zero crossings of the second derivative, following smoothing with a Gaussian filter as in the Marr–Hildreth algorithm.

The Canny edge detector

John Canny's 1986 algorithm remains a benchmark: the image is smoothed, gradients are computed, non-maximum suppression thins the responses to one-pixel lines, and hysteresis thresholding links strong edges to connected weak ones, giving good detection, good localisation and single responses to each edge.

Applications

Edge detection is a foundational step in image segmentation, feature extraction, medical imaging, document scanning and computer vision systems such as lane detection and industrial inspection.
