History play

A history play is a dramatic work whose plot is drawn from historical events and persons. The term is most closely associated with the English theatre of the late 16th and early 17th centuries, above all with William Shakespeare, whose ten plays on English kings — from King John to Henry VIII — were classed as "histories" in the First Folio of 1623. History plays dramatise questions of kingship, legitimacy, rebellion, and national identity, and the genre has recurred in later centuries in verse drama, historical film, and television series.

Origins

Historical material entered English drama through the morality plays and chronicle dramatisations of the mid-16th century, such as The Famous Victories of Henry V and the anonymous plays on which Shakespeare later drew. The genre grew alongside a surge of printed national history — Edward Hall's Union of the Two Noble and Illustre Families of Lancastre and Yorke (1548) and Raphael Holinshed's Chronicles — and a patriotic audience created by England's conflicts with Spain and anxiety over the succession to Elizabeth I.

Shakespeare's histories

Shakespeare wrote two great sequences. The first tetralogy (c. 1590–93) dramatises the Wars of the Roses from Henry VI through the rise and fall of Richard III. The second tetralogy, or Henriad (1595–99), runs from the deposition of Richard II, through Prince Hal's education in Henry IV Parts 1 and 2 — with the comic counterweight of Falstaff — to the triumph of Henry V at Agincourt. Critics have long debated whether the plays endorse the "Tudor myth" of providential history or subject power to sceptical scrutiny; modern criticism generally reads them as doing both at once. King John and the collaborative Henry VIII (1613) stand outside the sequences.

The genre after Shakespeare

Jacobean and Caroline dramatists continued the form, but the history play declined with the theatres' closure in 1642. It revived in Romantic and 19th-century verse drama (Schiller's Wallenstein trilogy and Mary Stuart are German exemplars), in closet drama, and in the 20th century in works such as Brecht's historically distanced plays. Robert Bolt's A Man for All Seasons (1960) is a modern stage landmark, while historical film and prestige television — from adaptations of the Henriad to series such as Wolf Hall — carry the genre's concerns to mass audiences.

Characteristics

History plays typically mix public scenes of council, battle, and ceremony with private or low-life scenes that comment on them; they draw their plots from chronicle sources while inventing dialogue and interior motivation; and they are preoccupied with the legitimacy of rulers, the costs of civil war, and the making of national memory. Because they dramatise real events selectively, they are also documents of the politics of their own time as much as of the periods they depict.
