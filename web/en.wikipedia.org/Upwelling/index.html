Upwelling

Wind blowing along a coast drives surface water offshore; deep water rises to replace it.

Upwelling is an oceanographic phenomenon that involves wind-driven motion of dense, cooler, and usually nutrient-rich water from deep water towards the ocean surface. It replaces the warmer and usually nutrient-depleted surface water. The nutrient-rich upwelled water stimulates the growth and reproduction of primary producers such as phytoplankton. The biomass of phytoplankton and the presence of cool water in those regions allow upwelling zones to be identified by cool sea surface temperatures and high concentrations of chlorophyll a.

The increased availability of nutrients in upwelling regions results in high levels of primary production and thus fishery production. Approximately 25% of the total global marine fish catches come from five upwelling regions that occupy only 5% of the total ocean area. Upwellings that are driven by coastal currents or diverging open ocean currents have the greatest impact on nutrient-enriched waters and global fishery yields.

Mechanisms

The three main drivers that work together to cause upwelling are wind, Coriolis effects, and Ekman transport. When wind blows along a coastline, friction and the Coriolis effect deflect the net transport of the surface layer about 90 degrees — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. If that transport is directed away from the coast, deeper water rises to fill the void. Upwelling also occurs in the open ocean where winds cause surface waters to diverge, for example along the equator and around Antarctica in the Southern Ocean.

Types

  • Coastal upwelling — the best-known type, occurring along western continental margins such as the California, Peru (Humboldt), Canary, and Benguela current systems.
  • Equatorial upwelling — driven by the divergence of the trade winds at the equator, especially in the Pacific and Atlantic.
  • Southern Ocean upwelling — powerful circumpolar upwelling that brings deep water to the surface around Antarctica.
  • Seasonal upwelling — driven by monsoon winds, as in the Arabian Sea off Somalia and Oman.
  • Artificial upwelling — proposed and experimental pumping of deep water to fertilise surface waters or generate ocean thermal energy.

Major upwelling regions

The eastern boundary current systems support some of the world's great fisheries. The Peru Current upwelling sustains the Peruvian anchoveta fishery, historically the largest single-species fishery on Earth; it weakens dramatically during El Niño events, when warm water caps the surface and fisheries collapse. The Benguela system off Namibia and South Africa supports sardine and hake fisheries, while the California Current system supports sardines, anchovies, and market squid.

Ecological importance

Because upwelled water is cold and rich in nitrate, phosphate, and dissolved iron accumulated in the deep ocean, upwelling zones are hotspots of marine productivity and biodiversity. They support dense phytoplankton blooms, large populations of zooplankton and forage fish, and the seabirds and marine mammals that feed on them. The interplay of upwelling and climate variability strongly influences coastal weather, fog, and regional ecosystems.

Downwelling

The opposite process, downwelling, occurs where surface waters converge and sink, carrying oxygen-rich surface water to depth — important for ventilating the deep ocean but generally reducing nutrient supply and productivity at the surface.
