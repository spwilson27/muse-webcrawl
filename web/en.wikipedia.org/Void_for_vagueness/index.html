Void for vagueness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Void for vagueness is a legal concept in American constitutional law that holds that a law is invalid and unenforceable if it is too unclear for the average person to understand what conduct it prohibits, and if it does not give clear standards to those who enforce it. The doctrine derives from the due process clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution.

Doctrine

The United States Supreme Court has identified two connected concerns. First, laws must give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that contemplated conduct is forbidden. Second, laws must set minimal guidelines for police, prosecutors and juries, so that enforcement does not become arbitrary or discriminatory. A statute may be held void for vagueness on its face or as applied to particular conduct.

Leading cases

Important decisions include Connally v. General Construction Co. (1926), which stated the classic fair-notice formulation; Grayned v. City of Rockford (1972); Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville (1972), striking down a vagrancy ordinance; Kolender v. Lawson (1983); Johnson v. United States (2015), which invalidated the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act; and Sessions v. Dimaya (2018), applying the same reasoning to an immigration statute.

Related doctrines

The doctrine is closely related to the rule of lenity, the prohibition on ex post facto laws, and the overbreadth doctrine in First Amendment law, though it applies to laws of every subject matter, civil provisions sometimes being held to a somewhat more tolerant standard than criminal ones.
