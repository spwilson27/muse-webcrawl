Jewish prayer


Jewish prayer (tefillah) is the prayer recitation that forms part of the observance of Rabbinic Judaism.

Jewish prayer (Hebrew: תְּפִלָּה, tefillah, plural תְּפִלּוֹת, tefillot) is the prayer recitation that forms part of the observance of Rabbinic Judaism. These prayers, often with instructions and commentary, are found in the siddur, the traditional Jewish prayer book.

Daily services

Traditionally, three prayer services are recited daily: Shacharit (morning), Mincha (afternoon), and Ma'ariv or Arvit (evening), corresponding, according to tradition, to the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and to the daily Temple sacrifices. An additional service, Musaf, is recited on Shabbat and festivals, and Ne'ila closes Yom Kippur. Communal prayer traditionally requires a quorum (minyan) of ten Jewish adults.

Structure

The central elements of the services include the Shema Yisrael — the declaration of God's unity from Deuteronomy, recited morning and evening — and the Amidah (Shemoneh Esrei), a series of blessings recited standing, which forms the core of every service. Other elements include the Pesukei Dezimra (verses of praise), the Torah reading on designated days, the Aleinu, and the Kaddish, recited by mourners and at section breaks.

Customs and rites

Observant Jews often pray wearing a tallit (prayer shawl) and, on weekday mornings, tefillin (phylacteries). Prayer texts vary by rite (nusach) — Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Mizrahi, and others — and by movement: Orthodox services are almost entirely in Hebrew, while Reform and other liberal movements incorporate more of the vernacular and egalitarian adaptations. Worshippers traditionally face Jerusalem, and in Jerusalem toward the Temple Mount.
