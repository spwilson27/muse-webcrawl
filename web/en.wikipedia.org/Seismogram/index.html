Seismogram

A seismogram records ground motion over time; the gap between P-wave and S-wave arrivals reveals the distance to the earthquake.

A seismogram is a graph output by a seismograph recording the ground motion at a measuring station as a function of time. Historically seismograms were recorded on paper wrapped around a rotating drum, on photographic film, or printed by a pen; modern instruments record digitally. Seismograms are the primary data of seismology, used to locate and characterise earthquakes and to probe the Earth's interior structure.

Reading a seismogram

An earthquake seismogram typically shows a sequence of arrivals: the first, relatively small oscillations are the P-waves (primary, compressional waves); the larger later arrivals are the S-waves (shear waves); and the largest-amplitude, longest-period oscillations are the surface waves. Because P-waves travel faster than S-waves, the time interval between their arrivals increases with distance from the epicentre; combining seismograms from at least three stations allows seismologists to triangulate the epicentre. Amplitudes and frequency content are used to determine magnitude and focal mechanism.

Components and stations

Complete stations record three orthogonal components of motion — vertical and two horizontal (north–south and east–west) — displayed as separate traces. Networks of stations share data in standard formats such as MiniSEED through organisations like the IRIS Consortium and the USGS, enabling rapid automatic location of earthquakes worldwide within minutes.

Uses

Beyond earthquake monitoring, seismograms are used in exploration geophysics, nuclear-test monitoring under the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty, volcano monitoring, and studies of ocean waves, glaciers and even human activity.
