John (24 December 1166 – 19 October 1216) was King of England from 1199 until his death in 1216. He lost the Duchy of Normandy and most of his other French lands to King Philip II of France, resulting in the collapse of the Angevin Empire and contributing to the growth in power of the French Capetian dynasty during the 13th century. The baronial revolt at the end of John's reign led to the Magna Carta, a document considered an early step in the growth of parliamentary democracy in England.

Early life

John was the youngest son of Henry II of England and Eleanor of Aquitaine. Nicknamed "Lackland" (Sans Terre) because no substantial territory was reserved for him, he nonetheless became his father's favourite in his later years, receiving the lordship of Ireland in 1177. During Richard I's absence on the Third Crusade and captivity (1192–1194), John intrigued with Philip II to seize the throne, but was forgiven by Richard, who named him heir over their nephew Arthur of Brittany.

Reign

John succeeded Richard in 1199, though his succession was contested by Arthur, backed by Philip II. The Treaty of Le Goulet (1200) recognised John, but war resumed; after the suspicious death of Arthur while John's prisoner in 1203, opinion turned against him. In 1204 Philip conquered Normandy, Anjou, and Maine, leaving England's kings mainly Gascony on the continent. John quarrelled with Pope Innocent III over the appointment of Stephen Langton as Archbishop of Canterbury, bringing an interdict on England (1208) and his own excommunication (1209), before submitting in 1213 and accepting England as a papal fief.

John raised unprecedented revenues through scutage, feudal reliefs, fines, and forest law to finance attempts to recover his French lands, alienating the barons. His campaign of 1214 ended in failure at the Battle of Bouvines, where his allies were crushed. In 1215 the barons rebelled and seized London; at Runnymede on 15 June 1215 John sealed the Magna Carta, limiting royal power and asserting legal rights. Pope Innocent annulled the charter, and civil war (the First Barons' War) followed, with the barons inviting Prince Louis of France to take the throne.

Death and legacy

While campaigning in the east in October 1216, John contracted dysentery and died at Newark Castle on 19 October 1216. He was buried in Worcester Cathedral. His nine-year-old son Henry III succeeded, and the Magna Carta was reissued in Henry's name, helping to rally support and end the war. John's reputation among historians was long dominated by hostile monastic chroniclers and the Robin Hood legends' "Prince John"; modern scholarship gives a more mixed verdict — an energetic and capable administrator whose fiscal demands, distrustfulness, and political failures provoked rebellion.
