Astronomical spectroscopy

Astronomical spectroscopy is the study of astronomy using the techniques of spectroscopy to measure the spectrum of electromagnetic radiation, including visible light, ultraviolet, X-ray, infrared and radio waves, emitted by stars and other celestial objects. A stellar spectrum can reveal an object's chemical composition, temperature, density, mass, distance, luminosity and radial velocity via the Doppler effect.

History

Joseph von Fraunhofer mapped hundreds of dark lines in the solar spectrum in 1814; Kirchhoff and Bunsen later showed that each chemical element produces a characteristic pattern of spectral lines. William Huggins applied spectroscopy to stars and nebulae in the 1860s, and Annie Jump Cannon's classification of stellar spectra at Harvard underpinned the Henry Draper Catalogue. Helium was identified in the Sun's spectrum (1868) before it was found on Earth.

Principles

Hot dense bodies emit a continuous spectrum; hot rarefied gases emit bright emission lines; and a continuous source viewed through cooler gas shows dark absorption lines. The wavelengths of lines shift with radial velocity (Doppler shift), broaden with temperature and rotation, and split in magnetic fields (Zeeman effect). Spectral resolution ranges from low-resolution survey spectrographs to echelle instruments exceeding R = 100,000 for exoplanet radial-velocity work.

Applications

Spectroscopy underlies stellar classification, the measurement of cosmic expansion through redshift, the discovery of exoplanets by the radial-velocity method, the determination of interstellar medium composition, and the study of active galactic nuclei and supernovae.

Instruments

Modern observatories use fibre-fed and multi-object spectrographs such as HARPS, ESPRESSO and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey instruments, which have collected millions of spectra of stars and galaxies.
