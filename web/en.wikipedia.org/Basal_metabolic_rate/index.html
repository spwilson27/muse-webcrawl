Basal metabolic rate

Basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the rate of energy expenditure per unit time by endothermic animals at rest. It is the minimum amount of energy required to sustain the body's vital functions — including circulation, breathing, cell production, nutrient processing, protein synthesis, and ion transport — while in a state of complete physical and mental rest, in a thermally neutral environment, and in a post-absorptive (fasting) state.

BMR is measured under strictly controlled resting conditions.

Measurement and estimation

BMR is properly measured by indirect calorimetry under controlled conditions: after an overnight fast and restful sleep, lying awake and motionless in a darkened, temperature-controlled room. In practice it is usually estimated from equations such as the Harris–Benedict equations (1918–19, revised 1984) and the Mifflin–St Jeor equation (1990), which use weight, height, age, and sex. For adult humans BMR typically ranges from about 1,200 to 2,400 kilocalories per day; the related resting metabolic rate (RMR) is measured under less stringent conditions and is slightly higher.

Factors affecting BMR

BMR varies with body mass and composition (muscle tissue is metabolically more active than fat), age (it declines roughly 1–2% per decade after early adulthood), sex, body surface area, hormonal status — particularly thyroid hormones — and genetic factors. The brain, liver, heart, and kidneys account for a disproportionately large share of resting energy expenditure relative to their mass.
