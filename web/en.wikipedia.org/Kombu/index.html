Kombu

Dried kombu, the foundation of Japanese dashi stock.

Kombu (Japanese: 昆布) is edible kelp, mostly of the species Saccharina japonica and relatives in the family Laminariaceae, harvested from the cold seas around northern Japan, especially Hokkaido, and widely used in Japanese, Korean and Chinese cooking. Dried into stiff, dark green-black sheets dusted with white mannitol crystals, kombu is prized as the principal source of the savoury taste umami.

Culinary use

Kombu's most important role is in dashi, the stock fundamental to Japanese cuisine: it is steeped in water heated to about 60 °C — boiling makes it bitter and slimy — often together with katsuobushi (bonito flakes), whose inosinic acid multiplies the glutamates of the kelp for a powerful synergistic umami. The chemist Kikunae Ikeda identified glutamic acid from kombu dashi in 1908 and named the taste umami. Kombu is also simmered in nimono dishes, cut into strips for tsukudani, used to make kombu tea (kobucha), salt-cured with fish (kobujime), and shaved into fluffy tororo and oboro garnishes.

Production and nutrition

Most kombu is wild-harvested, though aquaculture is growing; famous varieties include ma-kombu from Hakodate, Rausu, Rishiri and Hidaka kombu, which differ in flavour and use. Kombu is rich in iodine, dietary fibre, calcium and glutamates, but its high iodine content means excessive consumption can affect thyroid function.
