Glory (optical phenomenon)

A glory is an optical phenomenon, resembling an icon's halo, that appears as one or more concentric, successively dimmer, coloured rings around the shadow of an observer's head or of an aircraft cast onto clouds or mist below. Glories are caused by sunlight or (less commonly) moonlight interacting with the tiny water droplets of mist or clouds, the light being diffracted and backscattered toward its source.

Observation

Glories are most often seen by airline passengers, who can observe the shadow of the aircraft surrounded by coloured rings on clouds below; the rings always centre on the point directly opposite the Sun, the antisolar point. Mountaineers see them around the shadow of their own head when looking down into mist, a sight historically associated with the Brocken spectre, in which the magnified shadow of the observer appears on mist with a glory around its head. In China the phenomenon was traditionally called Buddha's light.

Explanation

Unlike a rainbow, which is produced by refraction and internal reflection in relatively large raindrops, a glory arises from wave tunnelling and diffraction in very small droplets. Light entering a droplet can be reflected internally once and emerge in the backward direction; interactions between rays travelling along different paths through the droplet produce interference, creating the concentric rings. The angular size of a glory is much smaller than that of a rainbow — typically between 5 and 20 degrees — and depends strongly on droplet size: smaller droplets produce larger rings. The full wave-optical explanation requires Mie scattering theory.

In spaceflight and other planets

Glories have been photographed from spacecraft, including from the Space Shuttle and the International Space Station. In 2014 the European Space Agency's Venus Express orbiter observed a glory in the atmosphere of Venus, the first seen on another planet, allowing scientists to estimate the size of the droplets forming Venus's cloud tops.
