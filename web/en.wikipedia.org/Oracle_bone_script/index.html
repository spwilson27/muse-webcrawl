Oracle bone script (Chinese: 甲骨文; pinyin: jiǎgǔwén) is the oldest attested form of written Chinese, dating to the late 2nd millennium BC. Inscriptions were made by carving characters into animal bones or shells — ox scapulae and turtle plastrons — which were used in pyromantic divination by the royal court of the late Shang dynasty. The vast majority of the inscribed pieces — over 150,000 — were excavated from Yinxu (modern Anyang, Henan).

The divinations concerned matters such as warfare, agriculture, childbirth, weather, and the king's health and dreams. A hot poker was applied to the bone or shell, producing cracks whose shapes were interpreted; the question, and sometimes the outcome, were inscribed alongside.

Discovery

Oracle bones had been sold for years as "dragon bones" for traditional medicine before the scholar-official Wang Yirong recognised inscriptions on them in 1899. The source was traced to Xiaotun village near Anyang, and systematic excavations began in 1928 under the Academia Sinica, directed initially by Li Ji and later Dong Zuobin, who established the dating framework of the inscriptions.

The script

About 4,500 distinct graphs are known, of which perhaps a third have been confidently deciphered. The script is already a mature writing system with a largely logographic character inventory including pictographs, ideographs and phono-semantic compounds. Its characters are direct ancestors of the Chinese characters still in use, and oracle bone inscriptions provide the earliest substantial corpus of early Chinese language and Shang history.
