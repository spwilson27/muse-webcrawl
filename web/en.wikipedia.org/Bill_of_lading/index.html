Bill of lading

A bill of lading (sometimes abbreviated as B/L or BoL) is a document issued by a carrier (or their agent) to acknowledge receipt of cargo for shipment. Although the term is historically related only to carriage by sea, a bill of lading may be used for any type of carriage of goods.

Functions

A bill of lading serves three main functions: it is a receipt for the goods shipped; it is evidence of the contract of carriage; and, where it is a negotiable ("order") bill, it is a document of title to the goods, so that the holder can claim delivery and can transfer the goods by endorsing and delivering the bill while the goods are in transit. These features make the bill of lading central to international trade and trade finance, including letters of credit.

Types

Bills may be "straight" (non-negotiable, consigned to a named party) or "order" bills (negotiable). A "clean" bill states the goods were received in apparent good condition; a "claused" or "dirty" bill notes defects. Electronic bills of lading have gained legal recognition in several jurisdictions in recent years.

History

The document's origins lie in medieval Mediterranean sea trade; its rules are now governed by international conventions including the Hague-Visby Rules and, in domestic trades, national carriage of goods legislation.
