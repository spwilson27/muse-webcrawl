Penal Laws

The Penal Laws (Irish: Na Péindlíthe) were a series of laws imposed in Ireland during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries to restrict the political, religious, educational, and economic rights of Roman Catholics and, to a lesser degree, Protestant dissenters such as Presbyterians.

Provisions

Enacted mainly between 1695 and 1728 after the Williamite victory, the laws barred Catholics from voting and sitting in Parliament, holding public office or military commissions, bearing arms, owning a horse worth more than £5, buying or inheriting land freely, and educating their children as Catholics at home or abroad. Catholic clergy were required to register, bishops were banished, and Catholic land passed under the rule of "gavelkind" subdivision while converts to the established Church of Ireland could inherit whole estates — provisions that steadily reduced Catholic landownership to a small fraction of the country.

Repeal

The laws were gradually dismantled by relief acts from 1778 onward; Catholics regained the vote in 1793, and Catholic emancipation in 1829, won under Daniel O'Connell's campaign, removed most remaining disabilities. The system left a lasting legacy of sectarian division in Irish political life.

Other usages

The term is also applied to the anti-Catholic penal statutes of England and Scotland during the Reformation era.
