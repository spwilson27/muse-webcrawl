Andrea del Verrocchio

Andrea del Verrocchio (born Andrea di Michele di Francesco de' Cioni, c. 1435 – 1488) was an Italian sculptor, painter, and goldsmith of the Florentine Renaissance, master of one of the most influential workshops of the 15th century. His nickname Verrocchio ("true eye") came from his first master, the goldsmith Giuliano Verrocchi. Though a leading sculptor in bronze — author of the equestrian monument to Bartolomeo Colleoni in Venice and the David now in the Bargello — he is today most famous as the teacher of Leonardo da Vinci, who trained in his workshop alongside Perugino, Ghirlandaio, and the young Botticelli's circle.

Life and workshop

Andrea was the son of a tile and brick maker who later became a tax collector. Apprenticed as a goldsmith, he probably trained in sculpture with Donatello's successors and by the mid-1460s was receiving Medici commissions. His Florence workshop in the Via de' Agnolo became a centre of technical innovation, executing tombs, fountains, armour, festival decorations, paintings, and monumental bronzes for patrons including the Medici, the Venetian Republic, and the city of Pistoia.

Major works

  • The Baptism of Christ (c. 1472–75, Uffizi) — painted largely by Verrocchio with his young assistant Leonardo, who painted the left-hand angel and parts of the landscape; Vasari's famous story that Verrocchio gave up painting after seeing his pupil's angel is almost certainly legend but reflects Leonardo's early brilliance.
  • David (c. 1473–75, Bargello) — a wiry, confident bronze youth, contrasted with Donatello's earlier David; tradition holds the young Leonardo modelled for it.
  • Christ and St. Thomas (1467–83) for Orsanmichele, and the Putto with Dolphin for the Palazzo Vecchio fountain.
  • Equestrian Monument to Bartolomeo Colleoni (Campo Santi Giovanni e Paolo, Venice) — modelled from 1480 and cast after his death by Alessandro Leopardi; rivalled only by Donatello's Gattamelata as the great Renaissance equestrian statue.
  • The silver relief altar frontal and the tomb of Piero and Giovanni de' Medici in San Lorenzo (1472).

Legacy

Verrocchio died in Venice in 1488 while supervising the Colleoni monument. Through his pupils — above all Leonardo — his workshop's fusion of goldsmith precision, anatomical study, and experimental technique shaped the High Renaissance. Lorenzo de' Medici's collection of his works, and the Medici tomb, anchor his reputation in Florence.
