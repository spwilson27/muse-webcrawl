Blue plaque

A blue plaque is a permanent sign installed in a public place in the United Kingdom, and certain other locations, to commemorate a link between that location and a famous person, event, or former building on the site, serving as a historical marker. The term is used in the United Kingdom in two senses: narrowly, for the official London scheme administered by English Heritage, and broadly, for the many similar schemes elsewhere in the country and around the world.

History

The scheme was proposed in 1863 by the MP William Ewart and taken up by the Society of Arts (later the Royal Society of Arts) in 1866, making it the oldest such scheme in the world. The first plaque was unveiled in 1867 to commemorate the poet Lord Byron at his birthplace, 24 Holles Street, Cavendish Square—a building later demolished, so that plaque no longer survives. The earliest surviving Society of Arts plaque, also erected in 1867, marks the lodging of Napoleon III in King Street, St James's.

The London County Council took over the scheme in 1901 and standardised the design; its successor, the Greater London Council, continued it until 1986, when English Heritage assumed responsibility. The familiar blue glazed ceramic design, made by Doulton, was standardised in the 1920s–30s.

Criteria

Under the English Heritage scheme, a person generally must have been dead for at least 20 years or have passed the centenary of their birth, must be regarded as eminent, and must have lived or worked in London at the building in question for a significant period. Buildings housing several plaques are rare, though some London addresses carry more than one.

Other schemes

Many cities, towns, civic societies, and local authorities run plaque schemes, not all of them blue: plaques in Oxford are of various colours, and elsewhere green, black, and maroon plaques exist. Similar commemorative plaque schemes exist in other countries, including Australia, Canada, France (Paris has its own marble plaques), Ireland, the Philippines, Russia, and the United States.

Notable plaques

Plaques in London commemorate figures as diverse as Charles Dickens, Ada Lovelace, Alan Turing, Freddie Mercury, Jimi Hendrix (whose plaque marks the flat at 23 Brook Street where he lived, adjacent to a plaque for George Frideric Handel), and the fictional-sounding but real plaque to the inventor of the Christmas cracker, Tom Smith. Public nominations are invited, and unveiling ceremonies are frequently attended by descendants and notable guests.
