The Classical period was an era of classical music between roughly 1750 and 1820. It followed the Baroque period and preceded the Romantic period, and it is associated above all with Joseph Haydn, Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, Ludwig van Beethoven and Franz Schubert. Classical style is characterised by clarity, balance, elegant homophonic textures, well-defined musical forms such as sonata form, and a new emphasis on public concerts and published music for a growing middle-class audience.

Characteristics

Compared with the dense polyphony of the Baroque, Classical music favours a single clear melody over a subordinate accompaniment (homophony), often using the Alberti bass pattern. Phrasing is regular and symmetrical, typically in two-, four- or eight-bar units. Dynamics became more flexible, with gradual crescendos and diminuendos replacing the terraced dynamics of the Baroque. The piano replaced the harpsichord as the principal keyboard instrument because it could respond to touch with varied volume.

Forms and genres

The era codified the principal instrumental forms still used today. The symphony grew into a standard four-movement cycle; the string quartet — perfected by Haydn, the "father of the string quartet" — became the emblematic chamber genre; and the solo concerto and piano sonata flourished. Sonata form (exposition, development, recapitulation) organised the first movements of symphonies, quartets and sonatas. Opera continued to thrive, with Mozart's Italian operas and the reform operas of Gluck reshaping the genre.

Historical development

The period began with the galant style and the empfindsamer Stil of C. P. E. Bach, reacting against Baroque complexity. The Mannheim school developed orchestral discipline and dynamic effects. The mature Viennese Classical style of Haydn and Mozart (sometimes called the First Viennese School) dominated from the 1780s. Beethoven, Haydn's pupil, expanded form, harmony and expressive scope so dramatically that his later works are often seen as ushering in Romanticism, while Schubert's songs and chamber music bridge the two eras.

Social context

Music-making shifted from courts and churches towards public concert halls and domestic music-making, aided by music publishing. Composers increasingly worked as freelancers rather than servants of aristocratic patrons — a transition embodied by Mozart's and Beethoven's careers in Vienna.
