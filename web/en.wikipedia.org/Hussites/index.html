Hussites

Hussites
Czech: Husité
Founder inspiration: Jan Hus (executed 1415)
Active: 1419–c. 1620 (as movement)
Key events: Hussite Wars (1419–1434), Compactata of Basel (1436)
Major factions: Utraquists, Taborites, Orebites

The Hussites were a Czech proto-Protestant Christian movement that followed the teachings of reformer Jan Hus, who was burned at the stake in 1415 at the Council of Constance. Centred in the Kingdom of Bohemia, the movement combined religious reform — above all communion under both kinds (bread and wine) for the laity — with Czech national sentiment and resistance to German and Catholic imperial authority.

Origins

Hus's preaching at Prague's Bethlehem Chapel built on earlier reform traditions and the writings of John Wycliffe. After his execution and that of Jerome of Prague, outrage in Bohemia grew. The First Defenestration of Prague in 1419, when a mob threw Catholic councillors from the town hall windows, ignited open revolt.

Hussite Wars

Five papal crusades against the Hussites were defeated between 1420 and 1431. Under the one-eyed general Jan Žižka and later Prokop the Great, Hussite armies pioneered the wagenburg — circles of armoured war wagons mounting early firearms and crossbows — and won battles such as Vítkov, Kutná Hora and Domažlice. Internal divisions split the movement between moderate Utraquists (Calixtines), who sought agreement with the Church, and radical Taborites, who pursued communal and egalitarian reforms.

Settlement and legacy

The Council of Basel negotiated the Compactata (1436), legalising communion under both kinds in Bohemia — a unique concession before the Reformation. Radical forces were crushed at Lipany in 1434 by a Utraquist–Catholic alliance. Bohemia later elected the Hussite king George of Poděbrady (1458). Hussite traditions fed into the Unity of the Brethren (Moravian Church) and influenced Luther, who acknowledged Hus as a forerunner.
