Subliminal channel

In cryptography, a subliminal channel is a covert communication channel that is created within an otherwise secure and authenticated channel — most famously within a digital signature scheme. The concept was introduced by Gustavus Simmons in the 1980s, partly motivated by the "prisoners' problem," in which two parties must communicate under the eye of a warden who permits only authenticated messages.

Mechanism

Many signature schemes, such as the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA) and ElGamal signatures, require a random or pseudorandom value for each signature. A signer who controls that randomness can bias it so that the resulting signature encodes a hidden message, recoverable by a receiver who shares a secret key. To an outside observer the signatures appear ordinary and valid.

Broadband and narrowband channels

Simmons distinguished broadband subliminal channels, which can carry a full message (for example, leaking an entire private key over a few signatures), from narrowband channels carrying only a few bits per signature. Broadband channels are especially dangerous because a malicious implementation of a signature standard could leak the signer's secret key undetectably; this risk was a major argument in debates over the design of DSA.

Countermeasures

Defenses include deterministic signature schemes (in which the ephemeral value is derived from the message and key, as in EdDSA and deterministic ECDSA under RFC 6979), zero-knowledge "subliminal-free" protocols designed by Simmons and refined by others, and trusted hardware or warden participation in generating the randomness.
