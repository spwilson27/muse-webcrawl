Game design

Game design is the process of creating the content and rules of a game — its goals, challenges, systems and the experience they produce for players. It applies to video games, board games, card games, role-playing games and sports, and is distinct from (though overlapping with) game programming, art, and writing. A game designer specifies mechanics and tunes them through playtesting so that the intended experience emerges from play.

Core concepts

Designers analyse games as systems of mechanics (rules and components), dynamics (the behaviour arising when mechanics meet player input) and aesthetics (the emotional responses produced) — the MDA framework of Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek (2004). Other common tools include the core gameplay loop, flow theory (matching challenge to skill, after Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi), meaningful choice, feedback loops that stabilise or escalate play, and difficulty balancing. Prototyping — first on paper, then in code — and iterative playtesting are the discipline's central working methods.

Branches

Specialisations include systems design (combat, economies, progression), level design (spaces and encounters), narrative design (story integrated with play), user-experience design, and, for live-service games, monetisation and event design. Video game development documents these decisions in a game design document, although agile studios increasingly treat designs as living artefacts.

History

Although games are ancient, game design as a named profession grew with the commercial video game industry in the 1980s, when credits began to single out designers such as Shigeru Miyamoto and Sid Meier. Academic study followed with journals, conferences, and textbooks: Salen and Zimmerman's Rules of Play, Raph Koster's A Theory of Fun for Game Design, and Jesse Schell's The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses are standard references.
