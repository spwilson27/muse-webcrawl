Programme for International Student Assessment

The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) is a worldwide study by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) in member and non-member countries intended to evaluate educational systems by measuring 15-year-old school pupils' scholastic performance on mathematics, science, and reading. It was first performed in 2000 and has been repeated every three years since (with the 2021 cycle postponed to 2022 because of the COVID-19 pandemic). PISA aims to provide comparable data to enable countries to improve their education policies and outcomes.

Design

PISA tests a sample of 15-year-olds in each participating country — roughly 600,000 students representing about 32 million pupils in the 2022 cycle, which covered 81 countries and economies. The assessments emphasise the application of knowledge to real-life problems rather than memorisation of curriculum content. Each cycle focuses in depth on one major domain — reading (2000, 2009, 2018), mathematics (2003, 2012, 2022), or science (2006, 2015) — while testing all three, and later cycles have added domains such as creative thinking, financial literacy, and collaborative problem solving. Background questionnaires gather data on students' home and school environments.

Results and influence

Results are published as country rankings and scores scaled so the OECD average is about 500 points. East Asian systems such as Singapore, China (participating regions), Japan, and South Korea, together with Estonia, Canada, and Finland, have frequently placed at or near the top. PISA results attract intense media and policy attention — Germany's poor showing in 2000 produced a "PISA shock" that prompted national education reforms — and are widely used, and sometimes criticised, as a benchmark for education policy.

Criticism

Academics have questioned aspects of PISA's methodology, including its statistical scaling model, cultural bias in test items, the treatment of countries with low school-enrolment rates among 15-year-olds, and the narrowing effect of league-table comparisons on diverse national education goals. The OECD defends the study's technical quality and transparency.
