Stephen D. Krashen (born 1941) is an American linguist and educational researcher, professor emeritus at the University of Southern California. He is best known for his theory of second-language acquisition, developed in the 1970s and 1980s, which has had a wide influence on language teaching.

The monitor model and input hypothesis

Krashen's theory comprises five hypotheses. The acquisition–learning hypothesis distinguishes subconscious "acquisition" of language through meaningful exposure from conscious "learning" of rules. The input hypothesis holds that learners progress when they receive comprehensible input slightly beyond their current level, denoted "i + 1". The monitor hypothesis assigns consciously learned knowledge only an editing role. The natural order hypothesis proposes that grammatical structures are acquired in a predictable sequence, and the affective filter hypothesis holds that anxiety and low motivation raise a "filter" blocking input from being used.

Later work and reception

Krashen has been a prominent advocate of free voluntary reading, arguing that extensive self-selected reading is the most powerful means of developing literacy and vocabulary, and a critic of high-stakes testing and of California's Proposition 227 restrictions on bilingual education. His hypotheses have been highly influential in communicative language teaching but have also been criticized by other researchers as difficult to test empirically, notably by Kevin Gregg and Barry McLaughlin.
