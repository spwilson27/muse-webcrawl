Representativeness heuristic

Representativeness heuristic
FieldCognitive psychology, behavioural economics
Proposed byAmos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman (1973)
DefinitionJudging probability by similarity to a prototype
Associated biasesBase-rate neglect, conjunction fallacy, gambler's fallacy

The representativeness heuristic is a mental shortcut used when judging the probability that an object or event belongs to a category: people assess how similar the case is to a prototype or stereotype of the category, largely ignoring prior probabilities (base rates) and sample sizes. Introduced by psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in the 1970s, it is one of the classic heuristics — alongside availability and anchoring — in the heuristics-and-biases research programme.

Effects

Base-rate neglect: when given a description of a person that resembles an engineer, people judge it likely the person is an engineer even when told engineers are rare in the sample. Insensitivity to sample size: people expect even small samples to mirror the population, underpinning the gambler's fallacy — expecting a coin's results to "even out". Insensitivity to predictability and the illusion of validity lead to overconfident forecasts from flimsy evidence.

The conjunction fallacy

The most famous demonstration is the "Linda problem" (1983): participants read about Linda, a single, outspoken philosophy graduate concerned with social justice, and most judged it more likely that she is "a bank teller and a feminist" than simply "a bank teller" — violating the rule that a conjunction cannot be more probable than one of its parts. Because the combined description is more representative of Linda's portrait, it feels more probable.

Interpretation

Tversky and Kahneman argued that representativeness is usually a useful and efficient shortcut, but its systematic errors reveal the structure of intuitive judgement. Later researchers debated whether the errors reflect irrationality or reasonable conversational interpretation, and evolutionary psychologists proposed that frequency-based presentations reduce the biases. The heuristic remains central to behavioural economics, where it helps explain overreaction in financial markets and stereotyping in social judgement.
