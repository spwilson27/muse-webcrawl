Barbaresco is an Italian wine made with the Nebbiolo grape in the Langhe hills of Piedmont, around the villages of Barbaresco, Neive and Treiso northeast of Alba. Together with Barolo, produced a short distance to the southwest from the same grape, it is considered one of Italy's greatest red wines — Barolo is nicknamed "the king of wines", while Barbaresco is often styled its "queen", a reference to its generally more elegant and approachable character.

History

Although Nebbiolo had long been grown in the area, the dry, structured Barbaresco style dates from the late 19th century, when Domizio Cavazza, director of the Royal School of Viticulture at Alba, founded the Cantina Sociale of Barbaresco (1894) and promoted the wine under its village name. The appellation was formalised as a DOC in 1966 and elevated to DOCG — Italy's highest classification — in 1980. The producer Angelo Gaja was instrumental from the 1960s onward in raising Barbaresco's international profile, pioneering single-vineyard bottlings such as Sorì San Lorenzo and temperature-controlled fermentation.

Production rules and style

Barbaresco DOCG must be made entirely from Nebbiolo grown within the delimited zone. Regulations require a minimum ageing of 26 months (including at least 9 months in wood) before release, and 50 months for the Riserva designation. The zone's proximity to the Tanaro river gives a slightly warmer, drier microclimate than Barolo, so the wines often mature a little earlier; typical Barbaresco is garnet in colour, full-bodied and tannic when young, with characteristic aromas of red cherries, dried roses, violets, liquorice, tar and white truffle, softening with age.

Crus

In 2007–2010 the appellation officially delimited additional geographical mentions (comparable to crus), including renowned slopes such as Asili, Rabajà, Ovello, Montestefano and Montefico in Barbaresco commune, and Albesani, Gallina and Pajoré in Neive and Treiso.
