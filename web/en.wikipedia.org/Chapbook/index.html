Chapbook


A chapbook, cheap popular literature sold by chapmen in early modern Europe.

A chapbook is a type of small printed booklet, a form of popular literature printed cheaply in early modern Europe and widely distributed by travelling pedlars known as chapmen. Chapbooks typically consisted of a single sheet folded into 8, 12, 16 or 24 pages, stitched rather than bound, and often illustrated with crude woodcuts. They sold for a penny or less and carried ballads, folktales, religious tracts, almanacs, jest books and abridged romances to a largely rural, semi-literate readership.

History

Chapbooks appeared in England in the late 16th century and flourished in the 17th and 18th centuries, when printers in London, Newcastle, Edinburgh and provincial towns produced them in enormous numbers. Comparable cheap print existed across Europe: the French bibliothèque bleue, Spanish pliegos sueltos and German Volksbücher.

Contents

Favourite titles included tales of Robin Hood, Tom Thumb, Dick Whittington, Guy of Warwick and Jack the Giant Killer, together with lives of saints and criminals, prophecies, dream books and practical manuals. For many readers chapbooks were the principal secular reading matter available and a key channel by which older medieval romances survived into popular culture.

Decline and modern usage

The chapbook trade declined in the 19th century as cheap newspapers, improving literacy and mass-market publishing displaced it. In modern usage the term has been revived for short, hand-made or small-press poetry collections, usually of fewer than forty pages.
