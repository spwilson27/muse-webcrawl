The Second Crusade (1147–1150) was the second major crusade launched from Europe. It was called in response to the fall of the County of Edessa in December 1144 to the forces of Imad ad-Din Zengi. Edessa was the first of the Crusader states to have been founded during the First Crusade (1096–1099), and it was the first to fall. Pope Eugene III issued the bull Quantum praedecessores calling for the crusade, and it was preached across Europe by Bernard of Clairvaux. For the first time kings led crusading armies: Louis VII of France and Conrad III of Germany.

The march to the East

The German army was largely destroyed by the Seljuk Turks at the Battle of Dorylaeum in October 1147. The French suffered heavy losses crossing Anatolia — notably at Mount Cadmus in January 1148 — and Louis abandoned much of his infantry at Attalia to sail to Antioch. Conrad, having regrouped and travelled by sea, arrived by ship. Contemporary critics, including Bernard himself (who blamed the crusaders' sins), wrestled with the crusade's failure.

Successes in Iberia and failure at Damascus

The crusade's clear successes came in Iberia, a recognised secondary front: in October 1147 an Anglo-Flemish-German crusader fleet helped King Afonso I of Portugal capture Lisbon from the Moors; Almería fell to a Genoese-Castilian force the same year, and Tortosa in 1148. In the Levant, however, the assembled crusader and Jerusalemite forces decided in council at Acre (June 1148) to attack Damascus — a decision historians still debate, since Damascus was then a useful counterweight to the rising power of Nur al-Din. The Siege of Damascus (24–28 July 1148) collapsed within four days when the besiegers moved to a poorly watered position on the city's east side and then withdrew amid mutual recrimination and rumours of bribery. The crusade dissolved in failure in the East, damaging the prestige of crusading and of Bernard and Louis VII, and leaving the Crusader states to face the growing unity of Muslim Syria under Nur al-Din.

Wendish Crusade

Simultaneously, northern German and Danish lords prosecuted the Wendish Crusade (1147) against the pagan Slavs east of the Elbe, authorised by Bernard — with mixed results, but it began the German eastward expansion associated with Henry the Lion and the later Northern Crusades.
