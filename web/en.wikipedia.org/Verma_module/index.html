Verma module

In representation theory, a Verma module is a fundamental construction associated with a Lie algebra, named after Daya-Nand Verma, who introduced the concept in his 1966 PhD thesis. Verma modules are the "largest" highest weight modules: every other module with the same highest weight is a quotient of the corresponding Verma module, and their study underlies the classification of representations of semisimple Lie algebras.

Construction

Let g be a semisimple Lie algebra with a Borel subalgebra b = h ⊕ n, where h is a Cartan subalgebra. For a weight λ, one forms a one-dimensional representation of b on which h acts by λ and n acts trivially. The Verma module M(λ) is the induced module obtained by tensoring this one-dimensional space with the universal enveloping algebra of g over that of b. It has a weight decomposition, and the multiplicity of a weight is given by the Kostant partition function.

Properties

Each Verma module has a unique maximal proper submodule, so its quotient is an irreducible highest weight module L(λ); every irreducible highest weight module arises this way. Whether M(λ) is itself irreducible, and the structure of its submodules, depends on the position of λ relative to the roots, as governed by the Harish-Chandra isomorphism and the action of the centre of the enveloping algebra. Characters of Verma modules feed into the Weyl character formula via the Bernstein–Gelfand–Gelfand (BGG) resolution, and the combinatorics of their submodule lattices is encoded in the Kazhdan–Lusztig conjectures (later theorems). Category O, introduced by Bernstein, Gelfand, and Gelfand, is the natural home for Verma modules.
