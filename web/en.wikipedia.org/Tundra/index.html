Tundra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physical geography, tundra (from Russian тундра, from the Sami word tūndâr, "uplands" or "treeless mountain tract") is a type of biome where the tree growth is hindered by low temperatures and short growing seasons. The term tundra comes through Russian from the Kildin Sami word for the uplands.

Types

There are three regions and associated types of tundra: Arctic tundra, alpine tundra, and Antarctic tundra. Tundra vegetation is composed of dwarf shrubs, sedges and grasses, mosses, and lichens. Scattered trees grow in some tundra regions. The ecotone (or ecological boundary region) between the tundra and the forest is known as the tree line or timberline.

Climate

Tundra climates have long, cold winters with mean temperatures below freezing for six to ten months, and short, cool summers. Most precipitation falls as snow. Beneath the surface lies permafrost — permanently frozen soil — which impedes drainage and creates vast wetlands of ponds and bogs in summer.

Flora and fauna

Characteristic animals include reindeer (caribou), musk oxen, Arctic hares, Arctic foxes, snowy owls, lemmings and, along the coast, polar bears. Migratory birds exploit the brief summer flush of insects. Plants are adapted to cold, wind and nutrient-poor soils with shallow roots, cushion growth forms and rapid flowering.

Carbon and climate change

Tundra soils store enormous quantities of carbon in frozen organic matter. Warming is thawing permafrost, releasing carbon dioxide and methane and potentially amplifying climate change — a feedback of major scientific concern.
