Helicase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Helicases are a class of enzymes that use energy from ATP hydrolysis to separate the two strands of a nucleic acid double helix, breaking the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases. They are essential motor proteins found in virtually all organisms — bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes, and many viruses — and are indispensable for DNA replication, DNA repair, recombination, transcription, and the processing of RNA.

Function

During DNA replication, helicase unwinds the parental duplex at the replication fork, producing single-stranded DNA templates for DNA polymerase. In eukaryotes this task is performed by the MCM2-7 complex, which forms the catalytic core of the CMG helicase (CDC45–MCM–GINS). RNA helicases, prominently the DEAD-box family, remodel RNA secondary structure and ribonucleoprotein complexes during translation initiation, splicing, and ribosome biogenesis.

Mechanism and classification

Helicases translocate along one strand of the duplex in a defined direction — either 3′→5′ or 5′→3′ — powered by ATP binding and hydrolysis in conserved sequence motifs (the Walker A and B boxes and related motifs). They are grouped into superfamilies SF1 through SF6 based on sequence and structure. Many form ring-shaped hexamers that encircle one strand and exclude the other, while others act as monomers or dimers that wedge the strands apart. Unwinding generates torsional stress ahead of the fork, which is relieved by topoisomerases.

Clinical relevance

Mutations in human helicase genes cause several genetic disorders: Werner syndrome (WRN helicase), Bloom syndrome (BLM), and Rothmund–Thomson syndrome (RECQL4) are all characterised by genomic instability and cancer predisposition, while defects in the XPB and XPD helicase subunits of TFIIH cause xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, and trichothiodystrophy. Helicases of viruses such as hepatitis C virus and SARS-CoV-2 have been investigated as antiviral drug targets.
