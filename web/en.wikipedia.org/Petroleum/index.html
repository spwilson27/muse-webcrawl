Petroleum

Petroleum, also known as crude oil, is a naturally occurring yellowish-black liquid mixture of mainly hydrocarbons found in geological formations. The name petroleum covers both crude oil and petroleum products refined from it. It is a fossil fuel formed when large quantities of dead organisms, mostly zooplankton and algae, are buried underneath sedimentary rock and subjected to prolonged heat and pressure.

Composition and classification

Crude oil is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons — chiefly alkanes, cycloalkanes and aromatic hydrocarbons — together with smaller amounts of compounds containing sulfur, nitrogen and oxygen, and trace metals. Oils are classified by density (API gravity) as light, medium, heavy or extra-heavy, and by sulfur content as "sweet" (low sulfur) or "sour". Benchmark crudes include Brent, West Texas Intermediate and Dubai/Oman.

Formation and reservoirs

Petroleum originates in organic-rich source rocks such as oil shale; with burial and heating, kerogen converts to oil and then gas (the "oil window"). The fluids migrate upward through permeable rock until trapped in reservoirs beneath impermeable caprock, in structures such as anticlines and fault traps, often together with natural gas and formation water.

History

Petroleum seeps were used since antiquity for waterproofing, mortar, medicine and warfare (including Greek fire). Hand-dug wells existed in China by the 4th century and in Baku by the medieval period. The modern industry dates from the 1850s: kerosene refining in Poland, Romania and Canada, and Edwin Drake's 1859 well at Titusville, Pennsylvania — the first successful well drilled specifically for oil. The internal combustion engine shifted demand decisively to gasoline in the 20th century.

Extraction and refining

Oil is produced by drilling wells, using primary recovery (natural pressure), secondary recovery (water or gas injection) and enhanced oil recovery (steam, CO₂ or chemicals). Unconventional resources include oil sands and tight oil produced by horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing. In refineries, crude is separated by fractional distillation and converted by cracking, reforming and treating into products: gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, fuel oils, liquefied petroleum gas, lubricants, asphalt and petrochemical feedstocks such as ethylene and propylene.

Economics and environment

World consumption is around 100 million barrels per day. The industry is dominated by national oil companies of OPEC members and large international firms; reserves are concentrated in Venezuela, Saudi Arabia, Canada, Iran and Iraq. Burning petroleum products is a major source of carbon dioxide emissions and air pollution, and spills cause severe local damage; these impacts drive the energy transition toward lower-carbon sources.
