Gruyère (French: [ɡʁɥijɛʁ]) is a hard Swiss cheese that originated in the district of Gruyères in the canton of Fribourg, Switzerland. It is a cooked, pressed cheese made from raw cow's milk, traditionally formed into large wheels weighing 25 to 40 kilograms (55 to 88 lb). Gruyère has been protected by an appellation d'origine protégée (AOP) in Switzerland since 2001, restricting the name to cheese made to specification in designated cantons.

Characteristics

Young Gruyère, aged about five months, is sweet, slightly salty, and nutty, with a dense, smooth paste that may contain a few small holes ("eyes"), though far fewer than Emmental. With longer aging — wheels are typically matured between five and twelve months, and reserve grades longer — the flavour deepens to earthy and complex with a firmer, slightly grainy texture as protein crystals develop. The natural rind is brown and brushed during maturation.

Production and history

Cheese has been produced in the Gruyères region since at least the twelfth century; records of cheese rents paid to the counts of Gruyères survive from 1249. Production follows strict rules: raw milk from cows fed on grass or hay (no silage), natural rennet, copper vats, and no additives beyond salt. Alpine Gruyère (Gruyère d'Alpage), made in mountain chalets during the summer grazing season, is a prized seasonal variant.

Culinary use

Gruyère is a classic melting cheese, essential to Swiss fondue (typically blended with Vacherin Fribourgeois), French onion soup, croque-monsieur, quiche Lorraine, and gratins. It is also eaten as a table cheese. A long-running dispute with French producers of a similarly named cheese was largely settled in European law, while in the United States a 2023 federal appeals court decision held that "gruyere" is a generic term there.
