Operon

An operon is a functioning unit of DNA containing a cluster of genes under the control of a single promoter. The genes are transcribed together into an mRNA strand and either are translated together in the cytoplasm, or undergo splicing to create monocistronic mRNAs that are translated separately. Operons are present mainly in prokaryotes but occur in some eukaryotes, including nematodes such as C. elegans.

History

The operon model was proposed in 1961 by François Jacob and Jacques Monod at the Pasteur Institute in Paris, based on their studies of lactose metabolism in Escherichia coli. Their work on the lac operon helped found modern molecular biology and earned them a share of the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Structure

A typical operon consists of a promoter, where RNA polymerase binds; an operator, where a repressor protein can bind to block transcription; and a set of structural genes encoding functionally related proteins. Regulatory genes producing activator or repressor proteins may lie elsewhere in the genome.

Regulation

Operons may be inducible, turned on by a small molecule such as a substrate metabolite, or repressible, turned off when a product accumulates. The lac operon is the classic inducible example, while the trp operon illustrates repression and attenuation. This economical arrangement lets bacteria coordinate the production of enzymes needed for a single pathway.
