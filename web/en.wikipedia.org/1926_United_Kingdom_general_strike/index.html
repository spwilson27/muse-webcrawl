1926 United Kingdom general strike

The 1926 general strike in the United Kingdom was a nationwide strike called by the Trades Union Congress (TUC) in support of coal miners who were locked out by mine owners seeking to cut wages and lengthen hours. It began on 3 May 1926 and lasted nine days, involving some 1.7 million workers, before the TUC called it off on 12 May without achieving its aims.

Background

Britain's coal industry was in decline after the First World War, struggling with lost export markets, the return to the gold standard in 1925, and competition from Germany, whose reparations coal undercut British prices. In 1925 the government subsidised miners' wages for nine months ("Red Friday"), but when the subsidy expired in April 1926 the owners demanded wage cuts of up to 13 percent and an eight-hour day. The miners' leader A. J. Cook rallied his members with the slogan "Not a penny off the pay, not a minute on the day."

The strike

Transport workers, printers, iron and steel workers and others struck in waves. The government of Stanley Baldwin had prepared through the Organisation for the Maintenance of Supplies, using volunteers, troops and special constables to run essential services and publish its own newspaper, the British Gazette. The TUC produced the British Worker. Remarkably, the strike remained largely peaceful, and strikers famously played football matches against police teams.

Outcome

Fearing legal exposure and loss of control, the TUC General Council ended the strike unconditionally on 12 May. The miners fought on alone until November, when starvation drove them back on the owners' terms. In 1927 the government passed the Trade Disputes and Trade Unions Act, which banned sympathetic and general strikes; it was repealed in 1946.
