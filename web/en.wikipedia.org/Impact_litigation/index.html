Impact litigation

Impact litigation or strategic litigation is a type of lawsuit in which the plaintiff seeks to enact social change. In many cases, the objective of the legal action is not only to secure relief for the individual plaintiff but to alter laws, policies, or practices affecting a broad class of people.

Impact litigation has been a significant tool in civil rights movements, particularly in the United States, where organizations such as the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund pursued cases designed to challenge segregation and discriminatory laws in a systematic, incremental fashion.

History

The modern impact litigation strategy in the United States is often traced to Charles Hamilton Houston and the NAACP's campaign against segregated education in the 1930s and 1940s. Rather than attacking the "separate but equal" doctrine of Plessy v. Ferguson directly, Houston and his protégé Thurgood Marshall brought a series of cases challenging inequalities in graduate and professional education, gradually establishing precedents that culminated in Brown v. Board of Education (1954).

Characteristics

  • The plaintiff is often selected because their case presents sympathetic facts and a clean vehicle for deciding a legal question.
  • The litigation is typically funded and directed by an organization pursuing a long-term legal agenda rather than by the individual plaintiff alone.
  • Test cases may be brought in favorable jurisdictions to build favorable precedent before more difficult cases are attempted.
  • Amicus curiae briefs from allied organizations frequently play a major role.

Criticism

Critics argue that impact litigation can be anti-democratic, shifting social policy decisions from legislatures to courts, and that it may privilege the priorities of funders and lawyers over those of the communities in whose name cases are brought. Supporters respond that courts are sometimes the only institution open to unpopular minorities and that litigation has been indispensable to protecting constitutional rights.
