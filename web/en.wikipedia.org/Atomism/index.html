Atomism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Atoms and the void

Atomism (from Greek atomos, meaning "uncuttable" or "indivisible") is a natural philosophy proposing that the physical universe is composed of fundamental indivisible components known as atoms. References to the concept of atomism and its atoms appeared in both ancient Greek and ancient Indian philosophical traditions.

Ancient Greek atomism

Leucippus (5th century BCE) is named as the earliest proponent of atomism, and his student Democritus developed the theory further. The atomists held that everything consists of atoms moving in empty space (the void), and that differences in shape, arrangement, and position of atoms account for the apparent diversity of the material world. Epicurus later adopted and modified atomism, adding the idea of the atomic "swerve" (clinamen) to account for free will; Lucretius expounded this version in his poem De rerum natura.

Indian atomism

In India, atomistic ideas appear in the Vaisheshika school of Kanada, which posited indivisible particles (paramanu) combining into dyads and triads, as well as in Jain and Buddhist traditions.

Decline and revival

Atomism was eclipsed during the Middle Ages, partly through the dominance of Aristotelian natural philosophy, but was revived in the 17th century by thinkers such as Pierre Gassendi and incorporated into the mechanical philosophy of Boyle and Newton. In the 19th century, John Dalton gave atomism a quantitative scientific form in chemistry, leading eventually to the modern atomic theory.
