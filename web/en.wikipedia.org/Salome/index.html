Salome

Salome has been a frequent subject in art, literature and music.

Salome was a Jewish princess, the daughter of Herod II and Herodias, and stepdaughter of Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee. According to the Gospels of Matthew and Mark, she danced before Herod Antipas at his birthday feast and, at her mother's prompting, asked for the head of John the Baptist on a platter as her reward.

Biblical account

The Gospels do not name the dancer; the historian Josephus identifies her as Salome in his Antiquities of the Jews, where he also records her later marriages, first to her uncle Philip the Tetrarch and then to Aristobulus of Chalcis, with whom she had three sons. Josephus does not connect her with the death of John the Baptist.

In art and culture

The story became a rich subject for Renaissance and Baroque painters, including Titian, Caravaggio and Guido Reni. In the 19th century the figure was reimagined as a femme fatale in works by Gustave Moreau and in Oscar Wilde's play Salomé (1891), written in French, which Richard Strauss adapted into his celebrated and once-scandalous opera Salome (1905). The "Dance of the Seven Veils" derives from Wilde's version rather than the biblical text.

Historical identity

Coins issued by Aristobulus of Chalcis bear the image of Salome, providing rare contemporary evidence for her life. Scholars generally accept the Gospel episode as having a historical core, though details such as the dance and the request are debated.
