Logocentrism

Logocentrism is a term coined by the German philosopher Ludwig Klages in the 1920s and later taken up by Jacques Derrida to describe what he saw as the central bias of Western philosophy: the privileging of speech (logos) over writing, on the assumption that spoken words are closer to an originally present truth or meaning.

Derrida's critique

In Of Grammatology (1967), Derrida argued that from Plato — who in the Phaedrus treats writing as a fallen copy of living speech — through Rousseau, Saussure and Lévi-Strauss, Western thought has treated writing as a mere supplement to speech. Derrida's deconstruction inverted the hierarchy, arguing that the features supposedly unique to writing — absence of the author, iterability, spacing — in fact characterise all language, including speech. He linked logocentrism to "phonocentrism" and to a "metaphysics of presence".

Reception

The concept became a cornerstone of post-structuralism and literary theory, though critics have questioned whether Derrida's readings fairly represent the philosophers he discusses.
