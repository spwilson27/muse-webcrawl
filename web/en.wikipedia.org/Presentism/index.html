Presentism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

FieldPhilosophy of time / metaphysics
ThesisOnly present things exist
Contrasts withEternalism; growing block theory

Presentism is a theory in the philosophy of time holding that only present entities exist: the past and the future are not real in the way the present is. It contrasts with eternalism, according to which past, present and future entities all exist equally, and with the growing block theory, in which the past and present exist but the future does not.

Arguments

Presentists argue that presentism matches common-sense experience and avoids commitments to a vast ontology of non-existent things. A common presentist claim is that statements about the past are made true by the present state of the world and its records, rather than by existing past objects.

Objections

Critics raise several problems. The truthmaker problem asks what makes claims about the past true if the past does not exist. Cross-temporal relations—such as admiring Socrates—seem to require both relata to exist. Most famously, Einstein's theory of relativity is often taken to undermine the idea of an absolute, universe-wide present, because simultaneity is relative to a frame of reference; presentists have responded with absolute simultaneity, privileging a reference frame, or relativising existence.

History

Ideas resembling presentism appear in Augustine of Hippo, who located past and future in the present mind's memory and expectation. In the 20th and 21st centuries the theory has been defended by philosophers such as A. N. Prior, the founder of tense logic.

Other uses of the term

"Presentism" is also used in historiography for the anachronistic judging of the past by present-day values, a usage distinct from the metaphysical theory.
