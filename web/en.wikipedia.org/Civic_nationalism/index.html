Civic nationalism

Civic nationalism
Also called: Liberal nationalism (in some usages)
Key concept: Nation defined by shared citizenship, values, and political institutions
Contrasted with: Ethnic nationalism

Civic nationalism is a form of nationalism in which the nation is defined by shared citizenship, common political values, and allegiance to political institutions, rather than by shared ethnicity, descent, language, or culture. In the civic conception, membership in the nation is in principle open to anyone who accepts the political community's laws and values, regardless of their ancestry or place of birth.

Origins and ideas

The idea draws on Enlightenment thought and on the traditions of the French and American revolutions, in which the nation was conceived as a voluntary political association — Ernest Renan famously described the nation in 1882 as a "daily plebiscite". Hans Kohn's classic distinction between "Western" (civic) and "Eastern" (ethnic) nationalism framed France, Britain, and the United States as civic nations and the nationalisms of Central and Eastern Europe as ethnic. Later scholars, including Yael Tamir and Michael Ignatieff, elaborated and criticized the dichotomy.

Criticism

Critics argue that no nationalism is purely civic: even ostensibly civic nations typically privilege a particular language and cultural tradition, and civic criteria can themselves be used exclusionarily. Scholars also note that civic and ethnic elements coexist in most real-world nationalisms, and that the civic–ethnic distinction, though influential in political science, is often more normative than descriptive.
