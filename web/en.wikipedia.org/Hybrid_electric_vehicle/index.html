Hybrid electric vehicle

Hybrid electric vehicle (HEV)
A vehicle using two or more distinct types of power, typically an internal combustion engine and an electric motor with a battery.

A hybrid electric vehicle (HEV) is a type of hybrid vehicle that combines a conventional internal combustion engine (ICE) with an electric propulsion system. The presence of the electric powertrain is intended to achieve either better fuel economy than a conventional vehicle, or better performance.

How hybrids work

HEVs recover energy normally lost as heat during braking (regenerative braking) and store it in a battery. The electric motor can assist the engine during acceleration, allow the engine to shut off at idle, and in "full hybrid" designs propel the vehicle on electric power alone at low speed. Architectures include parallel hybrids (both power sources drive the wheels), series hybrids (the engine only generates electricity), and power-split designs such as Toyota's Hybrid Synergy Drive.

History and market

Hybrid cars date to the Lohner–Porsche Mixte of 1900, but the modern era began with the Toyota Prius (Japan 1997, worldwide 2000) and the Honda Insight. The Prius became the best-selling hybrid nameplate, and hybrid technology spread across nearly every manufacturer. Plug-in hybrids (PHEVs) carry larger batteries that can be charged from the grid, offering tens of kilometres of electric-only range, while mild hybrids use a small motor-generator that cannot drive the car alone.

Benefits and criticism

Hybrids emit less CO2 and fewer local pollutants in city driving than comparable petrol cars, but they retain a combustion engine and tailpipe emissions, leading some regulators to treat them as a transitional technology on the path to battery-electric vehicles such as the electric hypercars and mainstream EVs.
