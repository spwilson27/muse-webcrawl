National Cancer Act of 1971

The National Cancer Act of 1971 is a United States federal law, signed by President Richard Nixon on 23 December 1971, that dramatically expanded the authority and resources of the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and launched what Nixon called the "war on cancer."

Background

Philanthropist Mary Lasker and her network of advocates campaigned for a national crusade against cancer, the nation's second-leading cause of death. A Senate panel chaired by Benno Schmidt recommended an independent, well-funded cancer programme modelled partly on the scale of the Apollo programme.

Provisions

The Act strengthened the NCI, made its director a presidential appointee, created the National Cancer Advisory Board and the President's Cancer Panel, authorised a national cancer research programme with designated comprehensive cancer centres, and appropriated $1.5 billion over three years. The NCI received a unique "bypass budget" submitted directly to the President.

Legacy

Although cancer was not "conquered" on the optimistic timetable some envisioned, the Act built the modern US cancer research infrastructure. US cancer death rates peaked in 1991 and have since declined substantially, reflecting prevention (especially reduced smoking), screening and improved treatments that emerged from the research system the Act created.
