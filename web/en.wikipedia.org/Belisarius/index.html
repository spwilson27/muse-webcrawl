Flavius Belisarius (Greek: Βελισάριος; c. 500 – March 565) was a military commander of the Byzantine Empire under the emperor Justinian I. One of the great generals of late antiquity, he led the imperial armies that reconquered much of the western Mediterranean for Constantinople, defeating the Persians in the east, destroying the Vandal kingdom of North Africa, and overrunning Ostrogothic Italy. The historian Procopius, who served on his staff, is the principal source for his career.

Early career and the Persian war

Belisarius rose in the bodyguard of the emperor Justin and caught the attention of Justin's nephew and successor Justinian. Appointed to command in Mesopotamia, he won a notable victory over the Sasanian Persians at the Battle of Dara in 530, but was defeated at Callinicum in 531. In 532 he helped suppress the Nika riots in Constantinople, in which some 30,000 people were killed.

Conquest of the Vandals

In 533 Justinian entrusted Belisarius with an expedition against the Vandal kingdom in North Africa: some 15,000–17,000 men in about 500 ships. Belisarius won decisive victories at Ad Decimum (September 533) and Tricamarum (December 533), captured the Vandal king Gelimer, and annexed the kingdom within a year — an astonishingly cheap reconquest. He celebrated a triumph in Constantinople in 534, the last granted to a private citizen in Roman history.

The Gothic War in Italy

In 535 Belisarius invaded Ostrogothic Italy, taking Sicily, Naples, and Rome. He held Rome against a year-long siege by the Gothic king Vitiges (537–538) and captured Ravenna in 540, taking King Vitiges prisoner. Recalled to fight Persia, he returned to Italy in 544 to face the resurgent Gothic king Totila, but with inadequate forces could not prevent the loss of Rome twice over; he was recalled in 549.

Final years

In 559, aged nearly sixty, Belisarius saved Constantinople from a Kutrigur Bulgar attack with a scratch force of veterans and civilians, his last campaign. In 562 he was tried on a trumped-up conspiracy charge and imprisoned, but was pardoned by Justinian. He died in March 565, a few months before the emperor. The later medieval legend that Justinian had him blinded and reduced to begging — popularised in art and in Marmontel's novel Bélisaire — has no basis in the sources.

Assessment

Belisarius achieved his victories habitually outnumbered, relying on disciplined combined-arms forces centred on heavily armed cavalry archers (bucellarii), careful logistics, and improvisation. He is often called the "Last of the Romans," and is counted among the most capable commanders of the early medieval world, though his reconquests imposed ruinous costs on Italy and were only partially durable.
