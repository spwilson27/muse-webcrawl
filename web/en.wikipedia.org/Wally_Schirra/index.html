Walter Marty Schirra Jr. (March 12, 1923 – May 3, 2007) was an American naval aviator, test pilot, and NASA astronaut. In 1959, he became one of the original seven astronauts chosen for Project Mercury, America's first human spaceflight program. Schirra is best remembered as the only astronaut to fly on all three of NASA's first human spaceflight programs: Mercury, Gemini, and Apollo.

Early life and military career

Schirra was born in Hackensack, New Jersey, into a family with a flying tradition; his father had been a pilot in the First World War and performed stunts at county fairs, and his mother accompanied him as a wing walker. Schirra graduated from the United States Naval Academy in 1945 and served briefly at the end of World War II. He became a naval aviator and flew combat missions during the Korean War, exchanging with the U.S. Air Force and flying F-84 jets, for which he was credited with downing one or two enemy aircraft and received the Distinguished Flying Cross. He later attended the U.S. Naval Test Pilot School and worked as a test pilot at Naval Air Station Patuxent River, participating in the development of weapons systems including the Sidewinder missile.

NASA career

Selected as one of the Mercury Seven in April 1959, Schirra piloted the third American orbital flight, Mercury-Atlas 8, on October 3, 1962. His capsule, Sigma 7, completed six orbits in about nine hours and splashed down remarkably close to the recovery ship, a precision performance that earned him a reputation for technical exactness.

In December 1965, Schirra commanded Gemini 6A alongside pilot Thomas P. Stafford. After their original mission was scrubbed when the target Agena vehicle failed, the flight was re-cast as a rendezvous mission; Gemini 6A achieved the first true rendezvous in space with Gemini 7. Earlier, Schirra had famously refused to pull the ejection handles after a pad abort of the first Gemini 6 launch attempt, correctly judging that the Titan booster had not lifted off—a decision that saved the mission and possibly the crew.

Apollo 7

Schirra's final flight was as commander of Apollo 7, launched on October 11, 1968, with Donn Eisele and Walter Cunningham. It was the first crewed Apollo mission, testing the Command and Service Module in Earth orbit for 11 days. The mission was a technical success, but Schirra, suffering from a severe head cold, clashed with mission control over schedule changes and television broadcasts. The tensions contributed to none of the Apollo 7 crew flying in space again, though they received NASA awards for the mission's achievements.

Later life

Schirra retired from NASA and the Navy, with the rank of captain, in 1969. He co-founded an environmental technology company, worked in business and finance, and became a familiar television commentator, joining Walter Cronkite in covering later Apollo flights. Known for his practical jokes—his "Gotcha" pranks were legendary among astronauts—he co-authored the memoir Schirra's Space (1988). Schirra was inducted into the U.S. Astronaut Hall of Fame in 1990. He died of a heart attack in La Jolla, California, on May 3, 2007, while battling abdominal cancer.
