Sophocles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sophocles (c. 497/6 – winter 406/5 BC) was an Athenian playwright and one of the three great tragedians of classical Athens, alongside Aeschylus and Euripides. He wrote more than 120 plays and won at least 18 victories at the Dionysia festival, never placing below second. Seven of his tragedies survive complete, including Oedipus Rex, Oedipus at Colonus, and Antigone.

Life

Sophocles was born at Colonus, just outside Athens, to a wealthy family. Besides writing, he served Athens as a general (strategos) — including during the Samian War — as a financial official, and on the board that managed the city after the Sicilian disaster of 413 BC. His first victory came in 468 BC, defeating the veteran Aeschylus.

Innovations and works

Aristotle credited Sophocles with adding the third actor and introducing scene painting, allowing more complex plots and character interaction. In the Poetics, Aristotle held up Oedipus Rex as the model tragedy for its plot of reversal (peripeteia) and recognition (anagnorisis). Antigone, pitting divine law against human decree, remains one of the most performed and philosophically discussed Greek plays. Freud later drew the term "Oedipus complex" from the Oedipus plays.
