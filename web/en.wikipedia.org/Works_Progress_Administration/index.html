The Works Progress Administration (renamed in 1939 the Work Projects Administration; WPA) was an American New Deal agency that employed millions of jobseekers to carry out public works projects, including the construction of public buildings and roads. It was established on May 6, 1935, by presidential order, as a key part of the Second New Deal.

A WPA road-building crew in the 1930s.

Programs

Directed by Harry Hopkins, the WPA employed roughly 8.5 million people over its existence. Its construction division built highways, streets, bridges, schools, courthouses, and airports. Its arts initiatives, collectively known as Federal Project Number One, included the Federal Art Project, Federal Music Project, Federal Theatre Project, and Federal Writers' Project, which produced the American Guide Series of state guidebooks and recorded interviews with formerly enslaved people.

Criticism and end

Critics charged the WPA with inefficiency and political patronage, while supporters credited it with useful infrastructure and relief. Congress terminated funding in 1943 as wartime employment reduced unemployment.

Legacy

The WPA left a large physical legacy of public works still in use and remains a touchstone in debates over government employment programs during recessions. See also the Civilian Conservation Corps and the New Deal.
