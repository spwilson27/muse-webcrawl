The plural (sometimes abbreviated as pl. and sometimes called the plural number), in many languages, is one of the values of the grammatical category of number. The plural of a noun typically denotes a quantity greater than the default quantity represented by that noun; the default quantity is usually one (a form said to represent this default quantity is the singular). Plural forms most commonly refer to two or more of something, although they may also denote fractional, negative, or zero quantities.

Formation

Languages mark plurality in varied ways: by suffix (English cat/cats, box/boxes), by internal change or ablaut (man/men, goose/geese), by borrowed patterns (criterion/criteria, octopus/octopuses), by reduplication (as in Indonesian), or by plural words and classifiers. Some nouns are uncountable or have no plural (information), while others exist only in the plural (pluralia tantum, such as scissors or trousers). English plurals show sound alternations conditioned by the final segment of the stem: /s/, /z/, or /ɪz/.

Number systems

Many languages distinguish more than singular and plural: the dual (exactly two) survives in Slovene and Sorbian and in classical Arabic and Sanskrit; some languages have a trial or a paucal (a few). Others, such as Japanese and Chinese, generally do not mark plural on nouns at all, relying on numerals, classifiers, and context. Agreement in verbs, adjectives, and pronouns often reflects number alongside person and gender.

Agreement and usage

In English, plural subjects take plural verbs ("the dogs are"), while collective nouns can vary by dialect ("the team is/are"). Zero and fractions between minus one and one commonly take plural nouns in English ("0.5 kilograms," "zero points"), whereas some languages (e.g. French) use the singular below two.
