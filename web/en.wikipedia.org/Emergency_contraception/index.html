Emergency contraception

Emergency contraception (EC) is a form of contraception used after unprotected sexual intercourse or contraceptive failure to prevent pregnancy. It includes emergency contraceptive pills — sometimes called the "morning-after pill" — containing levonorgestrel or ulipristal acetate, and the insertion of a copper intrauterine device (IUD), which is the most effective form.

Mechanism and effectiveness

Emergency contraceptive pills work primarily by delaying or inhibiting ovulation; they do not terminate an established pregnancy. Levonorgestrel is most effective when taken within 72 hours of intercourse, while ulipristal acetate remains effective up to 120 hours. A copper IUD inserted within five days is more than 99% effective and provides ongoing contraception.

Access

Availability varies by country: levonorgestrel pills are sold over the counter in many jurisdictions, while ulipristal may require a prescription. Emergency contraception is recommended by bodies such as the World Health Organization as a backup method, not a substitute for regular contraception.
