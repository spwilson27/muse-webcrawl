Technocracy

Technocracy is a form of government in which the decision-makers are selected on the basis of their expertise in a given area of responsibility, particularly scientific or technical knowledge. In a technocracy, decision-makers are chosen for their specialized knowledge and performance rather than through political affiliation or popularity.

The term was coined in 1919 by the American engineer William Henry Smyth. It gained prominence in the 1930s through the Technocracy movement in North America, associated with Howard Scott and the organization Technocracy Inc., which proposed replacing the price system with an energy-based accounting system during the Great Depression.

Definition and characteristics

Technocratic systems emphasize rational, scientific problem-solving and the management of society by experts. Supporters argue that technical specialists are better equipped than politicians to handle complex economic and technological questions. Critics contend that technocracy can be anti-democratic, since experts are not accountable to voters, and that many political questions involve values that expertise alone cannot settle.

Technocracy movement

The North American technocracy movement arose in the early 1930s amid the Great Depression. Figures such as Thorstein Veblen had earlier suggested rule by engineers, and Howard Scott's Technical Alliance conducted an "energy survey" of North America. Technocracy Inc. proposed a continental administrative unit called a Technate and an accounting system based on energy certificates instead of money. The movement attracted widespread attention briefly but declined after the mid-1930s.

Modern usage

In contemporary usage, "technocrat" often describes officials appointed for expertise rather than party loyalty, such as central bankers or the caretaker governments occasionally formed in European countries during crises. The term is also used in debates about governance in China and Singapore, where meritocratic selection plays a prominent role.
