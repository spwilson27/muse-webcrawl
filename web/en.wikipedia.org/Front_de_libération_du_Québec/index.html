The Front de libération du Québec (FLQ; "Quebec Liberation Front") was a militant Quebec separatist group. Founded in the early 1960s during Quebec's Quiet Revolution, it aimed at the complete independence of the province of Quebec from Canada and the establishment of a French-speaking socialist state. Between 1963 and 1970, the FLQ committed over 160 violent incidents, including bombings, bank hold-ups and kidnappings, which culminated in the October Crisis of 1970.

History

The FLQ emerged in 1963, inspired by decolonisation movements in Algeria and Cuba and by dissatisfaction with the economic position of French Canadians. Its early cells carried out bombing campaigns against federal targets, mailboxes, military installations and symbols of English-Canadian and American commercial power, including the Montreal Stock Exchange bombing in 1969, which injured 27 people.

October Crisis

In October 1970, FLQ cells kidnapped British trade commissioner James Cross and Quebec labour minister Pierre Laporte. Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau invoked the War Measures Act — the only peacetime use in Canadian history — deploying troops to Quebec and arresting hundreds. Laporte was found murdered on 17 October; Cross was released in December in exchange for his kidnappers' safe passage to Cuba. The crisis effectively destroyed the FLQ; surviving members were imprisoned, exiled or drifted into peaceful separatist politics, and the movement dissolved by 1971.

Legacy

The FLQ remains controversial: condemned as terrorist by the Canadian state, it is viewed by some Quebec nationalists as a misguided product of its era. Its violent path was largely repudiated in favour of democratic separatism through the Parti Québécois, founded in 1968, which later held independence referendums in 1980 and 1995.
