Atlantic Intracoastal Waterway


The Intracoastal Waterway in North Carolina

The Atlantic Intracoastal Waterway is a 3,000-mile (4,800 km) inland waterway along the Atlantic coast of the United States, running from Boston, Massachusetts, southward along the Atlantic seaboard and around the southern tip of Florida to the Gulf of Mexico. It consists of natural inlets, saltwater rivers, bays and sounds connected by artificial canals, providing a navigable route inside the coast that avoids the hazards of the open ocean.

Route

The most continuously maintained portion runs from the Chesapeake Bay area through North Carolina's sounds, the lowcountry of South Carolina and Georgia, and down the Florida coast to Miami and beyond. Major man-made cuts include the Chesapeake and Delaware Canal, the Albemarle and Chesapeake Canal, the Dismal Swamp Canal — one of the oldest surviving artificial waterways in the United States — and long canal sections in Florida. Together with the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway, it forms a sheltered route along much of the eastern and Gulf coasts.

Use and maintenance

The waterway is maintained by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and is toll-free. It carries commercial barge traffic — petroleum products, chemicals, building materials and farm goods — but is equally famous as a cruising route for recreational boaters, many of whom travel it seasonally in the "Great Loop" or in the autumn migration of "snowbird" yachts to Florida. Authorised depth is about 12 feet (3.7 m) for much of the route, though shoaling frequently reduces depths in places.

History

An inland route was envisioned as early as the 19th century for defence and commerce. Federal construction proceeded piecemeal, and the Atlantic Intracoastal Waterway was substantially completed by 1940, proving strategically valuable during the Second World War, when it allowed shipping to avoid U-boats operating off the coast.
