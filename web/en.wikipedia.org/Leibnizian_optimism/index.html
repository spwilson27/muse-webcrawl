Leibnizian optimism

Leibnizian optimism is the philosophical position associated with the German philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716), according to which the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. Leibniz developed the doctrine most fully in his Essays of Theodicy on the Goodness of God, the Freedom of Man and the Origin of Evil (1710), the only book-length work published in his lifetime.

The argument

Leibniz held that God, being omniscient, omnipotent and perfectly good, surveys an infinity of possible worlds in his understanding and freely chooses to actualise the one that is best — the world combining the greatest richness and variety of phenomena with the simplest and most harmonious laws. Because God necessarily acts for the best, the existence of evil is compatible with divine goodness: evils may be permitted as conditions of greater goods, or as unavoidable consequences of the best overall plan.

Theodicy and the problem of evil

The Theodicy was Leibniz's answer to the problem of evil, prompted in part by the sceptical writings of Pierre Bayle. Leibniz distinguished metaphysical evil (imperfection), physical evil (suffering) and moral evil (sin), arguing that a world containing some evil may still be better than any world without it. The doctrine rests on his principle of sufficient reason: nothing happens without a reason why it is so rather than otherwise.

Criticism and legacy

The most famous attack on Leibnizian optimism is Voltaire's satirical novella Candide (1759), whose character Dr Pangloss parodies the doctrine with the refrain that "all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds". The 1755 Lisbon earthquake, which killed tens of thousands, had already shaken confidence in optimistic theodicy across Europe. Later philosophers, including Arthur Schopenhauer — who inverted the claim, calling this the worst of all possible worlds — continued to debate the position. Leibniz's related doctrines of monads, pre-established harmony and the identity of indiscernibles remain central to the study of early modern rationalism.
