The Parliament of England was the legislature of the Kingdom of England from the 13th century until 1707. Evolving out of the great councils of bishops and peers summoned by medieval kings, it developed into a bicameral body comprising the House of Lords (spiritual and temporal peers) and the elected House of Commons (knights of the shire and burgesses). Under the Acts of Union 1707 it was merged with the Parliament of Scotland to form the Parliament of Great Britain.

Origins and medieval development

King Henry III's reign saw the first assemblies recognisably called "parliaments". In 1265 Simon de Montfort summoned knights and townsmen as well as magnates; Edward I's "Model Parliament" of 1295 regularised the inclusion of commons' representatives. Parliament's consent to taxation, confirmed repeatedly in statutes and charters, gave it a central role in authorising revenue, hearing petitions and enacting legislation. By the 14th century the Commons had gained the initiative in money bills and the power of impeachment.

Tudor and Stuart eras

Under the Tudors Parliament's statute-making authority was used to carry through the English Reformation, and its prestige grew even as monarchs managed it carefully. Under the early Stuarts, disputes over taxation, religion and royal prerogative led to the Petition of Right (1628), the Long Parliament, and ultimately the English Civil War, the trial and execution of Charles I in 1649, and the Commonwealth. The Restoration of 1660 restored the traditional constitution, but conflict resumed under James II, ending in the Glorious Revolution of 1688.

1689 settlement to Union

The Bill of Rights 1689 settled key principles: free elections, frequent parliaments, freedom of speech within Parliament, and no taxation or standing army without parliamentary consent. The Triennial Act and later Septennial Act regulated elections, while the financial revolution bound government to annual parliamentary supply. In 1707, following the Treaty of Union, the English and Scottish parliaments were replaced by a single Parliament of Great Britain sitting at Westminster.
