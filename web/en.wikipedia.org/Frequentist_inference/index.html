Frequentist inference

Frequentist inference is a type of statistical inference based on the idea of frequency probability: the probability of an event is interpreted as the limit of its relative frequency in a large number of trials. Inference is drawn from sample data by emphasizing the frequency or proportion of the data under hypothetical repetitions of an experiment. Frequentist inference underlies the most widely used frameworks of hypothesis testing and interval estimation, and contrasts with Bayesian inference, which incorporates prior probability distributions.

Philosophy

In the frequentist framework, unknown parameters are treated as fixed (but unknown) quantities, not as random variables, so it is not meaningful to assign probabilities to them. Instead, probability attaches to the procedure: a good estimator or test is one that behaves well over hypothetical repetitions. Ronald Fisher expressed this ideal by insisting that a statistical procedure be judged by its performance "in the long run."

Hypothesis testing

The dominant testing framework, developed by Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson, poses a null hypothesis H0 against an alternative H1 and controls two error rates: the probability of a Type I error (false rejection, denoted α, the significance level) and of a Type II error (β; its complement 1 − β is the power). The p-value, central to Fisher's earlier approach, is the probability, computed under H0, of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed.

Confidence intervals

A 95% confidence interval is produced by a procedure that, in 95% of repeated applications, generates an interval containing the true parameter value. The interval either contains the parameter or it does not; the 95% refers to the long-run coverage of the method, not to a probability that the parameter lies in the realized interval — a common point of misinterpretation.

History

Frequentist ideas trace to Jakob Bernoulli and to the work of Laplace and Gauss on errors. The modern framework crystallized in the 1920s–1930s through Fisher's Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925) and the Neyman–Pearson lemma (1933). Their approaches were later hybridized in textbook practice, a synthesis both men would partly have disowned. Karl Pearson and his son Egon also contributed key machinery, including the chi-squared test.

Criticisms

Critics note that frequentist procedures can depend on the sampling plan and on data that were not observed (the stopping-rule and likelihood-principle debates), that p-values are routinely misread as probabilities that the null is true, and that dichotomous "significant / not significant" reporting encourages poor science. Bayesian methods are offered as a coherent alternative, at the cost of specifying prior distributions.
