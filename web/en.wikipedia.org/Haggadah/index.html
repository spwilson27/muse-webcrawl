Haggadah

Haggadah (Hebrew: הַגָּדָה, "telling"; plural: Haggadot) is the Jewish text that sets forth the order of the Passover Seder.

The Haggadah (Hebrew: הַגָּדָה, meaning "telling" or "narrative"; plural Haggadot) is a Jewish text that sets forth the order of the Passover Seder. According to Jewish practice, reading the Haggadah at the Seder table is a fulfillment of the mitzvah to each Jew to tell their children the story from the Book of Exodus about God bringing the Israelites out of slavery in Egypt, with a strong hand and an outstretched arm. It is also the fulfillment of the commandment "And thou shalt tell thy son in that day, saying: It is because of that which the LORD did for me when I came forth out of Egypt" (Exodus 13:8).

History

The Haggadah emerged during the era of the Mishnah and Talmud, with its core structure established by the time of the Babylonian Talmud. The text was compiled over centuries, incorporating biblical passages, rabbinic commentary (Midrash), prayers, hymns, and folk songs. The oldest complete Haggadah manuscripts date to the 10th century. The Haggadah was a popular subject for illuminated manuscripts during the Middle Ages, most famously the Sarajevo Haggadah (c. 1350 in Barcelona) and the Golden Haggadah. With the advent of printing, the first printed Haggadah appeared in Guadalajara, Spain in 1482, and the Prague edition of 1526 set a model for many illustrated editions.

Contents

The Seder order, as laid out in the Haggadah, follows fourteen ritual steps, including:

  • Kadesh — the recitation of Kiddush over the first cup of wine
  • Urchatz — the first washing of hands
  • Karpas — dipping a green vegetable in salt water
  • Yachatz — breaking the middle matzah
  • Maggid — the telling of the story, including the Four Questions (Mah Nishtanah), the Four Sons, and the recital of the Ten Plagues
  • Rachtzah — the second, formal washing of hands before the meal
  • Motzi Matzah — the blessing over the matzah
  • Maror — eating the bitter herb
  • Korech — eating a sandwich of matzah and bitter herb, in memory of Hillel
  • Shulchan Orech — the festive meal
  • Tzafun — eating the afikoman
  • Barech — the grace after meals
  • Hallel — recitation of Psalms of praise
  • Nirtzah — the conclusion, including songs such as "Chad Gadya" and "Echad Mi Yodea"

The Four Questions

Traditionally asked by the youngest child present, the Four Questions ("Mah Nishtanah" — "Why is this night different from all other nights?") prompt the telling of the Exodus story. They ask why matzah is eaten instead of leavened bread, why bitter herbs are eaten, why food is dipped twice, and why participants recline while eating.
