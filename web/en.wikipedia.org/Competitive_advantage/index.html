Competitive advantage

FieldStrategic management
Popularized byMichael Porter (1980, 1985)
Generic strategiesCost leadership, differentiation, focus
Key testVRIO: valuable, rare, inimitable, organized

In business, a competitive advantage is an attribute that allows an organization to outperform its competitors — to generate greater sales, margins, or customer retention than rival firms. An advantage may arise from lower costs, superior products, strong brands, proprietary technology, privileged access to resources or distribution, or organizational capabilities that are difficult to imitate. For an advantage to be sustainable, it must be protected by barriers to imitation such as patents, switching costs, network effects, or causal ambiguity.

Two classic routes to competitive advantage

Porter's generic strategies

Michael E. Porter's Competitive Strategy (1980) and Competitive Advantage (1985) framed strategy as choosing between cost leadership (being the lowest-cost producer), differentiation (offering unique value for which buyers pay a premium), and focus (serving a narrow segment with either approach). Porter warned that firms attempting to be all things to all customers risk being "stuck in the middle." His value chain framework analyzed how each activity in a firm contributes to cost or differentiation.

The resource-based view

An influential alternative, the resource-based view of the firm, locates advantage inside the firm rather than in industry structure. In Jay Barney's formulation (1991), resources yield sustained advantage when they are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable, and non-substitutable (later organized as the VRIO framework). Related concepts include core competencies (Prahalad and Hamel) and dynamic capabilities — the capacity to reconfigure resources as markets change.

Temporary advantage

In fast-moving markets, scholars such as Rita Gunther McGrath argue that advantages are increasingly transient, requiring firms to continually launch new strategic initiatives and disengage from exhausted ones. Empirical research also cautions that most advantages erode over time as competitors imitate or leapfrog the leader.
