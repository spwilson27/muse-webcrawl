Overview

The Charter of the United Nations (UN Charter) is the foundational treaty of the United Nations, an intergovernmental organisation. It establishes the purposes, governing structure and overall framework of the UN system, including its six principal organs: the General Assembly, the Security Council, the Economic and Social Council, the Trusteeship Council, the International Court of Justice and the Secretariat.

History

The Charter was drafted at the United Nations Conference on International Organization, held in San Francisco from April to June 1945, building on proposals agreed at Dumbarton Oaks and Yalta. It was signed on 26 June 1945 by representatives of 50 countries and entered into force on 24 October 1945, after ratification by the five permanent members of the Security Council — China, France, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom and the United States — and a majority of the other signatories. The date is commemorated annually as United Nations Day.

Contents

The Charter consists of a preamble and 111 articles grouped into 19 chapters. Its preamble begins "We the peoples of the United Nations determined to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war…". Article 2(1) proclaims the sovereign equality of member states; Article 2(4) prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state; Article 2(7) bars intervention in domestic affairs except under enforcement measures authorised by the Security Council under Chapter VII. Under Article 103, obligations under the Charter prevail over those of any other international agreement.

Amendments

The Charter has been amended three times, in 1963, 1965 and 1971, chiefly to enlarge the Security Council from 11 to 15 members and the Economic and Social Council from 18 to 54 members.
