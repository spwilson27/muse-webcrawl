Coping (psychology)

In psychology, coping consists of conscious or unconscious strategies used to reduce or tolerate unpleasant emotions, stress and conflict. Coping responses are partly controlled by personality traits and social context, and partly by the nature of the stressor itself.

Types of coping

Researchers commonly distinguish problem-focused coping, which aims to change the stressful situation itself, from emotion-focused coping, which aims to regulate the emotional response to it. A third category, avoidant coping, involves evading the stressor, and is often associated with poorer outcomes when used chronically. Meaning-focused coping, in which a person draws on beliefs and values to sustain well-being, has also been described.

Appraisal

The transactional model of Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman holds that coping depends on appraisal: a primary appraisal of whether an event is a threat, and a secondary appraisal of the resources available to deal with it. Coping is therefore a dynamic process rather than a fixed trait.

Health effects

Adaptive coping strategies — seeking social support, planning, positive reappraisal — are associated with better mental and physical health, whereas persistent avoidance and rumination are linked to anxiety and depression.
