The Eleusinian Mysteries (Greek: Ἐλευσίνια Μυστήρια) were initiations held every year for the cult of Demeter and Persephone based at the Panhellenic Sanctuary of Eleusis in ancient Greece. They are considered the most famous of the secret religious rites of ancient Greece, and formed the basis of an agrarian cult with roots in the Mycenaean period. The rites, ceremonies and beliefs were kept secret and consistently preserved for nearly two thousand years.

The Mysteries dramatised the myth of Persephone's abduction by Hades and her mother's search for her: Demeter's grief, the withering of the earth, and the partial return of Persephone, which promised initiates a happier fate in the afterlife. Initiation was open to all Greek speakers — men, women and even slaves — who had not shed blood, an unusually inclusive rule for the ancient world.

The rites

The Lesser Mysteries were held in Athens in spring as a preliminary purification. The Greater Mysteries took place in the month of Boedromion (roughly September–October): initiates walked the Sacred Way from Athens to Eleusis in procession, fasted, drank the barley-and-pennyroyal drink called kykeon, and entered the great hall, the Telesterion, where sacred objects were revealed. Revealing the secrets was punishable by death, so their exact content remains unknown; famous initiates included Plato, Sophocles and Cicero.

End

The rites survived into Roman times under imperial patronage, but were suppressed in AD 392 under Theodosius I's decrees against pagan cults, and the sanctuary was destroyed by Alaric's Goths in 396.
