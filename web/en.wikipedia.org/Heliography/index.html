Heliography

Heliography (from Greek helios, "sun", and graphein, "to write") is the photographic process invented by French inventor Nicéphore Niépce in the 1820s. Using a plate coated with bitumen of Judea, which hardens where struck by light, Niépce produced the earliest surviving photograph made with a camera, View from the Window at Le Gras (c. 1826–1827). The term also covers his earlier experiments in reproducing engravings by contact exposure.

The process

Niépce dissolved bitumen of Judea, a naturally occurring asphalt, in lavender oil and coated the mixture thinly on a polished pewter plate (or glass for his early copies of engravings). After an exposure in a camera obscura lasting many hours — traditionally said to be eight hours, though modern research suggests several days — the unhardened bitumen was washed away with lavender oil and turpentine, leaving a direct positive image in the hardened coating. The plate could then be etched with acid and inked for printing, which was Niépce's original goal of photomechanical reproduction.

History

Niépce made his first successful heliographic copy of an engraving in 1822 (a portrait of Pope Pius VII on glass, later destroyed). In 1826 or 1827, from an upstairs window of his estate at Saint-Loup-de-Varennes, he captured the view now recognised as the oldest surviving camera photograph; the plate was rediscovered by historian Helmut Gernsheim in 1952 and is held by the University of Texas at Austin. In 1829 Niépce entered a partnership with Louis Daguerre to refine the process; after Niépce's death in 1833, Daguerre developed the daguerreotype, announced in 1839, which supplanted heliography. A cousin, Abel Niépce de Saint-Victor, later adapted bitumen techniques.

Legacy

Though impractical — extremely light-insensitive and low in detail — heliography is recognised as the birth of photography, and bitumen processes remained in use for photogravure and printing plates long after. Niépce's Le Gras photograph and his correspondence with his brother Claude are preserved as foundational documents of the medium.
