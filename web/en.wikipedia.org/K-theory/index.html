K-theory

FieldAlgebraic topology, algebraic geometry
OriginsGrothendieck (1957); Atiyah–Hirzebruch
Key resultsBott periodicity; Atiyah–Singer index theorem
BranchesTopological, algebraic, operator K-theory

In mathematics, K-theory is, roughly speaking, the study of a class of invariants defined on spaces and rings, arising from vector bundles and projective modules. It began with Alexander Grothendieck's 1957 reformulation of the Riemann–Roch theorem, in which he defined the group K(X) from vector bundles on an algebraic variety, and grew into several related theories: topological K-theory, algebraic K-theory, and the K-theory of operator algebras.

Topological K-theory

For a compact Hausdorff space X, K⁰(X) is the Grothendieck group of complex vector bundles over X: formal differences of bundles modulo stable equivalence. Michael Atiyah and Friedrich Hirzebruch developed this into a generalized cohomology theory. Its most famous feature is Bott periodicity: Kⁿ⁺²(X) ≅ Kⁿ(X), discovered by Raoul Bott for the homotopy groups of the unitary group. Topological K-theory was a key tool in the proof of the Atiyah–Singer index theorem (1963), which relates the analytical index of an elliptic differential operator to topological data.

Algebraic K-theory

Algebraic K-theory assigns groups Kₙ(R) to a ring R. The low-dimensional groups have concrete meanings: K₀ captures projective modules and generalizes the ideal class group; K₁ relates to determinants and the abelianization of the general linear group; K₂, identified by John Milnor, involves Steinberg symbols. Daniel Quillen's higher K-theory (1972–73), built with the plus construction and the Q-construction, opened applications to number theory, including connections with special values of zeta functions.

Applications

Beyond pure mathematics, K-theory appears in condensed matter physics in the classification of topological insulators and superconductors (the "periodic table" of topological phases), and in string theory, where D-brane charges are classified by K-theory groups rather than ordinary cohomology.
