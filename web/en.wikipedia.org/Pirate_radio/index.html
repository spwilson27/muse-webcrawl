Pirate radio

Offshore pirate stations broadcast from ships in international waters

Pirate radio is radio broadcasting that operates without a licence or in defiance of broadcasting regulations. The term is most famously associated with the offshore stations of the 1960s that broadcast popular music to Britain and other European countries from ships anchored in international waters, beyond the reach of national regulators. Such transmissions are usually considered a form of civil disobedience or cultural protest, though they can interfere with licensed services and emergency frequencies.

The offshore era

The modern pirate radio era began in Europe in the late 1950s and early 1960s, when state broadcasters offered little popular music. Radio Veronica broadcast to the Netherlands from 1960, and in 1964 Radio Caroline began broadcasting to Britain from a ship off the Essex coast, followed by stations such as Radio London. These stations attracted audiences of millions, employed disc jockeys who became household names, and demonstrated a huge unmet demand for pop music radio.

The British government responded with the Marine, &c., Broadcasting (Offences) Act 1967, which made it illegal for British citizens to supply or work for offshore stations, silencing most of them. In their place, the BBC restructured its radio services, launching BBC Radio 1 in 1967 and hiring many former pirate DJs.

Land-based pirates

From the 1970s onward, unlicensed broadcasting moved ashore, particularly in large cities. In London, pirate stations broadcasting from tower-block rooftops became central to the development of genres such as jungle, UK garage and grime, giving a platform to artists ignored by mainstream radio; stations such as Deja Vu, Rinse FM and Kiss (which later became licensed) began as pirates. Similar scenes exist worldwide, from community micropower stations in the United States to clandestine political broadcasters. Enforcement agencies periodically raid studios and confiscate transmitters, but stations frequently return to the air within days.

The word "pirate" in this context borrows from the older sense of unlicensed maritime raiding described under piracy, reflecting broadcasts made from outside the law's jurisdiction.
