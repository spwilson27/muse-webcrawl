Television

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Television (TV) is a telecommunication medium for transmitting moving images and sound. The term can refer to a television set, the medium of television transmission, or television programming. Television is a mass medium for advertising, entertainment, news, and sports. The word derives from the Greek tele ("far") and the Latin visio ("sight").

History

Television developed from competing mechanical and electronic systems in the early 20th century. John Logie Baird demonstrated a mechanical television in London in 1926, while Philo Farnsworth transmitted the first fully electronic image in San Francisco in 1927; Vladimir Zworykin's iconoscope and kinescope were developed at RCA around the same period. Regular broadcasts began in the 1930s — the BBC opened the world's first regular high-definition service in 1936 — and expanded rapidly after the Second World War, first in the United States and then worldwide.

Colour television was standardised in the 1950s and 1960s (NTSC, PAL, and SECAM systems), cable and satellite distribution widened channel choice from the 1970s onward, and flat-panel displays replaced cathode-ray tubes in the 2000s. Digital terrestrial broadcasting, high-definition TV, and then internet-based streaming services progressively displaced scheduled linear viewing in the 2010s and 2020s.

Technology

A television system captures images with a camera, encodes them as an electrical signal, transmits the signal by radio waves, cable, satellite, or internet protocol, and displays it on a receiver. Display technologies have included the cathode-ray tube, plasma panels, liquid-crystal displays (LCD and LED-backlit), and organic light-emitting diodes (OLED). Resolution standards progressed from a few hundred lines to standard definition (480i/576i), high definition (720p/1080i/1080p), and ultra-high definition (4K and 8K).

Social impact

Television became the dominant medium of the second half of the 20th century, shaping politics, advertising, and popular culture. Events such as the Apollo 11 Moon landing in 1969 were watched live by hundreds of millions. Scholars continue to debate its effects on attention, children, and public discourse, while the rise of on-demand streaming has fragmented the once-shared broadcast audience.
