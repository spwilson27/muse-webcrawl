Opioid

Opioids are a class of drugs that derive from, or mimic, natural substances found in the opium poppy plant. They work on opioid receptors in the brain and nervous system to relieve pain, producing effects that range from analgesia and sedation to euphoria. The class includes the illicit drug heroin, synthetic opioids such as fentanyl, and prescription pain relievers including oxycodone, hydrocodone, codeine and morphine. The term is often used loosely to include opiates, the natural alkaloids of opium.

Medical use

Opioids are among the most effective analgesics for moderate to severe acute pain, cancer pain and palliative care, and feature on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines. Their use for chronic non-cancer pain is more controversial because of tolerance, dependence and limited long-term benefit. Common adverse effects include constipation, nausea, sedation, itching and respiratory depression.

Risks and overdose

Regular use can lead to tolerance, physical dependence and opioid use disorder. Overdose causes life-threatening respiratory depression and can be rapidly reversed with the antagonist naloxone. Since the 1990s an opioid epidemic — driven first by over-prescription, then by heroin, and then by illicitly manufactured fentanyl — has caused hundreds of thousands of deaths in the United States and significant harm in many other countries.

Pharmacology

Opioids act chiefly at the μ-opioid receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor whose activation reduces neurotransmitter release and neuronal excitability. The body also produces endogenous opioids — endorphins, enkephalins and dynorphins — which modulate pain, reward and stress. Antagonists such as naloxone and naltrexone, and partial agonists such as buprenorphine, are used in overdose reversal and addiction treatment, as is the agonist methadone.
