The Messerschmitt Me 262, nicknamed the Schwalbe ("Swallow") in fighter versions and Sturmvogel ("Storm Bird") in fighter-bomber versions, was the world's first operational jet-powered fighter aircraft. Designed by a team led by Wilhelm Messerschmitt at the request of the Reich Air Ministry, it was developed during the later years of the Second World War and entered service with the Luftwaffe in 1944.

Development

Work on a jet fighter began before the war. The Me 262 first flew in April 1941 powered by a piston engine in the nose because its BMW 003 turbojets were not ready. On 18 July 1942 it flew under jet power alone with Junkers Jumo 004 engines. Production was delayed by engine reliability problems, shortages of strategic materials, and political interference, including a decision to adapt the aircraft as a fast bomber.

Design

The aircraft had a sleek, shark-like fuselage with two underslung jet engines, a slightly swept wing, and tricycle landing gear. In fighter configuration it was armed with four 30 mm MK 108 cannons in the nose. Later variants carried air-to-air rockets and the two-seat night-fighter version mounted radar antennas on the nose.

Operational history

The Me 262 was much faster than any Allied fighter — roughly 200 km/h faster than the P-51 Mustang — and pilots claimed about 542 Allied aircraft destroyed. However, it entered service too late and in too small numbers to change the course of the war. Its temperamental engines, vulnerable airfields, and fuel shortages limited its impact. Allied pilots learned to attack it during take-off and landing, when it was slowest.

Influence

After the war, captured examples were studied intensively in the United States, the Soviet Union, and Britain, influencing early jet fighter design. The Czechoslovak-built Avia S-92 continued the type briefly. Several originals survive in museums, and modern reproductions powered by reliable General Electric engines fly with the Messerschmitt Foundation.
