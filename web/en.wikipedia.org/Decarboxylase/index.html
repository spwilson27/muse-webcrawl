Decarboxylase

Decarboxylases, also known as carboxy-lyases, are carbon–carbon lyase enzymes (EC 4.1.1) that add or remove a carboxyl group from organic compounds, typically releasing carbon dioxide. They catalyse decarboxylation reactions that are central to cellular metabolism, including steps of the citric acid cycle and the synthesis of neurotransmitters.

Classification

Decarboxylases are categorised in the EC number system as EC 4.1.1, the carbon–carbon lyases acting on carboxyl groups. Many require a cofactor — most often pyridoxal phosphate (the active form of vitamin B6), thiamine pyrophosphate, or a metal ion.

Examples

  • Pyruvate decarboxylase — converts pyruvate to acetaldehyde in alcoholic fermentation (in yeast and bacteria).
  • Isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex — oxidative decarboxylation steps of the citric acid cycle (technically oxidoreductases with decarboxylating activity).
  • Aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (DOPA decarboxylase) — converts L-DOPA to dopamine and 5-HTP to serotonin.
  • Glutamate decarboxylase — converts glutamate to the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA.
  • Histidine decarboxylase — produces histamine.
  • Ornithine decarboxylase — the rate-limiting step in polyamine synthesis, a target of the drug eflornithine.
  • RuBisCO can act reversibly in some contexts, though it is normally a carboxylase.

Clinical significance

Defects in decarboxylases cause human disease: aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase deficiency is a rare neurotransmitter disorder, and autoantibodies against glutamate decarboxylase (GAD65) are a hallmark of type 1 diabetes. Carbidopa, given with levodopa in Parkinson's disease, inhibits DOPA decarboxylase outside the brain.
