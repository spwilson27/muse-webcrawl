In Judaism and Christianity, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Hebrew: עֵץ הַדַּעַת טוֹב וָרָע, ʿêṣ haddaʿaṯ ṭôḇ wārāʿ) is one of two specific trees in the story of the Garden of Eden in Genesis 2–3, along with the tree of life. God places the tree in the garden and forbids Adam and Eve to eat its fruit, on pain of death. Tempted by the serpent, Eve eats and gives the fruit to Adam; their eyes are opened, they become aware of their nakedness, and they are expelled from Eden — the event known in Christian theology as the Fall of man.

Biblical narrative

Genesis 2 states that the tree stood "in the midst of the garden" and that God commanded the man: "of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die." In Genesis 3, the serpent tells the woman that eating will make them "like God, knowing good and evil." After the couple eat, God pronounces curses — pain in childbirth, toilsome labor, enmity between serpent and humanity — clothes them in skins, and banishes them "lest he reach out his hand and take also of the tree of life and eat, and live forever," stationing cherubim with a flaming sword to guard the way.

Interpretation

The phrase "good and evil" has been understood variously: as a merism denoting all knowledge; as moral discernment; and as a maturity or sexual knowledge. Augustine and later Christian theology read the disobedience as original sin, the inherited corruption of humanity. Jewish interpretation generally stresses human moral responsibility and the acquisition of the knowledge of good and evil as a defining, if costly, feature of humanity. The identity of the fruit is never specified in the Hebrew text; Western tradition popularized the apple (partly through a Latin pun on mālum, apple, and malum, evil), while other traditions propose the fig, grape, wheat, or citron. Scholars have drawn comparisons with Mesopotamian motifs, including the plant of life in the Epic of Gilgamesh.

In art and culture

The tree and the Fall are among the most depicted subjects in Western art, from medieval sculpture and the Sistine Chapel ceiling to Milton's Paradise Lost. The tree remains a resonant symbol of forbidden knowledge, curiosity, and the loss of innocence.
