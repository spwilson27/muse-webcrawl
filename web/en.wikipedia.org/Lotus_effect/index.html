Lotus effect

The lotus effect refers to the self-cleaning properties of the leaves of the sacred lotus (Nelumbo nucifera), which result from their ultrahydrophobic (extremely water-repellent) surface. Dirt particles are picked up by rolling water droplets and carried off the leaf, leaving it clean even though the plant grows in muddy water.

Mechanism

Scanning electron microscopy in the 1970s by botanists Wilhelm Barthlott and Christoph Neinhuis showed that the leaf surface is covered with microscopic wax papillae. This micro- and nano-scale roughness makes water form near-spherical droplets with contact angles above 150°, minimising the contact area so droplets roll off at the slightest tilt, collecting contaminants. Barthlott and Neinhuis coined the term and patented biomimetic applications.

Applications

The effect has inspired biomimetic products including self-cleaning paints (such as Lotusan), coatings for glass, textiles and solar panels, and superhydrophobic sprays. Maintaining durability of the delicate micro-structure remains a key engineering challenge.
