X.25

TypePacket-switched network protocol suite
DeveloperITU-T (CCITT)
Introduced1976
LayersPhysical, data link (LAPB), packet (PLP)
StatusLegacy; largely superseded by IP networks

An X.25 public data network linking DTEs through switching exchanges.

X.25 is an ITU-T standard protocol suite for packet-switched data communication in wide area networks. Originally approved in 1976, it defined how terminals and computers (data terminal equipment, DTE) connect over telephone networks to packet-switching exchanges, and it underpinned the first generation of public data networks in the 1970s and 1980s.

Architecture

X.25 specifies three layers. The physical layer uses interfaces such as X.21; the data link layer uses LAPB, a balanced variant of HDLC providing reliable, error-corrected links between the subscriber and the network; and the packet layer protocol (PLP) establishes virtual calls — switched or permanent virtual circuits — over which data packets travel with flow control and error recovery at every hop.

This "intelligent network" design fitted an era of noisy analogue lines and dumb terminals: the network guaranteed in-order, error-free delivery. Public packet networks built on X.25 included Telenet and Tymnet in the United States, Datapac in Canada, Transpac in France, and PSS in Britain. The French Minitel system ran over such infrastructure, and X.25 long persisted in banking (ATM and point-of-sale networks), airline reservations, and amateur radio (AX.25).

Decline

Per-hop reliability imposed latency and overhead that became wasteful as digital lines improved. The Internet protocol suite, which places intelligence at the endpoints, displaced X.25 during the 1990s, though legacy X.25 services survived in payment and aviation systems into the 2010s, and protocols such as Frame Relay and MPLS inherited its virtual-circuit ideas.
