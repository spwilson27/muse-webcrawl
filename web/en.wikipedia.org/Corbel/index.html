Corbel

In architecture, a corbel is a structural piece of stone, wood or metal jutting from a wall to carry a superincumbent weight, a type of bracket. A corbel is a solid piece of material in the wall, whereas a console is a piece applied to the structure. A piece of timber projecting in the same way was called a "tassel" or a "bragger" in England.

Technique

The technique of corbelling, where rows of corbels deeply keyed inside a wall support a projecting wall or parapet, has been used since Neolithic times. In medieval architecture corbels were frequently carved with figures, foliage, or grotesques, and rows of corbels under a parapet form a corbel table. Corbels were also used to support oriels, turrets, chimney breasts, and the springing of arches and vaults. A corbel arch is formed by offsetting successive courses of masonry until they meet at the top, as in early Irish and Maya construction; a corbel vault extends the same principle to roof a chamber.

Examples

Corbelled domes and beehive huts are found in Neolithic sites such as the clochán huts of Ireland; the galleries of Mycenaean tholos tombs use corbel vaulting on a grand scale. In Gothic and Romanesque churches corbels commonly carry the roof timbers and the ribs of stone vaults, and are often richly sculpted. In domestic and fortified architecture, corbelled parapets and bartizans are characteristic of Scottish Baronial and French medieval building.
