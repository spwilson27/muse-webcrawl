Montreal Protocol

Montreal Protocol
Full name: Montreal Protocol on Substances That Deplete the Ozone Layer
Signed: September 16, 1987, Montreal
Effective: January 1, 1989
Parties: 198 (universally ratified)
Administers: UNEP

The Montreal Protocol is an international treaty designed to protect the ozone layer by phasing out the production and consumption of numerous substances responsible for ozone depletion, principally chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and halons. Signed in 1987 under the Vienna Convention framework, it is often cited as the most successful environmental treaty in history—the first treaty to achieve universal ratification by all United Nations member states.


The Antarctic ozone hole, which the protocol aims to heal

Background

In 1974, Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland proposed that CFCs—used in refrigerators, aerosols, and foams—were destroying stratospheric ozone; in 1985 British Antarctic Survey scientists reported a dramatic seasonal "ozone hole" over Antarctica, galvanizing action. Negotiations under the United Nations Environment Programme produced the Vienna Convention (1985) and then the binding Montreal Protocol two years later.

Terms and implementation

The protocol set timetables for developed and developing countries to freeze and then eliminate ozone-depleting substances, and has been strengthened by successive adjustments and amendments (London 1990, Copenhagen 1992, Montreal 1997, Beijing 1999) that accelerated phase-outs and added substances such as methyl bromide and HCFCs. A Multilateral Fund, established in 1991, has disbursed billions of dollars to help developing countries comply. The Kigali Amendment (2016) extended the protocol to phase down hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)—ozone-safe but potent greenhouse gases—potentially avoiding up to 0.5 °C of warming by 2100.

Results

Production and consumption of controlled ozone-depleting substances have fallen by about 99 percent from peak levels, and atmospheric concentrations are declining; the ozone layer is projected to recover to 1980 levels around 2040 globally and about 2066 over Antarctica. The phase-out also delivered major climate benefits, since CFCs are powerful greenhouse gases. The treaty is widely invoked as a model for science-driven, adaptable international environmental governance.
