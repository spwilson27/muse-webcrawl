Sauger

The sauger (Sander canadensis) is a freshwater perciform fish of the family Percidae that resembles its close relative, the walleye. The species is a member of the largest order of vertebrates, the Perciformes. It is the most migratory percid species in North America. Saugers have two dorsal fins; the first is spiny and the posterior dorsal fin is soft-rayed. Their paired fins are in the thoracic position and their caudal fin is truncated, which means squared off at the corners, a characteristic of the family Percidae. Saugers have a fusiform body structure, and as percids, they are covered in ctenoid scales.

Distribution and habitat

Saugers are native to large rivers and lakes of North America, from the Great Lakes and Hudson Bay drainages south through the Mississippi River basin to Alabama and west to Montana and Wyoming. Unlike walleyes, saugers tolerate turbid, silt-laden water, owing in part to a light-reflecting layer in the eye (the tapetum lucidum) that aids vision in murky conditions. They favour swift currents, rocky substrates, and deep pools near dams and riffles.

Description

Saugers are generally smaller and slimmer than walleyes, typically 30 to 45 cm (12 to 18 in) long and weighing under 1 kg, though fish above 2 kg occur. They can be distinguished from walleyes by the dark spots scattered on the spiny dorsal fin, the absence of a white tip on the lower lobe of the tail, and their mottled, saddle-like blotching. Saugers hybridize naturally with walleyes, producing the fertile cross known as the saugeye, which is stocked in some reservoirs.

Diet and reproduction

Saugers feed chiefly on small fish such as gizzard shad, emerald shiners, and young drum, as well as aquatic insects and crustaceans. Spawning occurs in spring when water temperatures reach roughly 4–10 °C (40–50 °F); females broadcast adhesive eggs over gravel and cobble in flowing water, and adults may migrate long distances—hundreds of kilometres—to reach spawning grounds.

Angling

Saugers are a valued food fish, with firm white flesh comparable to walleye. They are taken on jigs and live-bait rigs, particularly in tailwaters below dams during winter and early spring. Many jurisdictions manage sauger fisheries with size and creel limits, and some populations have declined because of river fragmentation and habitat change.
