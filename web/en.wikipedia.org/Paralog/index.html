Paralog

In genetics, paralogs are genes that are related by duplication within a genome. When a gene is copied — through tandem duplication, segmental duplication, or whole-genome duplication — the two copies are paralogous to each other. Because only one copy generally remains under full selective constraint, paralogs often evolve new or subdivided functions, a process called neofunctionalization or subfunctionalization.

Orthologs and homologs

Paralogs contrast with orthologs, which are homologous genes in different species that diverged by speciation and usually retain the same function. Both paralogs and orthologs are types of homologs. The terms were introduced by Walter Fitch in 1970 to clarify evolutionary relationships among sequences. A further distinction separates in-paralogs (duplicated after a speciation event) from out-paralogs (duplicated before), which matters when inferring orthology from sequence similarity.

Examples

Classic paralog families include the globin genes (myoglobin and the alpha- and beta-hemoglobins), the Hox genes in animals, and the large families of olfactory receptor genes in vertebrates. In medicine, paralog relationships matter because drug targets and disease genes often have paralogs that can compensate, cause side effects, or serve as alternative targets.
