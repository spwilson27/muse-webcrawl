Glacier

A glacier is a persistent body of dense ice that is constantly moving under its own weight. It forms where the accumulation of snow exceeds ablation (melting and sublimation) over many years, often centuries, compacting into firn and then glacial ice. Glaciers cover about 10% of Earth's land surface and store roughly 70% of the world's fresh water.

Formation and movement

Snow that survives the summer is buried and compressed; over decades it recrystallises into dense glacial ice. Under gravity, ice deforms internally and may slide over its bed, especially where meltwater lubricates the base. The upper, brittle zone develops crevasses, while the terminus retreats or advances depending on the balance between accumulation and ablation. Glaciers erode distinctive U-shaped valleys, fjords and moraines, and deposit till as they retreat.

Types

Alpine (valley) glaciers flow down mountain valleys; piedmont glaciers spread out on lowland plains; tidewater glaciers calve icebergs into the sea; ice caps and the continental ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland are the largest forms. Surging glaciers periodically accelerate dramatically.

Glaciers and climate

Most of the world's glaciers have been retreating since the end of the Little Ice Age, with retreat accelerating under anthropogenic global warming. Meltwater contributes to sea-level rise and is a crucial dry-season water source for regions such as the Himalaya-fed rivers of Asia and the Andes.
