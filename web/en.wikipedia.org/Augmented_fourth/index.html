Augmented fourth

An augmented fourth is a musical interval formed by expanding a perfect fourth by one chromatic semitone, giving a span of six semitones. It is enharmonically equivalent to the diminished fifth, and together the two spellings make up the interval commonly known as the tritone.

Properties

In twelve-tone equal temperament the augmented fourth divides the octave exactly in half, which makes it inversionally symmetric: an augmented fourth inverts to a diminished fifth, itself another tritone. Historically the interval was treated as highly dissonant; medieval theorists associated it with the phrase mi contra fa est diabolus in musica ("mi against fa is the devil in music"), although historians note the phrase was a mnemonic about solmisation rather than an outright ban.

Use

The augmented fourth appears naturally between the fourth and seventh degrees of the major scale (F and B in C major) and is a defining component of the dominant seventh chord, whose tension resolves to the tonic. In jazz, tritone substitution exploits the interval's symmetry by replacing a dominant chord with one a tritone away. In just intonation, an augmented fourth may be tuned as the ratio 45:32 or 7:5, depending on context.
