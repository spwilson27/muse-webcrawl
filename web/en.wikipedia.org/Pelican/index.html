Pelican

Pelicans (genus Pelecanus) are a genus of large water birds that make up the family Pelecanidae. They are characterized by a long beak and a large throat pouch used for catching prey and draining water from the scooped-up contents before swallowing.

Description

Pelicans are among the largest flying birds, with wingspans reaching over 3 metres in the Dalmatian pelican. They have long bills with a hooked tip, short legs, and fully webbed feet. Plumage ranges from white to brown and grey depending on species.

Species and distribution

Eight species are generally recognized, distributed on coasts and inland waterways on all continents except Antarctica. Some species plunge-dive for fish, while others fish cooperatively in groups, herding fish into shallow water.

Feeding and breeding

Pelicans feed mainly on fish but also take amphibians, crustaceans, and occasionally small birds. They breed in colonies, building nests on the ground or in trees, and both parents feed the young by regurgitation.

In culture

The pelican has been a symbol of self-sacrifice in Christian iconography, based on the legend that a mother pelican wounds her own breast to feed her young with her blood.
