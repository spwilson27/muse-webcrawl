The White Revolution (Persian: Enqelāb-e Safid) was a far-reaching series of reforms in Iran launched in 1963 by Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. Named "white" because it was intended to be bloodless, the programme sought to modernise Iran's economy and society while strengthening the monarchy.

Programme

The original six points included land reform breaking up large estates, nationalisation of forests and pastureland, sale of state-owned factories to finance land reform, profit-sharing for industrial workers, electoral reform extending the vote to women, and a Literacy Corps that sent conscript teachers to villages. The programme later grew to nineteen points, adding health corps, water nationalisation, and reconstruction efforts.

Effects and opposition

The reforms spurred rapid economic growth, urbanisation, and literacy, and women's suffrage was a landmark change. However, land reform often left peasants with plots too small to farm profitably, driving migration to cities. Clerical opposition, led by Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, condemned the reforms as un-Islamic and an instrument of Western influence; Khomeini's arrest in June 1963 sparked major riots, and he was exiled the following year.

Legacy

Although the White Revolution modernised much of Iranian society, its disruption of traditional structures and the inequalities it created contributed to the discontent that culminated in the Iranian Revolution of 1979, which overthrew the Shah.
