Arche

In early Greek philosophy, arche (Greek ἀρχή; also spelled arkhe or archê) is the first principle or originating substance of all things. The term's older uses in Greek literature mean "beginning" or "source", and it came also to denote rule or sovereignty, as in "archon" and "monarchy".

Presocratic thought

Aristotle reports that the earliest philosophers sought a single underlying material principle. Thales proposed water; Anaximander, rejecting any familiar element, named the apeiron — the indefinite or boundless — as arche; Anaximenes chose air, explaining change by rarefaction and condensation. Heraclitus emphasized fire and the logos of constant change, while the Pythagoreans looked to number. Later pluralists such as Empedocles (four roots) and the atomists (atoms and void) multiplied the principles.

Later usage

The word appears in the Septuagint and the Gospel of John ("In the beginning", en archē), and archē retained technical senses in later philosophy, for example as "principle" or "starting point" in Aristotle's analyses of knowledge and causation.
