Turing test

Proposed byAlan Turing
Year1950
Source paper"Computing Machinery and Intelligence"
FieldArtificial intelligence, philosophy of mind

The Turing test, proposed by Alan Turing in his 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", is a test of a machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behaviour indistinguishable from that of a human. Turing replaced the question "Can machines think?" with an "imitation game" in which a human evaluator converses, via text, with both a machine and a human without knowing which is which; if the evaluator cannot reliably tell them apart, the machine is said to pass.

Background

Turing predicted that by the year 2000 machines with sufficient storage would fool interrogators in five-minute conversations about 30 percent of the time. He also anticipated and answered objections ranging from theology to mathematical incompleteness.

Criticisms and variants

Philosophers have questioned whether passing the test demonstrates thought or understanding: John Searle's Chinese room argument is the most famous critique. Variants include the reverse Turing test (embodied in CAPTCHAs), total Turing tests adding perceptual abilities, and modern benchmark suites for large language models.

Influence

The Loebner Prize, awarded from 1991, ran annual Turing test competitions for chatbots. The test remains a landmark of AI philosophy even as the field has moved toward task-based benchmarks, and systems such as modern chatbots have reignited debate about what passing it would mean.
