Wassailing

Wassailing is a traditional English practice of singing and drinking to the health of trees, crops, or people, most often at Christmastide and around Twelfth Night. The word derives from the Old Norse salutation ves heill, "be in good health," to which the reply was drink hail.

Forms

The custom took two main forms. In house-visiting wassailing, carolers went from door to door singing and offering a drink from a communal wassail bowl — typically filled with spiced ale, cider, or mulled wine — in exchange for gifts or refreshment; this practice contributed to the tradition of Christmas caroling. In orchard wassailing, still practiced in cider-producing regions such as Somerset and Devon, villagers gather among apple trees, sing to them, pour cider over their roots, place toast soaked in cider in the branches, and make noise to awaken the trees and drive away evil spirits, encouraging a good harvest.

History

Wassailing is documented from the Middle Ages and was widely practiced through the early modern period. Many traditional wassail songs survive, including the Gloucestershire Wassail, popularized as "Wassail! Wassail! All Over the Town." The custom has enjoyed revivals in recent decades, with community orchard wassails held each January.
