In literary theory, a paratext is any material that accompanies and presents a text, mediating between the text and its readers. The concept was developed by the French literary theorist Gérard Genette in Palimpsestes (1982) and most fully in Seuils (1987), translated as Paratexts: Thresholds of Interpretation. Genette called the paratext a "threshold" (seuil) — a zone between the inside and the outside of the work that controls the reader's reception of it.

Peritext and epitext

Genette divided the paratext into two categories. The peritext consists of elements physically attached to the volume: the author's name, the title and subtitle, prefaces and introductions, dedications, epigraphs, chapter headings, notes, and the cover and other publisher's apparatus. The epitext consists of elements outside the book: interviews with the author, letters, diaries, publicity material, and reviews or commentaries that circulate the work's reputation. Together, peritext and epitext make up the total paratext.

Functions

Paratexts perform several functions: they present the text, identify its author and genre, guide interpretation, and seek to secure a favourable reception. Genette stressed that a preface, for example, exists "to get the book read and to get it read well". The concept has been widely adopted in book history, narratology, translation studies, and the study of digital media, where menus, thumbnails and metadata can be analysed as paratextual frames.

Criticism and development

Later scholars have questioned Genette's author-centred model, noting that paratexts may be produced by publishers, translators, fans and platforms, and may resist as well as serve authorial intention. The notion of the paratext has been extended to film (trailers, posters), video games, and websites.
