Hilton of Cadboll Stone

The Hilton of Cadboll Stone is a Class II Pictish symbol stone, dating from about 800 AD, discovered at Hilton of Cadboll in Easter Ross, in the Scottish Highlands. It is one of the most celebrated Pictish stones, noted for its intricate carved hunting scene, and is now displayed in the Museum of Scotland in Edinburgh, with a full-scale replica standing at the original site near the Cadboll chapel ruins.

Description

The stone stands about 2.3 metres high. Its seaward face bears a Christian cross flanked by angels and decorated with interlace. The landward face — the famous panel — is divided into three sections: at the top are the Pictish symbols of the crescent and V-rod, a double disc and Z-rod, and a mirror and comb; the middle panel shows a richly detailed hunting scene with a mounted lady, often interpreted as a noblewoman or as the biblical figure of the hunt, accompanied by horsemen, hounds, and a stag; the bottom panel features swirling interlace and vine-scroll ornament.

History

The stone stood for centuries beside the chapel at Hilton of Cadboll. In 1676, its cross face was defaced — the cross was chiselled away and an inscription to a local laird, Alexander Duff and his wives, was carved in its place. The stone was moved to Invergordon Castle in the 19th century and later donated to the British Museum, provoking controversy; it was transferred to Edinburgh in 1921. In 2001, excavations at the chapel site recovered the missing lower portion of the stone along with thousands of carved fragments.

Replica

A modern replica, carved by sculptor Barry Grove using traditional techniques, was erected on the original site in 2000, restoring the monument's presence in its home landscape.
