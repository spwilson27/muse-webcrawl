Fear

TypeBasic emotion
FunctionResponse to perceived danger or threat
Key brain regionAmygdala
Related statesAnxiety, phobia, fight-or-flight response

Fear is an intensely unpleasant emotion induced by perceiving or recognising a danger or threat. Fear causes psychological changes that may produce behavioural reactions such as mounting an aggressive response or fleeing the threat; this is known as the fight-or-flight response. Extreme cases of fear can trigger a "freeze" or paralysis response.

Mechanism

In humans and other animals, fear is processed largely through the amygdala, which triggers the release of stress hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol via the hypothalamus. The result is increased heart rate and blood pressure, rapid breathing, muscle tension, and heightened vigilance — preparing the body to confront or escape the threat. Fear differs from anxiety in that fear is a response to a present, identifiable threat, whereas anxiety concerns anticipated future threats.

Learning and disorders

Fears can be innate (such as responses to heights or loud noises), learned through direct conditioning or observation, or culturally transmitted. When fear is persistent, excessive, and impairing, it may take the form of a phobia or an anxiety disorder; these are commonly treated with exposure therapy and other psychological or pharmacological interventions. Fear also plays a large role in culture, from horror entertainment to political rhetoric.
