In Greek mythology, maenads were the female followers of Dionysus, god of wine and ecstasy, and the most important members of his retinue, the thiasos. Their name literally translates as "raving ones." Maenads were known as Bacchantes in Roman religion, after the Roman name for the god, Bacchus. Often the maenads were portrayed as inspired into a state of ecstatic frenzy through a combination of dancing and intoxication.

A maenad dancing with a thyrsus, from Greek vase painting.

Mythology

In myth, maenads roamed the mountains and forests, dressed in fawn skins, crowned with ivy, carrying the thyrsus — a fennel staff tipped with a pine cone — and sometimes handling or wearing snakes. In their frenzy they were said to perform the sparagmos, tearing wild animals apart with their bare hands, and to eat the raw flesh (omophagia). The most famous literary treatment is Euripides' tragedy The Bacchae, in which the maenads of Thebes, led by Agave, tear apart King Pentheus, who had spurned Dionysus — Agave, his own mother, carrying his head in triumph, believing it a lion's.

Worship

Historical maenadism involved organised groups of women who periodically withdrew to the mountains for Dionysian rites. Dionysian cult, and the maenads with it, spread through Greece and into the Roman world, where the Bacchanalia scandal of 186 BCE led the Roman Senate to suppress the cult's organisation.
