Bourbon whiskey is a type of barrel-aged American whiskey made primarily from corn. Named after Bourbon County, Kentucky — itself named for the French House of Bourbon — it has been produced since the 18th century and is legally defined by United States federal regulations.

Legal definition

Under US law, bourbon must be made in the United States from a grain mixture (mash bill) of at least 51% corn, distilled to no more than 160 proof (80% ABV), put into new charred oak containers at no more than 125 proof, and bottled at no less than 80 proof. No flavouring or colouring additives are permitted. "Straight bourbon" must additionally be aged at least two years, and any age statement below four years must appear on the label. Contrary to popular belief, bourbon need not be made in Kentucky, though the great majority — brands such as Maker's Mark, Wild Turkey, Jim Beam, Woodford Reserve, and Buffalo Trace — is.

Production and flavour

The remainder of the mash bill typically consists of rye or wheat (producing spicier or softer "wheated" bourbons respectively) plus malted barley. After fermentation and distillation, the clear spirit ("white dog") gains its amber colour and flavours of vanilla, caramel, and oak from the charred new barrels and Kentucky's hot summers and cold winters, which drive the spirit in and out of the wood. Small batch and single barrel expressions are marketed as premium bottlings.

History

Scots-Irish and other settlers in Kentucky distilled corn whiskey from the late 1700s; the name "bourbon" became attached to whiskey shipped down the Ohio and Mississippi rivers from "Old Bourbon" county. The industry was reshaped by the Bottled-in-Bond Act (1897), Prohibition (1920–1933), and the 1964 congressional resolution recognising bourbon as a distinctive product of the United States. Since the 2000s bourbon has enjoyed a global resurgence, with the Kentucky Bourbon Trail drawing millions of visitors.
