Annealing (materials science)

In metallurgy and materials science, annealing is a heat treatment that alters the physical and sometimes chemical properties of a material to increase its ductility and reduce its hardness, making it more workable. It involves heating a material above its recrystallisation temperature, maintaining a suitable temperature for an appropriate amount of time and then cooling.

Process

In annealing, atoms migrate in the crystal lattice and the number of dislocations decreases, leading to a change in ductility and hardness. The process occurs in three stages: recovery, recrystallisation, and grain growth.

Recovery

During recovery, internal stresses introduced by cold working are relieved as dislocations rearrange into lower-energy configurations, with little change in grain structure or strength.

Recrystallisation

At higher temperatures, new strain-free grains nucleate and grow, replacing the deformed grains of the cold-worked material. Hardness drops sharply and ductility is restored.

Grain growth

If heating continues, grains coarsen. Excessive grain growth can reduce strength and produce an undesirable "orange peel" surface on forming, so annealing practice balances softness against grain size.

Applications

Annealing is routinely applied to steel, copper, aluminium and brass between cold-working operations, to relieve stresses in castings and welds, and to produce specific microstructures. Glass is also annealed to remove internal stresses that would otherwise cause spontaneous fracture. The term has been borrowed by computing for the optimisation heuristic of simulated annealing.
