Helvetii

The Helvetii were a Celtic people, or confederation of tribes, who occupied the Swiss Plateau — the region between the Rhine, the Jura mountains, Lake Geneva and the Alps — in the late Iron Age. Their name survives in the Latin name for Switzerland, Confoederatio Helvetica, and in the abbreviation CH.

Origins and territory

The Helvetii probably migrated into the region from southern Germany in the 2nd century BC. According to Julius Caesar, they were divided into four cantons or pagi, among them the Tigurini, who had famously defeated a Roman army at the Battle of Burdigala in 107 BC, forcing the captured soldiers to pass under the yoke. Their territory contained fortified settlements (oppida) such as those at Bern-Engehalbinsel, Basel and Mont Vully, and important gold coinage circulated among them.

Migration and defeat, 58 BC

In 58 BC, under pressure from Germanic peoples and feeling confined by their mountainous territory, the Helvetii decided to migrate en masse to southwestern Gaul, burning their towns and villages behind them. Caesar, as proconsul of Gaul, refused them passage through Roman territory and, after they took a route through the lands of the Sequani, defeated them in the hard-fought Battle of Bibracte. Caesar's account claims a migrating host of some 368,000 people, a figure modern scholars regard as exaggerated. The survivors were ordered to return home and rebuild, thereafter serving Rome as a buffer against Germanic incursions.

Roman period and legacy

The Helvetii were gradually integrated into the Roman Empire; their territory was administered from Aventicum (modern Avenches), which became a Roman colony, and their elites adopted Roman language and customs. The name Helvetia was revived in the early modern period as a poetic and then official designation for the Swiss Confederation, and the female personification Helvetia appears on Swiss coins and stamps.
