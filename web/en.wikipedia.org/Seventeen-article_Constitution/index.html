Seventeen-article Constitution

Japanese十七条憲法 (Jūshichijō Kenpō)
Date604 AD (traditional)
Attributed toPrince Shōtoku (Shōtoku Taishi)
Recorded inNihon Shoki (720 AD)
Content17 moral and bureaucratic precepts for officials

The Seventeen-article Constitution (十七条憲法, Jūshichijō Kenpō) is a document traditionally dated to 604 AD and attributed to the Japanese prince-regent Prince Shōtoku. Preserved in the Nihon Shoki (720), it is less a legal constitution in the modern sense than a set of moral guidelines for court officials, drawing heavily on Confucian and Buddhist principles.

Content

The articles open with the famous injunction "Harmony (wa) is to be valued, and avoidance of wanton opposition is to be honoured." Other articles enjoin reverence for the Three Treasures of Buddhism (the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha), obedience to imperial commands, proper conduct and impartiality of officials, diligence in public service, opposition to corruption and private gain, and decision-making by consultation on important matters. Article 12 declares that there is only one sovereign and that officials must not levy taxes on their own authority — an early statement of centralised state power.

Historical significance

The document reflects the Asuka-period effort, associated with Shōtoku and the Soga clan, to build a centralised, bureaucratic state modelled on Sui-dynasty China. Some scholars have questioned whether the text dates exactly to 604, but it is generally accepted as an authentic product of the period. The Seventeen-article Constitution is often cited as Japan's earliest "constitution", and its first article's emphasis on harmony remains a celebrated cultural reference in Japan.
