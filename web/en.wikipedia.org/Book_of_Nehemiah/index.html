The Book of Nehemiah is a book of the Hebrew Bible, forming with the Book of Ezra the composite work Ezra–Nehemiah. It is largely written in the first person as the memoir of Nehemiah, a Jewish official at the Persian court who became governor of Judah and rebuilt the walls of Jerusalem in the mid-5th century BC.

Summary

Nehemiah, cupbearer to King Artaxerxes I at Susa, learns that the walls of Jerusalem lie in ruins and obtains the king's permission and support to rebuild them. Arriving in Jerusalem around 445 BC, he inspects the walls by night, organises the population by families and districts, and completes the rebuilding in 52 days despite the opposition of Sanballat the Horonite, Tobiah the Ammonite, and Geshem the Arab, who mock, threaten, and plot against the work.

The second half of the book describes the public reading of the Law by Ezra, the people's confession and renewal of the covenant, the dedication of the wall, and Nehemiah's second term as governor, during which he enforces Sabbath observance, tithing, and the separation from foreign wives.

Composition

The "Nehemiah memoir" (much of chapters 1–7 and 11–13) is widely regarded as deriving from an authentic first-person account, later combined with material about Ezra and census lists shared with the Book of Ezra. The combined Ezra–Nehemiah reached its final form probably in the 4th century BC.
