The cuisine of Rome (Italian: cucina romana) is the traditional food of Italy's capital, rooted in the frugal cooking of the city's working classes and the produce of the surrounding Lazio countryside — sheep's-milk pecorino cheese, cured pork cheek (guanciale), artichokes, and the offal of the old Testaccio slaughterhouse, the so-called quinto quarto ("fifth quarter").

Famous dishes

Rome claims four celebrated pasta dishes: spaghetti alla carbonara (egg, guanciale, pecorino and black pepper, without cream), tonnarelli cacio e pepe (cheese and pepper), bucatini all'amatriciana (tomato, guanciale, pecorino and chilli, from nearby Amatrice) and penne all'arrabbiata. Equally emblematic are carciofi alla romana and deep-fried carciofi alla giudia from the old Jewish ghetto, saltimbocca alla romana (veal with prosciutto and sage), coda alla vaccinara (oxtail stew), trippa alla romana, and the crisp thin-crusted pizza al taglio sold by weight. Supplì — fried rice croquettes with a mozzarella heart — are the city's classic street snack.

Character

Roman cooking is famously strict about its canonical recipes, and debates over ingredients — guanciale versus pancetta, pecorino versus parmesan — are a matter of civic pride, even as the modern city layers a global food scene over its trattoria tradition.
