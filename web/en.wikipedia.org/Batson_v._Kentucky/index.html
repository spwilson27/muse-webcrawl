Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79 (1986), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States ruling that a prosecutor's use of peremptory challenges to exclude jurors of the defendant's race violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Background

James Kirkland Batson, an African American man charged with burglary and receipt of stolen goods in Kentucky, objected when the prosecutor struck all four Black prospective jurors, leaving an all-white jury. The trial judge denied the objection, relying on Swain v. Alabama (1965), which required proof of systematic exclusion across many cases. Batson was convicted.

Decision

In a 7–2 decision written by Justice Lewis F. Powell Jr., the Court held that a defendant could establish a prima facie case of racial discrimination in jury selection based on the facts of his own trial. Under the resulting "Batson challenge", the struck party must show the circumstances raise an inference of discrimination; the striking party must then offer a race-neutral explanation; and the court decides whether purposeful discrimination occurred.

Legacy

The framework was later extended to civil trials (Edmonson v. Leesville), to defense strikes (Georgia v. McCollum), and to sex-based strikes (J.E.B. v. Alabama). Critics argue the standard is easily evaded by pretextual explanations, while later cases such as Flowers v. Mississippi (2019) have reinforced its enforcement.
