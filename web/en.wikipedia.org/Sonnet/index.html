A sonnet is a fixed-form lyric poem of fourteen lines, usually written in iambic pentameter (in English) and following a strict rhyme scheme. The name derives from the Italian sonetto, "little song". One of the most durable poetic forms, the sonnet has been used for seven centuries to treat love, time, mortality, faith and politics.

Forms

The Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet divides into an octave (ABBA ABBA) posing a problem or question and a sestet (CDE CDE or variants) resolving it, with a turn — the volta — between them. It was perfected by Petrarch in his Canzoniere (366 poems, 317 of them sonnets, to his beloved Laura). The Shakespearean (English) sonnet uses three quatrains and a closing couplet (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG), allowing a more argumentative build-up and a punchy conclusion — the form of Shakespeare's 154 Sonnets (1609). The Spenserian sonnet links its quatrains with interlocking rhymes (ABAB BCBC CDCD EE).

History

Invented at the Sicilian court of Frederick II by Giacomo da Lentini in the 1230s, the sonnet spread through Italy (Dante, Petrarch), then to France (Ronsard), Spain and England, where Wyatt and Surrey introduced it and Sidney, Spenser and Shakespeare made it the vehicle of great sonnet sequences in the 1590s. Milton extended its subjects to politics and religion. After a decline, the Romantics — Wordsworth, Keats, Shelley — revived it, and it has remained alive through the modernists to today, with poets such as Edna St. Vincent Millay and Terrance Hayes (whose "American sonnets" loosen the form) exploiting its tension between constraint and freedom.
