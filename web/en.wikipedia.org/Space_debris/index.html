Space debris

Space debris (also called space junk, space pollution, or orbital debris) consists of defunct human-made objects in space — principally in Earth orbit — which no longer serve a useful function. These include derelict spacecraft, spent rocket stages, mission-related debris, and fragments from collisions and explosions. Tens of thousands of objects larger than 10 cm are tracked, while estimates of objects between 1 and 10 cm run into the hundreds of thousands, and millimetre-scale particles number in the tens of millions.

The problem

At orbital velocities of roughly 7–8 km/s in low Earth orbit, even small debris can severely damage or destroy a satellite. In 1978, NASA scientist Donald Kessler proposed the Kessler syndrome: a scenario in which the density of objects in low Earth orbit becomes high enough that collisions create cascades of further debris. Notable events include the 2007 Chinese anti-satellite test against FengYun-1C and the 2009 collision between Iridium 33 and Cosmos 2251, each generating thousands of trackable fragments.

Tracking and mitigation

The United States Space Surveillance Network and other systems track large debris so operators can maneuver active satellites out of harm's way; the International Space Station performs regular avoidance maneuvers. Mitigation guidelines recommend passivating spent stages and moving satellites at end of life to graveyard orbits or ensuring they re-enter within 25 years. Active debris removal concepts — nets, harpoons, robotic arms, and lasers — are under development by agencies and companies.
