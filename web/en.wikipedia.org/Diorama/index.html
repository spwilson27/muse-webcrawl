Diorama

A diorama is a replica of a scene, typically a three-dimensional model either in miniature or as a full-size exhibit in a museum. The word was coined in 1822 for a theatrical entertainment in Paris invented by Louis Daguerre and Charles Marie Bouton, in which audiences viewed huge paintings whose appearance was transformed by changing light shone through and onto them.

Museum dioramas

The modern sense of the term refers to the habitat dioramas pioneered in natural history museums in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: taxidermied animals posed on modelled terrain before a curved painted background, viewed through glass. Carl Akeley is regarded as the father of the form, creating celebrated displays for the Milwaukee Public Museum and the American Museum of Natural History, whose Akeley Hall of African Mammals opened in 1936.

Miniature dioramas

Scale-model hobbyists build dioramas to present model vehicles, aircraft, figures, and railway scenes in realistic settings, combining kits with sculpted terrain, buildings, and weathering effects. Miniature dioramas are also used by architects, military planners, and in forensic reconstruction.

Other uses

The term is also applied to the layered "shoebox" scenes made in classrooms, to peep-show boxes and panoramic illusions, and, in modern media, to virtual-reality and video-game environments that recreate scenes in three dimensions.
