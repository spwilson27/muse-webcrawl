Murder of Kitty Genovese

Date: 13 March 1964
Location: Kew Gardens, Queens, New York City
Victim: Catherine "Kitty" Genovese, 28
Perpetrator: Winston Moseley (convicted)

The murder of Kitty Genovese took place in the early hours of 13 March 1964 in the Kew Gardens neighbourhood of Queens, New York City. Catherine Genovese, a 28-year-old bartender, was stabbed to death near her home by Winston Moseley. The case became a national sensation after a New York Times article claimed that 38 witnesses had seen or heard the attack and done nothing — a story that later research showed was substantially exaggerated but which gave rise to the psychological concept of the bystander effect.

The crime

Genovese returned from work at about 3:15 a.m. and parked near the Long Island Rail Road station. Moseley, who had been driving around looking for a victim, followed and stabbed her twice in the back on the street. A neighbour shouted from a window, driving him off briefly, but finding no police response he returned and attacked her again in a vestibule inside her apartment building, stabbing her repeatedly and sexually assaulting her. One neighbour, Karl Ross, eventually called friends and then police after hesitation; another, Sophia Farrar, ran to hold Genovese, who died in her arms. Police arrived within minutes of the call, but she died in the ambulance.

The "38 witnesses" story

Two weeks later the Times published "37 Who Saw Murder Didn't Call the Police" (the number later given as 38), based on remarks attributed to the police commissioner. The story portrayed dozens of respectable neighbours as having watched for over half an hour without acting. Subsequent investigation — including archive research and a 2016 retrospective by the Times itself — found the account seriously flawed: few witnesses saw the attacks clearly, only a handful saw any part of them, most heard only brief cries they could not interpret, and at least two called or attempted to call the police. Moseley was arrested days later during a burglary and confessed; he was convicted and died in prison in 2016.

Legacy

Whatever its inaccuracies, the case spurred reforms including the adoption of the 911 emergency number in the United States. Social psychologists Bibb Latané and John Darley, inspired by the story, conducted the classic experiments that established the bystander effect and diffusion of responsibility. Genovese's life — she lived with her partner Mary Ann Zielonko — also later drew attention in accounts reclaiming her story from the myth.
