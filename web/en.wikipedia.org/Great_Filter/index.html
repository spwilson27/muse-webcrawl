Great Filter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Filter is a concept in astrobiology and futures studies, proposed by economist Robin Hanson in a 1996 essay, "The Great Filter — Are We Almost Past It?" It offers one possible resolution of the Fermi paradox: the apparent absence of extraterrestrial civilisations despite the vast number of potentially habitable planets. The hypothesis holds that at least one step on the path from lifeless matter to a visible, expanding technological civilisation is extremely improbable, acting as a "filter" that almost all life fails to pass.

The steps

Hanson listed a chain of hurdles: a suitable star system and planet; the origin of self-reproducing molecules (abiogenesis); simple and then complex (eukaryotic) cells; sexual reproduction; multicellular life; tool-using animals with large brains; a civilisation at humanity's stage; and, finally, expansion throughout the galaxy. The universe's silence implies the combined probability of the chain is tiny — but not where the impasse lies.

Behind us or ahead of us?

If the filter lies in our past — for instance, if the origin of life or of eukaryotes is vanishingly rare — humanity may already have passed it, and the future is comparatively open. If it lies ahead — some catastrophe that reliably destroys technological civilisations, such as nuclear war, engineered pandemics, or runaway climate change — the odds for humanity are grim. Hanson argued that discovering complex life elsewhere (e.g., fossil microbes on Mars) would be bad news, since it would suggest the early steps are easy and the filter is still to come. The concept has become a standard framework in discussions of existential risk and SETI.
