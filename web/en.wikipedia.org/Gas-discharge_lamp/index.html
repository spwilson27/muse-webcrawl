Gas-discharge lamp

A gas-discharge lamp is an electric lamp that produces light by sending an electric discharge through an ionised gas (a plasma). Unlike an incandescent lamp, which heats a filament, a discharge lamp has no filament; instead, electrons accelerated by an electric field collide with gas atoms, exciting them so that they emit photons. The colour and spectrum of the light depend on the gas and any phosphor coatings used.

Types

Common gas-discharge lamps include neon lamps and neon signage; fluorescent lamps, which use mercury vapour whose ultraviolet emission excites a phosphor coating; and high-intensity discharge (HID) lamps such as mercury-vapour, metal-halide, and sodium-vapour lamps, the latter long used for street lighting because of their efficiency and characteristic orange glow. Low-pressure sodium lamps are among the most efficient light sources ever commercialised, but their monochromatic yellow light renders colours poorly.

Operation

Discharge lamps exhibit negative resistance once struck: the current tends to increase without limit, so they must be operated with a ballast that limits the current. Starting usually requires a high voltage pulse to ionise the gas. Discharge lamps generally take some time to reach full brightness and cannot be restarted immediately after being switched off while hot. Although historically dominant in commercial and street lighting, many applications have been displaced by LED lamps, which are more efficient, longer-lived, and free of mercury.
