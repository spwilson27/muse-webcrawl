Instrument of Accession

The Instrument of Accession was a legal document devised under the Government of India Act 1935 and used in 1947, at the partition of British India, by which the ruler of a princely state acceded to one of the new dominions, India or Pakistan. By signing it, a ruler ceded to the dominion government control over defence, external affairs and communications, while retaining internal autonomy in other matters.

Background

British India comprised directly ruled provinces and more than 560 princely states under British paramountcy. The Indian Independence Act 1947 provided that paramountcy would lapse on 15 August 1947, leaving the states technically independent. The departing British, and the leaders of both new dominions, urged rulers to accede to the dominion with which their state was geographically contiguous and demographically aligned. Lord Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, addressed the Chamber of Princes on 25 July 1947 pressing them to sign before the transfer of power. By 15 August 1947 almost all states within India's borders had acceded.

Terms

The standard instrument provided that the ruler acceded on the three subjects of defence, external affairs and communications (listed in a schedule), that the dominion would not interfere in internal administration beyond those subjects, and that the ruler's sovereignty and existing arrangements would otherwise continue. Acceptance by the Governor-General completed the accession. Later, most states signed merger agreements or covenants forming unions of states, fully integrating them into India or Pakistan in return for privy purses and privileges (abolished in India in 1971).

Disputed accessions

Three accessions remained controversial. In Junagadh, a Hindu-majority state with a Muslim ruler who acceded to Pakistan, India held a plebiscite in 1948 that voted overwhelmingly for India. Hyderabad, the largest state, held out under its Nizam until Indian military action (Operation Polo) in September 1948. Most famously, Maharaja Hari Singh of Jammu and Kashmir, a Hindu ruler of a Muslim-majority state, signed the Instrument of Accession to India on 26 October 1947 after tribal forces invaded from Pakistan; Governor-General Mountbatten accepted it on 27 October with a note that the accession should later be confirmed by reference to the people. That proviso, and rival interpretations of the accession's finality, lie at the root of the Kashmir conflict and successive Indo-Pakistani wars.
