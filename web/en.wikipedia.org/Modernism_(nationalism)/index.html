Modernism (nationalism)

Modernism, in the study of nationalism, is the theoretical approach holding that nations and nationalism are products of modernity — of industrialisation, capitalism, urbanisation, secularisation, and the modern state — rather than ancient or natural features of human society. It is contrasted with primordialism and perennialism, which regard nations as rooted in deep ethnic pasts.

Main theorists

Ernest Gellner's Nations and Nationalism (1983) argued that industrial society requires a homogeneous, literate "high culture" transmitted by mass education, and nationalism supplies the ideology for this process. Benedict Anderson's Imagined Communities (1983) described nations as imagined political communities made possible by print capitalism. Eric Hobsbawm emphasised the "invention of tradition" in the nineteenth century, while Miroslav Hroch studied the phases of national movements among smaller European peoples.

Variants

Modernist accounts differ in emphasis: economic transformation (Gellner), communication and print (Anderson, Karl Deutsch), political state-building (John Breuilly), and cultural construction (Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger). Ethnosymbolists such as Anthony D. Smith accept much of the modernist case but stress the pre-modern ethnic myths and symbols from which nations were built.

Influence

Modernism became the dominant paradigm in nationalism studies after 1945, displacing earlier views of nations as awakened ancient communities.
