Antillean Creole

Antillean Creole (native name: Kreyol, Kwéyòl, or Patwa) is a French-based creole language spoken primarily in the Lesser Antilles. Closely related varieties are spoken in Martinique, Guadeloupe, Dominica, Saint Lucia, Grenada, and Trinidad and Tobago, as well as in French Guiana and (in a related form) Haiti.

History

The language developed during the era of African slavery on French sugar plantations in the 17th and 18th centuries, combining a French-derived vocabulary with grammatical influences from West African languages. Long stigmatized as a corruption of French, it has since the late 20th century gained literary use, standardized orthographies, and some recognition in education.

Features

Like other French-based creoles, Antillean Creole places preverbal markers for tense, mood and aspect, and derives most of its lexicon from French. In Dominica and Saint Lucia, heavy historical contact with English has added many English loanwords, while the varieties of Martinique and Guadeloupe remain closer to French.
