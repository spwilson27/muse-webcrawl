Don Juan (Spanish: [doŋ ˈxwan]) is a legendary, fictional Spanish libertine who is devoted to seducing women. He is famous for his roguish charm and his refusal to repent, culminating in his being dragged to Hell by a stone statue of one of his victims. The character has been retold in hundreds of works of literature, theatre, opera and film, and "Don Juan" has entered many languages as a common noun for a seducer or womaniser.

Origins

The first literary treatment is the Spanish play El burlador de Sevilla y convidado de piedra ("The Trickster of Seville and the Stone Guest"), published around 1630 and attributed to Tirso de Molina. In the play, Don Juan Tenorio seduces a series of women, kills the commander Don Gonzalo de Ulloa, and mockingly invites the dead man's funerary statue to dinner; the statue accepts, returns the invitation, and drags the unrepentant Don Juan down to Hell. The legend may draw on real figures, such as Don Juan de Mañara or the Sevillian rake Miguel de Mañara, and on older folklore motifs of the dead guest.

Major versions

  • Molière, Dom Juan (1665) — a prose comedy portraying Don Juan as a freethinking hypocrite.
  • Mozart, Don Giovanni (1787), libretto by Lorenzo Da Ponte — widely considered the definitive operatic version, blending comedy and horror; his servant Leporello's "catalogue aria" lists conquests numbering 1,003 in Spain alone.
  • Lord Byron, Don Juan (1819–1824) — an epic satire in which Juan is as much seduced as seducer.
  • José Zorrilla, Don Juan Tenorio (1844) — the version traditionally performed in Spain on All Saints' Day, in which Juan is redeemed by the love of Doña Inés.
  • Other treatments include works by Pushkin (The Stone Guest), Kierkegaard's analysis in Either/Or, Shaw's Man and Superman, and Richard Strauss's tone poem Don Juan.

Interpretations

Interpreters have read Don Juan variously as a rebel against social and religious authority, a pathological seducer, an embodiment of sensual immediacy, or a figure of existential defiance who accepts damnation rather than submission. The "Don Juanism" of psychology describes a pattern of compulsive conquest attributed to the character.
