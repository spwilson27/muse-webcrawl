In geology, a rift is a linear zone where the Earth's lithosphere is being pulled apart, creating a lowland region bounded by normal faults. Rifting is an example of extensional tectonics and, if it continues, can split a continent in two and create a new ocean basin. The central depression of a rift is commonly called a graben, and the uplifted blocks on either side are horsts.

Formation

Rifts form where tectonic forces stretch the crust. The brittle upper crust breaks along normal faults while the ductile lower crust and mantle thin and neck. Upwelling mantle material may partially melt, producing basaltic volcanism along the rift axis. Over millions of years the rift floor subsides and fills with sediments and lava flows; failed rifts that stop progressing are called aulacogens, many of which are preserved within continents as sediment-filled troughs.

Examples

The East African Rift is an active continental rift where the African Plate is splitting into the Somali and Nubian plates; it contains deep lakes such as Tanganyika and a chain of volcanoes including Kilimanjaro. The Baikal Rift in Siberia holds the world's deepest lake. In Europe, the Rhine Graben forms a long valley between France and Germany. Beneath the oceans, mid-ocean ridges mark rifts that have developed into seafloor spreading centres, while the Red Sea represents an intermediate stage between continental rifting and true oceanic spreading.

Resources and significance

Rift basins are important sites of petroleum accumulation, since their thick sedimentary fills can contain both source rocks and reservoirs. Geothermal energy is exploited in rifts in Iceland, Kenya and elsewhere. The East African Rift is also famous as the "cradle of humankind": its sediments and volcanic ash layers preserve abundant fossils of early hominins, which can be dated by the interbedded lavas.
