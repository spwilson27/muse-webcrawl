TypeSoup

VariationsTom yum goong, tom yum pla, tom yum gai, tom yum kha

Tom yum or tom yam (Thai: ต้มยำ, pronounced [tôm jām]) is a family of hot and sour Thai soups. The name derives from two Thai words: tom refers to the boiling process, while yam means "mixed". The soup is characteristically distinguished by its fragrant broth flavoured with lemongrass, galangal and kaffir lime leaves, and seasoned to a hot and sour balance with chillies, lime juice and fish sauce. Tom yum is widely regarded as one of Thailand's signature dishes.

Preparation

The broth of tom yum is traditionally made by briefly boiling the aromatic herbs — lemongrass, galangal and makrut (kaffir) lime leaves — in water or stock, often with straw mushrooms. Crushed fresh chillies or chilli jam (nam prik phao) provide heat, while lime juice, added at the end of cooking to preserve its brightness, provides sourness. Fish sauce supplies saltiness and depth. The soup is usually finished with fresh coriander leaves.

Types

Named varieties are distinguished by their main protein. Tom yum goong, made with river prawns, is the best-known version internationally. Tom yum pla uses fish, tom yum gai uses chicken, and mixed-seafood versions are called tom yum thale or tom yum po taek. A richer, creamy style incorporates evaporated milk or coconut milk; the coconut-milk chicken version overlaps with the related soup tom kha gai.

History and spread

Hot and sour boiled dishes of this type originated in the riverside communities of central Thailand, where freshwater prawns and fish were abundant. With the global popularity of Thai cuisine from the late 20th century onward, tom yum became a staple of Thai restaurants worldwide, and its flavour profile has been applied to noodles, fried rice, snacks and instant noodle seasonings. In 2024, tom yum kung was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
