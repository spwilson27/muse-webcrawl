Belgae

The Belgae were a large confederation of tribes living in northern Gaul, between the English Channel, the west bank of the Rhine, and the northern bank of the river Seine, from at least the third century BC. They are discussed in detail by Julius Caesar in his Commentarii de Bello Gallico, who famously began by remarking that Gaul is divided into three parts, inhabited by the Belgae, the Aquitani, and the Gauls proper (Celtae), and that "of all these, the Belgae are the bravest."

Origins and language

Caesar reported that the Belgae were partly descended from peoples who had crossed the Rhine from Germania long before, and modern scholarship debates the extent to which the Belgic tribes were Celtic-speaking, Germanic-speaking, or spoke a distinct language sometimes called Belgic. Tribal names and personal names among them are largely Celtic in form, and their material culture belongs to the La Tène world, though groups such as the Eburones and the tribes Caesar grouped as the Germani cisrhenani may have had Germanic affiliations.

Tribes

Among the peoples Caesar counts as Belgae are the Bellovaci, Suessiones, Remi, Nervii, Atrebates, Eburones, Menapii, Morini, Ambiani, Caleti, Veliocasses, Viromandui, Aduatuci, Condrusi, and Paemani. The Remi, whose capital was at Durocortorum (modern Reims), allied early with Rome and supplied Caesar with intelligence on the others.

Roman conquest

In 57 BC the Belgic tribes formed a confederacy against Rome, but Caesar defeated them piecemeal in campaigns including the Battle of the Axona and the Battle of the Sabis, where the Nervii nearly destroyed his army. The revolt of Ambiorix of the Eburones in 54 BC, which wiped out a legion and a half under Sabinus and Cotta, provoked devastating Roman reprisals. Augustus later organized the region as the province of Gallia Belgica.

Belgae in Britain

Diviciacus of the Suessiones was remembered as having held power in Britain as well as Gaul, and archaeology shows Belgic influence in southeastern Britain before the Claudian conquest: tribes such as the Atrebates and Belgae there issued coins and used continental burial rites. Winchester (Venta Belgarum) was the civitas capital of a British tribe called Belgae.
