Spanish transition to democracy
Spanish: Transición española
Period: 1975–1982
Key dates: Death of Franco (1975); Constitution (1978); 23-F coup attempt (1981)

The Spanish transition to democracy (Transición española) is the period in Spanish history following the death of dictator Francisco Franco on 20 November 1975, during which Spain moved from the Francoist dictatorship to a parliamentary constitutional monarchy under King Juan Carlos I. It is widely studied as a model of negotiated democratic transition.

Course of the transition

Juan Carlos dismissed the Francoist prime minister Carlos Arias Navarro in 1976 and appointed Adolfo Suárez, who steered through the Law for Political Reform — passed by the Francoist Cortes and ratified by referendum — dismantling the regime by legal means. Political parties, including the Communist Party, were legalised, and the first free elections since 1936 were held in June 1977, won by Suárez's centrist UCD.

Constitution of 1978

The elected Cortes drafted the Spanish Constitution of 1978 by consensus among government, Socialists, Communists, Catalan and Basque nationalists and the right. It established a parliamentary monarchy, a system of autonomous communities, and broad civil rights. Approved by referendum on 6 December 1978, it remains in force. The Moncloa Pacts of 1977 addressed the economic crisis, and an Amnesty Law covered political prisoners and, controversially, Franco-era officials.

Consolidation

The process faced violence from ETA and far-right and far-left groups, and an attempted military coup on 23 February 1981 (23-F), defeated when the King defended the constitutional order on television. The peaceful transfer of power to Felipe González's Socialist government after the 1982 elections is generally taken as the transition's successful conclusion.
