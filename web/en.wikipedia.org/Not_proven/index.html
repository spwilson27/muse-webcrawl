Not proven

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Not proven is a verdict available in Scots criminal law, alongside "guilty" and "not guilty". It is an acquittal: the accused is free to leave and cannot generally be retried for the same offence, subject to the exceptions in the Double Jeopardy (Scotland) Act 2011. Nevertheless the verdict carries a lingering stigma and is popularly described as "not guilty, but don't do it again".

History

Historically, Scottish juries found facts "proven" or "not proven" on each charge, with the court deciding guilt. In the early eighteenth century — notably after the 1728 trial of Carnegie of Finhaven — juries asserted the right to return a general verdict of "not guilty", and the older "not proven" survived alongside it, creating the unusual three-verdict system.

Use and controversy

Not proven is returned in a minority of acquittals, disproportionately in rape and sexual offence cases. Critics argue it confuses jurors, fails complainants, and leaves accused persons under a cloud; supporters argue it allows juries to express genuine doubt while formally acquitting. Successive consultations and research commissioned by the Scottish Government have examined abolishing it, and reform proposals — often paired with changes to jury size and majority requirements — have repeatedly been debated in the Scottish Parliament.

Elsewhere

The verdict is unique to Scotland in the United Kingdom; England and Wales, and most common law systems, offer only guilty or not guilty. A similar historical verdict existed in some other jurisdictions but did not survive. See also Scots law, acquittal and presumption of innocence.
