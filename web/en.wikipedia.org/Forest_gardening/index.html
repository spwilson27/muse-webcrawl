Forest gardening

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Forest gardening is a low-maintenance, sustainable, plant-based food production and agroforestry system based on woodland ecosystems, incorporating fruit and nut trees, shrubs, herbs, vines and perennial vegetables which have yields directly useful to humans. Mixing these plants together in a succession of layers, this style of gardening aims to mimic the structure of a natural woodland to create a productive, ecologically stable system.

The seven layers

The forest garden is usually designed in seven layers: (1) the canopy layer of large fruit and nut trees; (2) the low-tree layer of smaller fruit trees; (3) the shrub layer of currants, berries, and hazels; (4) the herbaceous layer of perennial vegetables and herbs; (5) the ground-cover layer; (6) the underground (rhizosphere) layer of root crops; and (7) the vertical layer of climbers and vines. Polycultures in each layer are chosen to benefit one another, for example nitrogen-fixing plants feeding fruit trees.

History

Forest gardens have existed for millennia in the tropics — the home gardens of Kerala and Sri Lanka, the Maya forest gardens of Mesoamerica, and Indonesian pekarangan. The modern temperate-climate approach was pioneered in Britain by Robert Hart, who developed a forest garden at Wenlock Edge in Shropshire from the 1960s, and was later popularised by permaculture designers and by Martin Crawford, founder of the Agroforestry Research Trust, in his book Creating a Forest Garden (2010).

Benefits and criticism

Advocates claim forest gardens need little input once established: no digging, minimal weeding, no artificial fertiliser, and high habitat value for wildlife, alongside yields of fruit, nuts, herbs, and perennial vegetables. Critics note that yields per hectare of staple foods are low compared with conventional agriculture, that establishment takes years, and that evidence for some claimed benefits is largely anecdotal.
