Norovirus

FamilyCaliciviridae
GenomePositive-sense single-stranded RNA, ~7.5 kb
DiseaseAcute gastroenteritis ("winter vomiting bug", stomach flu)
Incubation12–48 hours
Named forNorwalk, Ohio (1968 outbreak)

Norovirus, also known as the "winter vomiting bug", is a genus of viruses in the family Caliciviridae and the most common cause of acute gastroenteritis worldwide. Infection is characterised by non-bloody diarrhoea, vomiting and stomach pain; fever and headaches may also occur. Symptoms usually develop 12 to 48 hours after exposure and recovery typically occurs within one to three days.

Transmission and epidemiology

The virus spreads via the faecal–oral route — contaminated food or water, person-to-person contact, and aerosolised particles from vomiting — and is highly contagious: as few as a few dozen particles may establish infection. It causes an estimated 685 million cases of acute gastroenteritis annually, including about 200 million in children under five, and is responsible for roughly 200,000 deaths per year, mostly in the developing world and among the elderly and immunocompromised.

Outbreaks frequently occur in closed and semi-closed settings such as hospitals, schools, nursing homes and cruise ships. The virus is named after Norwalk, Ohio, where an outbreak in 1968 led to its identification in 1972 by Albert Kapikian, who visualised the particle by immune electron microscopy.

Prevention and treatment

There is no specific antiviral treatment; management is supportive, principally oral rehydration. Prevention rests on hand washing with soap and water (alcohol-based sanitisers are relatively ineffective against the non-enveloped virus) and disinfection of contaminated surfaces with chlorine-based agents.
