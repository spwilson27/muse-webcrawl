Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health

Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health, 497 U.S. 261 (1990), was a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court involving the right to die. It was the first "right to die" case to reach the nation's highest court, and it concerned whether a competent person's, or a family's on behalf of an incompetent person's, right to refuse life-sustaining treatment was protected by the United States Constitution.

Background

Nancy Beth Cruzan (July 20, 1957 – December 26, 1990) was rendered comatose and in a persistent vegetative state after a car accident on January 11, 1983. Surgeons implanted a feeding tube at the request of her husband, but by 1988 her parents, Joe and Joyce Cruzan, asked hospital employees to terminate artificial hydration and nutrition so that she could be allowed to die. The hospital refused without a court order, and the family filed suit.

A Missouri trial court ruled in the family's favor, finding a constitutional right to refuse treatment. The Missouri Supreme Court reversed, holding that no one could refuse treatment for another person absent clear and convincing evidence of the patient's wishes, expressed in formal terms or while competent. The United States Supreme Court agreed to hear the case.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision, the Court upheld the Missouri Supreme Court. Chief Justice William Rehnquist, writing for the majority, assumed for purposes of the case that a competent person has a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause in refusing unwanted medical treatment, including artificial hydration and nutrition. However, the Court held that an incompetent person is not able to exercise that right personally, and that a state could constitutionally require clear and convincing evidence of the patient's wishes before allowing a surrogate to withdraw life-sustaining treatment. Missouri's requirement was therefore permissible.

Aftermath

After the Supreme Court decision, the Cruzans gathered additional evidence of Nancy's wishes, including testimony from former coworkers. A Missouri judge found clear and convincing evidence and authorized removal of the feeding tube. Protests and legal challenges followed, but the tube was removed on December 14, 1990, and Nancy Cruzan died on December 26, 1990, at age 33. The case spurred enormous public interest in advance directives, living wills, and health care proxies, and Congress passed the Patient Self-Determination Act of 1990 in its wake.
