Jacobean drama

Jacobean drama is the theatre of England during the reign of King James I (1603–1625), continuing into the Caroline era up to the closing of the theatres in 1642. It developed out of Elizabethan drama and is generally seen as darker, more cynical and more preoccupied with corruption, violence and moral decay than the drama of the preceding reign — though it also produced some of the most celebrated plays in English.

Major figures

The period opened with the greatest works of William Shakespeare: the major tragedies Othello, King Lear, Macbeth and Antony and Cleopatra, the "problem plays", and the late romances such as The Winter's Tale and The Tempest were all Jacobean works. Ben Jonson perfected the comedy of humours in Volpone (1606), The Alchemist (1610) and Bartholomew Fair (1614), and, with designer Inigo Jones, developed the court masque.

Revenge tragedy and city comedy

Jacobean tragedy is famous for its bleak masterpieces of revenge and intrigue: Thomas Middleton's The Revenger's Tragedy and (with William Rowley) The Changeling; John Webster's The White Devil and The Duchess of Malfi (c. 1613–14); Cyril Tourneur's plays; and John Ford's 'Tis Pity She's a Whore in the Caroline period. Alongside them flourished "city comedy" — Middleton's A Chaste Maid in Cheapside, Dekker and Middleton's The Roaring Girl — satirising London merchants and gallants, and the romantic tragicomedy of Beaumont and Fletcher, whose collaborations dominated the repertory for a generation.

Context

The King's Men, Shakespeare's company, performed at the Globe and, from 1608, the indoor Blackfriars theatre, whose higher prices and intimate staging suited the period's taste for intrigue and spectacle. Drama was licensed by the Master of the Revels and could be dangerous: plays touching on politics, religion or living monarchs risked suppression. The era ended abruptly in 1642 when Parliament closed the theatres at the outbreak of the Civil War.
