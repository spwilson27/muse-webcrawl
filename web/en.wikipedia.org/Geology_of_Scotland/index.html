Geology of Scotland

The geology of Scotland is unusually varied for a country of its size, and Scotland has played a foundational role in the development of geology as a science. The country's rocks record the assembly of fragments of several ancient continents, and it was in Scotland that James Hutton recognised the immensity of geological time from unconformities such as the famous section at Siccar Point.

The Scottish Highlands, built from some of the oldest rocks in Europe.

Terranes

Scotland is divided into five distinct crustal blocks, or terranes, separated by great faults. From north to south these are the Hebridean Terrane, the Northern Highlands Terrane, the Grampian Terrane, the Midland Valley Terrane, and the Southern Uplands Terrane. The Great Glen Fault and the Highland Boundary Fault mark two of the most prominent boundaries.

History

The Lewisian gneiss of the northwest, up to three billion years old, is among the oldest rock in Europe. The Caledonian orogeny, culminating about 430 million years ago, united the northern terranes (once part of Laurentia) with the southern terranes (once part of Avalonia) along the Iapetus Suture. Later events included Carboniferous coal deposition in the Midland Valley, and Palaeogene volcanism on Skye and Mull as the North Atlantic opened.

Glaciation

During the Pleistocene, ice sheets repeatedly covered Scotland, carving the glens, lochs, and corries of the Highlands and depositing the drumlins of the lowlands. The landscape that emerged about 11,500 years ago remains strongly glacial in character.
