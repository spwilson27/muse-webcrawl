The Battle of the Somme, also known as the Somme offensive, was a major battle of the First World War fought by the armies of the British Empire and the French Third Republic against the German Empire. It took place between 1 July and 18 November 1916 on both sides of the upper reaches of the river Somme in France and remains one of the largest and bloodiest battles in history.

Background

The offensive was planned as part of a coordinated Allied strategy for 1916 and to relieve pressure on the French army under attack at Verdun. The British sector was assigned to General Henry Rawlinson's Fourth Army, largely composed of volunteer "Pals battalions" of Kitchener's New Army.

The battle

After a week-long artillery bombardment, the infantry attacked on 1 July 1916. On the northern part of the front the assault failed disastrously against intact German defences; the British Army suffered about 57,470 casualties that day, including 19,240 killed — the worst day in its history. French forces in the south made better progress. Fighting continued for months in a series of subsidiary attacks, with tanks used for the first time at Flers–Courcelette in September. The battle ended in November mud, with the Allies having advanced at most about 10 kilometres (6 miles).

Aftermath

Total casualties exceeded one million men. The Somme relieved Verdun and wore down the German army, and German officers later called it "the muddy grave of the German field army." In British memory the battle, above all its first day, has become a symbol of the futility and cost of trench warfare.
