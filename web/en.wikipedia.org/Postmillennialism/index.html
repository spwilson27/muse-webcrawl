Postmillennialism

In Christian eschatology, postmillennialism is an interpretation of chapter 20 of the Book of Revelation which sees Christ's second coming as occurring after (Latin post-) the "Millennium", a golden age in which the gospel will have progressively transformed the world. It is one of three main millennial views, alongside premillennialism (Christ returns before the Millennium) and amillennialism (the Millennium is symbolic of the present church age).

Beliefs

Postmillennialists hold that the kingdom of God will expand throughout the world through preaching, conversion, and the gradual Christianisation of culture, so that righteousness, peace, and prosperity will increasingly prevail before Christ returns to a substantially Christianised world. The view is optimistic about the success of the Great Commission and typically rests on a preterist or idealist reading of prophetic texts. It differs from premillennialism in not expecting a sharp catastrophic break between the present age and the kingdom, and it does not involve a rapture of the church before a tribulation period.

History

Ideas resembling postmillennialism appear in Eusebius and were developed by Puritan and Reformed theologians. Jonathan Edwards, the American theologian of the First Great Awakening, was a notable proponent, and the view was widespread among 19th-century evangelicals and was associated with the Social Gospel and missionary optimism. Its popularity declined after the two world wars damaged confidence in inevitable progress, and dispensational premillennialism became dominant in much of American evangelicalism. In the later 20th century postmillennialism was revived within Reformed circles by writers such as Rousas John Rushdoony and the Christian Reconstruction movement, as well as by theologians like Kenneth Gentry.
