The Troubles (Irish: Na Trioblóidí) was an ethno-nationalist conflict in Northern Ireland that lasted about 30 years, from the late 1960s to 1998. It is also known as the Northern Ireland conflict. The conflict pitted mainly Protestant unionists/loyalists, who wished Northern Ireland to remain part of the United Kingdom, against mainly Catholic nationalists/republicans, who sought a united Ireland.

Background

Northern Ireland was created in 1921 when Ireland was partitioned. Its unionist-dominated government was accused of discrimination against the Catholic minority in housing, employment and voting. A civil rights movement in the late 1960s met loyalist opposition and heavy-handed policing; rioting in Derry and Belfast in 1969 led the British government to deploy troops, initially welcomed by many Catholics.

The conflict

The Provisional IRA emerged in 1969–70 and waged an armed campaign against British security forces, while loyalist paramilitaries such as the UVF and UDA attacked republicans and Catholic civilians. Internment without trial (1971) and Bloody Sunday (30 January 1972), when British soldiers shot 26 unarmed civil rights protesters in Derry, killing 14, sharply escalated the violence. Direct rule from London was imposed in 1972. The conflict also saw hunger strikes in 1981, in which Bobby Sands and nine others died, and attacks in England and elsewhere in Europe. The security forces were themselves accused of collusion with loyalists.

Peace process

The Anglo-Irish Agreement (1985) and the Downing Street Declaration (1993) paved the way for paramilitary ceasefires in 1994. The Good Friday Agreement of 10 April 1998 established a power-sharing assembly in Belfast, cross-border institutions, and the principles of consent and decommissioning; the IRA completed decommissioning in 2005. Sporadic dissident violence has continued, but the agreement is widely regarded as having ended the Troubles.
