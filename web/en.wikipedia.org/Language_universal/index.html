Language universal

A language universal is a pattern that occurs systematically across all natural human languages. Universals range from absolute properties — all languages have consonants and vowels, pronouns, and ways of forming questions — to statistical tendencies, such as the strong preference for subject–object–verb or subject–verb–object word orders. The study of universals is central to linguistic typology and to theories of how language is acquired and structured in the mind.

Greenberg's universals

Joseph Greenberg's 1963 study of 30 languages established the modern typological approach, proposing 45 universals, many implicational: for example, if a language has verb–subject–object order, it always has prepositions rather than postpositions. Greenberg identified six basic word orders with strong statistical skew, noting that dominant orders imply other features, such as the placement of adjectives and genitives.

Universal Grammar

Noam Chomsky and the generative tradition interpret universals as evidence of an innate Universal Grammar: principles common to all languages, with parameters set by exposure (such as head-first versus head-last ordering). From this view, recursion and structure-dependence of rules are candidate design features of the human language faculty. Critics, including many typologists and usage-based linguists, argue that apparent universals can arise from general cognition, communication pressures, and shared ancestry, and cite exceptions such as the debated case of Pirahã recursion.

Types of universals

Scholars distinguish absolute universals (true of every language, e.g. all spoken languages distinguish nouns and verbs, though even this is debated for some languages), statistical universals (strong tendencies), and implicational universals linking two properties. Semantic universals include shared basic colour-term hierarchies described by Berlin and Kay. Sign languages show that universals transcend the speech modality.
