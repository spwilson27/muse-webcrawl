Corvidae is a cosmopolitan family of passerine birds comprising the crows, ravens, rooks, jackdaws, jays, magpies, treepies, choughs, and nutcrackers. With roughly 130 species, corvids range from small jays to the ravens, which are among the largest songbirds. They are renowned for their intelligence, adaptability, and complex social behaviour.

Characteristics

Corvids are medium-to-large, stoutly built birds with strong bills and feet. Many are black or black-and-white, but the jays include vividly coloured species. They are omnivorous and opportunistic feeders, taking insects, carrion, eggs, seeds, and human food waste, which has allowed many species to thrive alongside people. Several species cache food for later recovery, remembering thousands of hiding places.

Intelligence

Corvids, together with parrots, are considered the most intelligent birds. Experiments have shown capacities for tool manufacture and use (notably in the New Caledonian crow), causal reasoning, planning for the future, and social cognition such as recognising individual humans. Their brains are large relative to body size, and their forebrains contain neuron counts comparable to those of some primates.

Distribution and culture

The family occurs on every continent except Antarctica, with the greatest diversity in Asia and the Americas. Corvids figure prominently in mythology and folklore worldwide — ravens serve as messengers of Odin in Norse myth and as tricksters in the traditions of the Pacific Northwest, while crows and magpies are omens, lucky or unlucky, across Eurasia.
