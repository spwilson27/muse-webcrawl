Karai Senryū

Karai Senryū (Japanese: 柄井 川柳, 1718 – 30 October 1790), real name Karai Hachiemon, was a Japanese poet and judge of haikai poetry during the Edo period. He is best known as the founder and namesake of senryū, the short comic and satirical verse form that shares the 5-7-5 syllabic structure of haiku but treats human foibles with humour rather than focusing on nature.

Life and career

Born in Edo (now Tokyo), Karai served as a tenja — a judge or point-master — at haikai gatherings, where he evaluated verses submitted by contestants. From 1757 he conducted famous judging sessions at which he selected winning verses and awarded points. The verses he chose tended to be witty, irreverent observations of everyday life, in contrast to the more formal standards of haikai.

The Senryū form

Collections of the verses he selected were published as Haifū Yanagidaru ("Haiku-style willow barrel"), beginning in 1765. Over time the comic verses themselves came to be called senryū after their selector's pen name. Unlike haiku, senryū do not require a seasonal reference (kigo) and often satirise human nature, vanity, and social pretension.

Legacy

Karai Senryū's name became inseparably linked with the form he championed; senryū remains a popular genre in Japan, with newspaper columns and contests devoted to it. His grave is at the temple Ryūhō-ji in Tokyo.
