The Treaty of Perth, signed on 2 July 1266, ended hostilities between Norway, under King Magnus VI, and Scotland, under King Alexander III, over sovereignty of the Hebrides and the Isle of Man.

Background

The Hebrides and Man had been part of the Norwegian realm since the Viking Age, but Scottish kings pressed steadily westward during the thirteenth century. In 1263 King Haakon IV of Norway led a large fleet to Scotland to reassert his authority; after inconclusive fighting around the Battle of Largs and a storm-damaged withdrawal, Haakon died at Kirkwall in Orkney that December. His son Magnus, preferring peace, opened negotiations.

Terms

Norway ceded the Hebrides and the Isle of Man to Scotland in return for a payment of 4,000 marks sterling and an annual payment — the "Annual of Norway" — of 100 marks, which was paid irregularly and eventually commuted. Scotland recognised Norwegian sovereignty over Orkney and Shetland, which remained Scandinavian for another two centuries until they too passed to Scotland in 1468–69.

Legacy

The treaty fixed the western boundary of the Scottish kingdom and is one of the earliest surviving Scottish diplomatic instruments; the original is held in the National Records of Scotland.
