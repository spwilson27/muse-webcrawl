Inca mythology is the body of myths and beliefs of the Inca Empire and the Andean peoples it incorporated. Centred on a pantheon of nature deities headed by the creator Viracocha and the sun god Inti, it explained the origins of the world, the Inca dynasty and the peoples of the Andes, and it was woven into the state's calendar of festivals, sacrifice and ancestor veneration.

The pantheon

Viracocha was the remote creator who fashioned the world, humanity and the other gods, and then withdrew. Inti, the sun, was the divine ancestor of the Sapa Inca, whose rulers claimed direct descent from him; his temple, the Coricancha in Cusco, was the richest shrine in the empire, its walls once sheeted in gold. His sister-wife Mama Quilla governed the moon and the calendar. Pachamama, the earth mother, received offerings of food and chicha from farmers. Other major gods included Illapa, lord of thunder and weather, and the sea goddess Mama Cocha.

Beyond the great gods, the landscape itself was sacred: mountains, springs, stones and places of origin were huacas — shrines and sacred objects numbering in the thousands, organised in and around Cusco along ritual lines called ceques.

Creation and origin myths

In the best-known origin story, Viracocha created the sun, moon and stars at Lake Titicaca, then fashioned peoples and sent them into the world through caves, springs and rocks — their places of emergence (pacarinas). In the Cusco valley's dynastic myth, the first Inca, Manco Cápac, and his sister-wife Mama Ocllo emerged from the cave of Pacaritambo (or, in another version, from Lake Titicaca) bearing a golden staff; where the staff sank into the earth, they founded Cusco.

Cosmos and ritual

The Inca cosmos had three realms: Hanan Pacha, the upper world of the gods; Kay Pacha, this world; and Uku Pacha, the inner world of the dead and of fertility. The dead themselves remained active: the mummified bodies of emperors (mallqui) were housed, fed, consulted and carried in procession, and their estates continued to support their households. Ritual life included great solar festivals such as Inti Raymi, divination, and offerings that could extend, on occasions of crisis, to the capacocha sacrifice of children on high mountain shrines, where their frozen remains have been found by archaeologists.

Sources and survival

The Inca kept records with knotted cords (quipu) rather than writing, so the mythology is known chiefly through Spanish chroniclers such as Cieza de León, Juan de Betanzos and Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, and through Quechua-language works like the Huarochirí Manuscript. Suppressed after the conquest, many beliefs survived in syncretism with Catholicism and in modern Andean practice, and festivals such as Inti Raymi are celebrated in Cusco today.
