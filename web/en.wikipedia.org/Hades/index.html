Hades (Greek: ᾍδης, translit. Háidēs) was the ancient Greek chthonic god of the dead and the king of the underworld, with which his name became synonymous. He was the eldest son of Cronus and Rhea, and with his brothers Zeus and Poseidon defeated their father's generation of gods, the Titans, receiving the underworld as his lot when the three divided the world between them. His wife is Persephone, whom he abducted from her mother Demeter — a myth that explained the changing of the seasons.

Despite his modern associations, Hades was not considered evil in ancient Greek religion: he was strict and unyielding rather than malevolent, maintaining the balance of life and death. He was rarely worshipped and seldom depicted in art, partly because the Greeks were reluctant to utter his name — he was often addressed euphemistically as Plouton ("the Wealthy One"), since the riches of the earth, including harvests and precious metals, come from below.

Mythology

Swallowed at birth by Cronus along with his siblings, Hades was freed when Zeus forced their father to disgorge them. In the Titanomachy the Cyclopes gave Hades his famous helm of invisibility. Thereafter he ruled the dead from his palace in the underworld, aided by the three-headed dog Cerberus, and only rarely left his realm — most famously to carry off Persephone in his chariot. Heroes who descended to his kingdom include Heracles, who wrestled Cerberus, and Orpheus, who nearly won back his wife Eurydice with music.

The underworld

The realm of Hades was imagined as a gloomy region across the river Styx, where the ferryman Charon conveyed the dead for the price of a coin placed in the mouth. Its regions included the Asphodel Meadows for ordinary souls, Tartarus for the damned, and the Elysian Fields for the blessed. The name "Hades" was later used in the Septuagint to render the Hebrew Sheol, and in Christian usage for the abode of the dead.
