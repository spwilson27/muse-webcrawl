David Ricardo (18 April 1772 – 11 September 1823) was a British political economist, politician, and member of the Parliament of Great Britain and Ireland. He is credited with systematising economics in the scientific manner and is, along with Adam Smith and Thomas Malthus, considered one of the founders of classical economics.

Born in London into a Jewish family of Portuguese origin, Ricardo began work at his father's stockbrokerage at the age of fourteen. He made a large fortune speculating on the outcome of the Battle of Waterloo and retired from business in his early forties, thereafter devoting himself to study, writing, and politics.

Life

Ricardo was the third of seventeen children of Abigail and Abraham Israel Ricardo, a successful stockbroker who had emigrated from the Netherlands. After breaking with his family at twenty-one over his marriage to Priscilla Anne Wilkinson, a Quaker, Ricardo set up his own business and amassed sufficient wealth to purchase Gatcombe Park in Gloucestershire. In 1819 he entered Parliament as member for Portarlington, a seat he held until his death from an ear infection in 1823.

Works

Ricardo's first published work was a series of letters in the Morning Chronicle in 1809 on the depreciation of bank notes. His most famous work, On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817), contains his most celebrated contributions, including the theory of comparative advantage, the theory of differential rent, and an analytical treatment of the distribution of income between wages, profits, and rent.

Theory of comparative advantage

In perhaps his best-known argument, Ricardo demonstrated that two countries can both gain from trade even if one is more efficient at producing every good, provided each specialises in the good in which it has a comparative advantage — that is, the good it produces at lower opportunity cost. His example of English cloth and Portuguese wine remains the standard textbook illustration of the gains from trade and underlies modern theories of international trade.

Theory of rent and value

Ricardo argued that rent arises from differences in the fertility of land: as population grows, inferior land is brought into cultivation, and the price of corn is set by the cost of production on the least fertile (marginal) land in use, with more fertile land earning a differential rent. On value, he held that the relative prices of commodities are governed predominantly by the quantities of labour required to produce them, though he acknowledged modifications caused by differing capital structures — a puzzle that occupied him until his death.

Legacy

Ricardo's rigorous, abstract deductive method became the model for much subsequent economic analysis. His ideas influenced Karl Marx's labour theory of value, the marginalist school, and the modern concept of Ricardian equivalence, named for his suggestion that government deficits financed by debt may not stimulate spending because taxpayers anticipate future taxes.
