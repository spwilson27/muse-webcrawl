Jade

Jade is a mineral used as jewellery or for ornaments, applying to two different silicate materials: nephrite, a silicate of calcium and magnesium of the amphibole group, and jadeite, a silicate of sodium and aluminium of the pyroxene group. It is most valued in its green forms and has been worked for thousands of years, above all in China and Mesoamerica.

History

Jade working dates to the Neolithic period. In China, jade (玉, yù) was carved into ritual objects such as bi discs and cong tubes, ornaments and burial suits, and held profound cultural significance associated with virtue, purity and immortality; the Liangzhu and Hongshan cultures produced notable early jades. In pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, jadeite from the Motagua valley of Guatemala was the most precious material of the Maya and Olmec, valued above gold. Jade was also worked by the Māori of New Zealand (pounamu), and in Neolithic Europe for axe heads.

Properties

Jade is exceptionally tough because of its interlocking crystal structure, making it suitable for tools and weapons as well as ornaments. Nephrite ranges from creamy white to dark green; jadeite occurs in green, lavender, white and other colours, the finest translucent emerald-green material being known as "imperial jade" and coming chiefly from Myanmar (Burma).

Modern trade

Myanmar supplies most of the world's gem-quality jadeite, while nephrite is mined in Canada, Russia, China and elsewhere. Jade remains a major luxury commodity in Chinese markets, and imitations and treated stones are common, making gemmological identification important.
