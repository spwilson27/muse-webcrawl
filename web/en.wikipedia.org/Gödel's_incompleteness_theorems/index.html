Gödel's incompleteness theorems

Gödel's incompleteness theorems are two theorems of mathematical logic, each of which demonstrates the inherent limitations of every formal axiomatic system containing basic arithmetic. These results, published by Kurt Gödel in 1931, are important both in mathematical logic and in the philosophy of mathematics.

The first incompleteness theorem states that no consistent system of axioms whose theorems can be listed by an effective procedure (i.e. an algorithm) is capable of proving all truths about the arithmetic of natural numbers. For any such consistent formal system, there will always be statements about natural numbers that are true, but that are unprovable within the system. The second incompleteness theorem, an extension of the first, shows that the system cannot demonstrate its own consistency.

Background

Gödel's theorems were a response to the foundational programme associated with David Hilbert, which sought a complete and consistent axiomatization of all of mathematics. Gödel showed that such a system, if consistent and strong enough to include elementary arithmetic, could not prove all truths and could not establish its own consistency.

Gödel numbering and the proof

The proof proceeds by assigning to each formula and proof a unique natural number — a Gödel number — so that statements of arithmetic can encode claims about provability. Gödel then constructed a self-referential sentence G that effectively asserts "G is not provable in this system". If the system proved G, it would be inconsistent; if it proved the negation of G, it would also be inconsistent (under the original ω-consistency assumption, later weakened by Rosser). Hence, if the system is consistent, G is true but unprovable.

Second theorem

By formalizing the argument of the first theorem inside the system itself, Gödel showed that a statement expressing the system's consistency is itself unprovable unless the system is inconsistent. Thus no sufficiently strong, consistent system can prove its own consistency.

Implications

The theorems ended the search for a single complete axiomatization of mathematics and are frequently invoked in discussions of the philosophy of mind, the limits of computation, and the nature of mathematical truth. Related results include Tarski's undefinability theorem and Turing's halting problem.
