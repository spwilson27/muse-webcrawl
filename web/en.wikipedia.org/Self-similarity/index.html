In mathematics, a self-similar object is exactly or approximately similar to a part of itself; that is, the whole has the same shape as one or more of the parts. Many objects in the real world are statistically self-similar: parts are statistically similar to the whole. Self-similarity is a defining property of a fractal, and it is expressed by power laws such as the relationship between an object's apparent size and the scale at which it is measured.

Definitions

A compact topological space X is self-similar if there exists a finite set S indexing a family of non-surjective homeomorphisms whose union of images is X. If the maps are contractions with the same ratio r, X is the unique attractor of an iterated function system, and its self-similarity (Hausdorff) dimension is log |S| / log (1/r). The Sierpiński triangle (dimension log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585) and the Koch snowflake (log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.262) are canonical exact examples. Weaker forms include quasi-self-similarity, as in the Mandelbrot set, and statistical self-similarity, which holds for coastlines: Benoit Mandelbrot showed that the measured length of a coast grows as finer rulers are used, following a power law with a fractional exponent.

In nature and applications

Self-similar structures recur in Romanesco broccoli and fern fronds, branching blood vessels and bronchial trees, river drainage networks, lightning bolts, and mountain ranges. In technology and science, self-similarity appears in fractal antennas, image compression, financial time series, network traffic, and the distribution of galaxies. It is distinguished from self-determination, an unrelated legal and political principle.
