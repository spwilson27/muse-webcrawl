Internet censorship in Russia

Internet censorship in Russia is enforced by federal law and administered principally by Roskomnadzor, the federal communications regulator. Censorship expanded steadily from the early 2010s and intensified dramatically after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, when most independent media and major foreign social platforms were blocked.

Legal framework

A turning point was the 2012 law creating a unified register of prohibited websites, ostensibly aimed at child protection, drug promotion and suicide content. Later measures included the 2015 data-localisation law requiring Russians' personal data to be stored on servers in Russia, the 2016 "Yarovaya law" mandating data retention and encryption assistance, and the 2019 "sovereign internet" law, which gave the state tools to isolate the Russian segment of the internet (Runet) and centralised traffic control at Roskomnadzor through deep packet inspection equipment installed at providers.

Blocked content

Blocked material has included opposition websites, the professional network LinkedIn (blocked in 2016 over data localisation), the messaging app Telegram (blocked unsuccessfully from 2018 to 2020), and Wikipedia articles — Wikipedia itself was repeatedly threatened with blocking. Laws against "extremism" and, from 2022, against "false information" about the armed forces were used to suppress war reporting.

After 2022

Following the invasion of Ukraine, Facebook, Instagram and Twitter were blocked, Meta was designated an extremist organisation, and thousands of websites — including nearly all remaining independent Russian media — were added to the register. Access to VPN services has been progressively restricted, and video platforms such as YouTube have been throttled, pushing users towards state-favoured domestic services.
