Sibylline Books

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sibylline Books (Latin: Libri Sibyllini) were a collection of oracular utterances, said to have been purchased from a sibyl by the last king of Rome, Tarquinius Superbus, and consulted by the Roman Senate at times of crisis throughout the Republic and Empire. They are distinct from the Sibylline Oracles, a later collection of Jewish and Christian prophecies.

Legend of acquisition

According to legend, the Cumaean Sibyl offered nine books of prophecy to King Tarquin. When he refused her price, she burned three and offered the remaining six at the same price; refused again, she burned three more, and Tarquin bought the final three for the original price of all nine. The books, written in Greek hexameters, were kept in a stone chest beneath the Temple of Jupiter Capitolinus.

Use and custodians

The books were guarded by a college of priests — first two men (duumviri sacris faciundis), later ten (decemviri), and finally fifteen (quindecimviri) — and could be consulted only by order of the Senate, typically in response to plagues, defeats or portents. Their advice usually prescribed new rites, temples or the introduction of foreign cults, such as that of the Great Mother (Cybele) in 204 BC.

Fate

The original books were destroyed when the Capitoline temple burned in 83 BC. A replacement collection was assembled from oracles gathered across the Mediterranean, but it was eventually burned, probably under the Christian general Stilicho around AD 405. No genuine fragments survive.
