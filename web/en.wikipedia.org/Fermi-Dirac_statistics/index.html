Fermi–Dirac statistics

Fermi–Dirac statistics is a type of quantum statistics that applies to the physics of a system consisting of many non-interacting, identical particles that obey the Pauli exclusion principle. It is named after Enrico Fermi and Paul Dirac, who each independently derived it in 1926. Particles that obey Fermi–Dirac statistics are called fermions; they have half-integer spin and include electrons, protons, neutrons, and quarks.

The distribution

The average number of particles in a single-particle state of energy ε is given by the Fermi–Dirac distribution:

n̄(ε) = 1 / ( e(ε−μ)/(kBT) + 1 )

where μ is the chemical potential, kB is Boltzmann's constant, and T is the absolute temperature. At zero temperature the distribution becomes a step function: all states below the Fermi energy EF are fully occupied and all above are empty. The +1 in the denominator encodes the exclusion principle — no state can hold more than one particle.

Consequences

Fermi–Dirac statistics explains the electronic heat capacity of metals (only electrons near the Fermi surface contribute, resolving a failure of classical theory), the pressure of a degenerate Fermi gas that supports white dwarf stars against gravitational collapse, and the behavior of electrons in semiconductors, where the related Fermi level governs device physics. In the high-temperature, low-density limit, the distribution reduces to the classical Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.

Comparison

Fermi–Dirac statistics contrasts with Bose–Einstein statistics, which applies to bosons (integer-spin particles) that may all occupy the same quantum state. Together with the spin–statistics theorem, the two distributions partition the particle world into fermions and bosons.
