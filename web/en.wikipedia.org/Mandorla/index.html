Mandorla

A mandorla is an almond-shaped frame that surrounds the full figure of a sacred person in Christian art, especially in medieval and Byzantine painting, sculpture, and illuminated manuscripts. The word is Italian for "almond"; the shape is also known as a vesica piscis ("fish bladder"), formed geometrically by the intersection of two circles whose centres lie on each other's circumference.

Use in Christian art

The mandorla most commonly encloses Christ in Majesty (Maiestas Domini), seated on a throne or rainbow and displaying the wounds of the Passion or holding the Book of Life, as in countless Romanesque tympana over church portals, apse paintings, and Last Judgment scenes. It is also frequently used to surround the Virgin Mary, particularly in images of the Assumption and the Dormition, and occasionally other saints shown in glory.

The mandorla signifies divine light and uncreated glory: unlike a halo, which frames only the head and signifies sanctity, the mandorla envelops the whole body, marking the figure as a manifestation of the divine and situating it outside ordinary space and time. Mandorlas are often filled with concentric bands of colour, stars, cherubim, or the symbols of the four Evangelists, and their borders may be carried or supported by angels.

History

The device developed in Early Christian and Byzantine art from the round clipeus (shield portrait) and the aureole of light; by the Carolingian and Romanesque periods the almond-shaped full-body frame had become standard for depictions of Christ in glory. It remained prominent through the Gothic era — examples include the Royal Portal at Chartres Cathedral and the tympanum of the Last Judgment — before becoming rarer in Renaissance art, which favoured naturalistic clouds and glories of putti, although it survived in icons and in provincial and devotional imagery. The motif enjoyed a revival in the nineteenth century among the Pre-Raphaelites and in Arts and Crafts design.

Analogous forms

Similar whole-body aureoles occur in other traditions: Buddhist art surrounds Buddhas and bodhisattvas with flaming or petal-shaped mandorlas (as in the sculptures of Gandhara and Japan), and Hindu images of deities often place the figure within a pointed-arch prabhavali or halo of flames. Art historians debate how far the Christian mandorla derives from these older eastern forms.
