Alfred North Whitehead OM FRS FBA (15 February 1861 – 30 December 1947) was an English mathematician and philosopher. He is best known as the defining figure of the philosophical school known as process philosophy, which today has found application in a wide variety of disciplines, including ecology, theology, education, physics, biology, economics, and psychology.

Life and career

Whitehead was born in Ramsgate, Kent, and educated at Sherborne School and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he became a fellow in 1884. He collaborated with his former pupil Bertrand Russell on the three-volume Principia Mathematica (1910–1913), one of the most influential works in modern logic. In 1914 he became Professor of Applied Mathematics at Imperial College London, and in 1924 he moved to the United States to take up a professorship in philosophy at Harvard University, where he remained for the rest of his life.

Philosophy

In his later work Whitehead developed a comprehensive metaphysical system centred on "actual occasions" of experience rather than enduring material substances. His magnum opus, Process and Reality (1929), based on his Gifford Lectures, introduced terms such as "prehension" and "concrescence" and argued that reality itself is processual. He is also remembered for the remark that the European philosophical tradition "consists of a series of footnotes to Plato".

Legacy

Whitehead's thought influenced process theology, constructive postmodernism, and thinkers such as Gilles Deleuze. Centres and societies devoted to his work exist in the United States, Europe, and China.
