TypeRadio navigation / precision approach aid
ComponentsLocalizer, glide slope (glide path), marker beacons / DME

The instrument landing system (ILS) is a ground-based radio navigation system that provides precision lateral and vertical guidance to an aircraft approaching a runway, enabling landings in poor visibility — the most widely used precision instrument approach system in the world. It consists of two main transmissions: the localizer, which guides the pilot left–right along the extended runway centreline, and the glide slope (glide path), which guides the aircraft down the descent path, usually 3°. Distance information was historically provided by marker beacons and is now typically supplied by DME collocated with the glide slope.

How it works

The localizer antenna array, sited beyond the far end of the runway, radiates two overlapping lobes modulated at 90 Hz (left) and 150 Hz (right) in the VHF band. The aircraft receiver compares the depths of modulation: equality means the aircraft is on the centreline. The glide slope station, sited beside the touchdown zone, uses the same principle in the UHF band with the 90 Hz lobe above and 150 Hz below the glide path. The cockpit instrument — traditionally a course deviation indicator with two needles, now usually integrated into electronic flight displays and flight directors — displays both deviations, and an autopilot can couple to the signals to fly an automatic approach and, in suitably equipped aircraft, an autoland.

Categories

ILS approaches are classified by how low they permit descent:

  • Category I — decision height not below 200 ft (60 m), runway visual range (RVR) not less than 550 m.
  • Category II — decision height below 200 ft but not below 100 ft, RVR not less than 300 m.
  • Category IIIA — decision height below 100 ft (or none), RVR not less than 175 m.
  • Category IIIB — RVR between 50 and 175 m.
  • Category IIIC — no decision height and no RVR limitation (no airport currently operates routine Cat IIIC arrivals, as taxiing would also be impossible in zero visibility).

Limitations and successors

ILS signals are vulnerable to interference and multipath reflections from buildings, terrain and taxiing aircraft, requiring protected "critical areas"; only one ILS can serve a given runway direction, and the 40-channel plan limits availability at busy airports. The microwave landing system (MLS) was intended as a successor but was largely overtaken by satellite navigation: GBAS (ground-based augmentation system) approaches, known as GLS, provide ILS-like guidance from a single airport installation to all runways, and satellite LPV approaches now offer comparable minima at many smaller airfields. Nevertheless, ILS remains the backbone of low-visibility operations at major airports worldwide.
