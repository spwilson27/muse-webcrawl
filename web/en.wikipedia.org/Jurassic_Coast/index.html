Jurassic Coast

Location: Dorset and East Devon, southern England
Length: 96 miles (154 km)
Designation: UNESCO World Heritage Site (2001)
Geological span: Triassic, Jurassic, and Cretaceous periods — about 185 million years

The Jurassic Coast is a stretch of coastline on the English Channel in southern England, running 96 miles (154 km) from Exmouth in East Devon to Studland Bay in Dorset. Its cliffs expose an almost continuous sequence of rock formations spanning the Triassic, Jurassic, and Cretaceous periods — some 185 million years of Earth's history. It was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001, England's first natural World Heritage Site.

Geology

Because the strata were tilted and then eroded, a walk along the coast is a walk through time: the red Triassic sandstones and mudstones around Exmouth give way eastward to the Jurassic limestones and shales of Lyme Regis and the Isle of Portland, and finally to the Cretaceous chalk of Studland. Landforms include some of England's most famous coastal features: the natural limestone arch of Durdle Door, the sweeping shingle of Chesil Beach and the Fleet lagoon, the cove at Lulworth, and the landslip coast of the Undercliff near Lyme Regis.

Fossils and Mary Anning

The coast is one of the world's richest fossil localities. At Lyme Regis in the early 19th century, Mary Anning unearthed the first complete ichthyosaur and plesiosaur skeletons recognized by science, finds that transformed understanding of prehistoric life and extinction. Fossils — ammonites, belemnites, and occasionally marine reptiles — continue to be exposed by winter storms and cliff falls, and fossil collecting is a major attraction, governed by a code of conduct to protect the site.

Management and tourism

The South West Coast Path follows the entire shoreline. The site is managed to balance conservation, research, and millions of visitors a year; ongoing coastal erosion, though a hazard, is also what continually refreshes the fossil record that gives the coast its significance.
