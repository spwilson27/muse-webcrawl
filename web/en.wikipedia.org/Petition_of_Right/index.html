Petition of Right

The Petition of Right, passed in 1628, is one of the most famous constitutional documents in English legal history. Presented by Parliament to King Charles I, it set out specific liberties of the subject that the King was prohibited from infringing. Alongside Magna Carta and the Bill of Rights 1689, it forms a cornerstone of the uncodified constitution of the United Kingdom.

Background

Charles I's wars against Spain and France had been financed by a "forced loan" levied without Parliament, and refusers were imprisoned without trial — upheld by the judges in the Five Knights' Case (1627). Soldiers were billeted in private homes, and martial law was imposed on civilians. When Parliament met in 1628 it resolved, under the leadership of Sir Edward Coke, to restate the law rather than legislate anew.

Provisions

The Petition declared that:

  • No tax, loan or benevolence could be levied without the consent of Parliament;
  • No freeman could be imprisoned without cause shown (asserting habeas corpus);
  • Soldiers and sailors could not be billeted in subjects' houses against their will;
  • Commissions of martial law against civilians were unlawful.

Aftermath

Charles assented to the Petition in June 1628 after attempting an evasive answer, but continued his policies; within a year Parliament was dissolved and the Personal Rule began. In the run-up to the Civil War the Petition was repeatedly cited as evidence of the King's bad faith, and its guarantees were re-enacted and extended by later statutes, including the Habeas Corpus Act 1679 and the Bill of Rights.
