Romanesque Revival architecture (also called Norman Revival, or Rundbogenstil — "round-arched style" — in Germany) is a style of building popular in the mid-to-late 19th century, drawing on the Romanesque architecture of the 11th and 12th centuries: round arches, thick walls, small windows, barrel vaults and sturdy towers. It was one of the many historicist revivals of the Victorian era, applied especially to churches, universities, railway stations, town halls and other public buildings. The style is named for medieval "Roman-like" architecture — not for the languages derived from Latin (see Romance languages) nor for Romantic music.

Development

The revival began in the 1840s in Germany and Britain — where it was initially associated with new Anglican and Nonconformist churches — and offered a plainer, more "honest" alternative to the Gothic Revival. Architects valued its massive masonry construction for the new large-span public buildings of the industrial age. In the United States it was widely used for courthouses, libraries and college buildings in the decades after the Civil War.

Richardsonian Romanesque

The style's most influential variant was created by the American architect Henry Hobson Richardson. His Trinity Church, Boston (1872–77), and later works such as the Allegheny County Courthouse in Pittsburgh and the Marshall Field Wholesale Store in Chicago distilled the Romanesque into broad, rock-faced stone masses with deeply recessed round-arched openings and short, weighty columns — the "Richardsonian Romanesque." Widely copied for American railroad stations and public libraries, it faded after about 1900 in favour of Beaux-Arts classicism and the Gothic Revival.
