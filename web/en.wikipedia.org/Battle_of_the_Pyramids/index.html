The Battle of the Pyramids was fought on 21 July 1798 between the French army of Napoleon Bonaparte and the forces of Egypt's ruling Mamluk beys, near the village of Embabeh on the west bank of the Nile, within sight of the Giza pyramids. The French victory opened the way to Cairo and made Napoleon master of Lower Egypt. It is a land battle of the Egyptian campaign, not to be confused with the naval Battle of the Nile fought at Aboukir Bay eleven days later.

Background

Napoleon's expedition had landed at Alexandria on 1 July 1798 and marched on Cairo. The Mamluk rulers Murad Bey and Ibrahim Bey concentrated their forces at Embabeh: Murad's army entrenched on the west bank with the splendid Mamluk horsemen, while Ibrahim watched from the east bank. Napoleon famously addressed his troops before the battle: "Soldiers, from the heights of these pyramids, forty centuries look down upon you."

The battle

Napoleon deployed his divisions in large hollow squares, six ranks deep, with artillery at the corners and baggage in the centre. The Mamluk cavalry—superb individual horsemen but fighting without coordinated tactics—charged the squares repeatedly and were shattered by disciplined musket and cannon fire. A French flanking move against the Embabeh entrenchments rolled up the Mamluk position; thousands of Murad's men drowned trying to swim the Nile. Murad fled to Upper Egypt and Ibrahim to Syria.

Aftermath

Cairo was occupied on 24 July. French rule proved precarious: Nelson's destruction of the fleet at the Nile a week later cut Napoleon off from France, and a Cairo revolt was bloodily suppressed in October. The battle nonetheless became a set-piece of Napoleonic legend and a landmark demonstration of European fire discipline against feudal cavalry.
