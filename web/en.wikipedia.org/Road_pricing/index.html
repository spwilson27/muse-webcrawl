Road pricing

Electronic road pricing gantry

Road pricing (also known as road user charging, distance-based charging or congestion pricing when applied in congested areas) refers to charging motorists directly for using a road or network of roads, in order to manage demand, reduce congestion and raise revenue for transport improvements.

The concept derives from the economic theory of externalities: driving imposes costs on others — delays, pollution, crash risk — that the driver does not otherwise pay. A charge set equal to the marginal external cost is a Pigouvian tax intended to produce a more efficient allocation of road space.

Forms

Road pricing takes several forms: tolls on a specific facility such as a bridge or motorway; cordon charges for entering a defined zone, as in the London congestion charge; area licences; and distance-based charges per kilometre driven, differentiated by time, place and vehicle type.

Examples

Singapore introduced the world's first urban road pricing scheme in 1975, later upgraded to Electronic Road Pricing gantries. London's congestion charge began in 2003, and Stockholm's congestion tax, made permanent in 2007 after a trial and referendum, reduced traffic across the cordon by roughly a fifth. Several United States cities have studied or adopted priced express lanes.

Reception

Public acceptability typically rises after implementation, once benefits are visible, but schemes remain politically contentious: critics raise privacy concerns about vehicle tracking and equity concerns about the burden on low-income drivers.
