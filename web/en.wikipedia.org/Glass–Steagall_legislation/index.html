Glass–Steagall legislation

Glass–Steagall legislation describes four provisions of the United States Banking Act of 1933 that separated commercial banking from investment banking. Named for Senator Carter Glass and Representative Henry B. Steagall, the provisions (sections 16, 20, 21, and 32 of the Act) prohibited commercial banks from underwriting or dealing in securities, barred securities firms from taking deposits, and restricted affiliations and interlocking directorates between the two kinds of institution.

Background and enactment

The legislation was a response to the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the wave of bank failures that followed. Congressional hearings led by Ferdinand Pecora exposed conflicts of interest in which commercial banks had used depositors' funds to support speculative securities activities. The same Banking Act also created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) to insure deposits.

Repeal and aftermath

From the 1960s onward, regulators and courts progressively loosened the separation, and the affiliation restrictions were formally repealed by the Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act of 1999, permitting financial holding companies combining banking, securities, and insurance. Economists and historians continue to debate the role of the repeal in the 2008 financial crisis. In 2010 the Dodd–Frank Act introduced the Volcker Rule, restricting proprietary trading by banks and often described as a partial revival of the Glass–Steagall principle; calls to restore a full separation have recurred in American politics since.
