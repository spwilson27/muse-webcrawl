Narcissus (mythology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Greek mythology, Narcissus (/nɑːrˈsɪsəs/; Ancient Greek: Νάρκισσος, Nárkissos) was a hunter from Thespiae in Boeotia who was known for his beauty and disdain for those who loved him. He is the origin of the term narcissism, a fixation with oneself and one's physical appearance or public perception.

The myth

Narcissus was the son of the river god Cephissus and the nymph Liriope. The seer Tiresias prophesied that the child would live to a ripe old age if he never "came to know himself". As a youth, Narcissus was exceptionally beautiful, and many fell in love with him, but he rejected them all cruelly.

The nymph Echo fell in love with him. Cursed by Hera to repeat only the last words spoken to her, Echo could not declare her love; when Narcissus rejected her, she wasted away until only her voice remained. In the best-known version, told by the Roman poet Ovid in his Metamorphoses, the goddess Nemesis punished Narcissus's cruelty by luring him to a pool, where he saw his own reflection. Not realising it was merely an image, he fell in love with it and, unable to leave, died beside the pool. In his place a flower grew — the narcissus, or daffodil.

Legacy

The myth has inspired countless works of art and literature, including paintings by Caravaggio and Waterhouse, Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray, and the psychoanalytic concept of narcissism developed by Freud and others. The psychological condition narcissistic personality disorder also takes its name from the myth.
