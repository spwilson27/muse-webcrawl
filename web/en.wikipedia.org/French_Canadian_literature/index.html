French Canadian literature

French Canadian literature is the literature written in French by Canadians, overwhelmingly concentrated in Quebec, where it is often simply called Quebec literature. It developed from the chronicles and relations of New France into a distinct national literature expressing the identity of a francophone society within anglophone North America.

Early writing

The earliest texts were explorers' accounts, missionary relations such as the Jesuit Relations, and the history of François-Xavier Garneau in the 1840s, which aimed to give French Canadians a national past after the Durham Report. The nineteenth century produced the roman du terroir, the novel of the land, exemplified by Louis Hémon's Maria Chapdelaine (1914), celebrating rural Catholic life and the survival of the French fact in America (la survivance).

Modern literature

The Quiet Revolution of the 1960s transformed Quebec writing. Authors such as Gabrielle Roy (Bonheur d'occasion, 1945), Anne Hébert, Hubert Aquin, Marie-Claire Blais, and Réjean Ducharme broke with the terroir tradition, exploring urban life, identity, and sovereignty. Playwright Michel Tremblay brought the Montreal working-class dialect joual to the stage in Les Belles-sœurs (1968).

Contemporary writing

Later writers include Dany Laferrière, a member of the Académie française, and Kim Thúy, along with a growing body of Indigenous and immigrant writing in French. French-language literature also exists in Ontario, Acadia, and the West, supported by dedicated publishers and prizes.
