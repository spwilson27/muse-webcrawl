Damnatio memoriae

A defaced portrait, the classic result of damnatio memoriae

Damnatio memoriae (Latin for "condemnation of memory") is a modern term for a form of dishonour in ancient Rome in which the Senate could condemn the memory of a person who had betrayed or disgraced the state. The punishment sought to erase the condemned person from recorded history: names were chiselled out of inscriptions, statues were toppled or re-carved, portraits were defaced, and coins bearing their image were sometimes recalled or countermarked.

Purpose and practice

In Roman culture, memory and reputation after death were central to social identity; to be remembered honourably was a kind of immortality. Damnatio memoriae attacked that immortality directly. Though the phrase itself is a coinage of early modern scholarship (first appearing in a 1689 dissertation), the underlying practices were real and widespread. Measures could include the destruction of images, the erasure of names from public monuments and documents, and prohibitions on the use of a family's names or insignia.

Examples

Notable targets included the emperor Geta, whose images were removed after his murder by his brother Caracalla in 211 AD; the praetorian prefect Sejanus, destroyed after his fall in 31 AD; and the emperor Domitian, condemned by the Senate after his assassination in 96 AD. Earlier precedents existed in pharaonic Egypt, where the names and images of rulers such as Hatshepsut and Akhenaten were systematically erased.

Effectiveness

The punishment was rarely complete: erased names remain legible in damaged inscriptions, and the act of erasure itself often drew attention to the victim. Historians note that damnatio frequently served as political theatre by which a new regime distanced itself from its predecessor.
