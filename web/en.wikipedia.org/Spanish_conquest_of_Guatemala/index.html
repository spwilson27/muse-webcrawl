Spanish conquest of Guatemala

The Spanish conquest of Guatemala was the prolonged campaign by which Spanish conquistadors and their allies incorporated the territory of modern Guatemala into the Spanish Empire, as part of the Spanish colonization of the Americas. Spanish contact began in 1524 with the invasion of the Guatemalan Highlands by Pedro de Alvarado, sent from Mexico by Hernán Cortés, and the last independent Maya kingdom, the Itza of Petén, did not fall until 1697.

The highland campaigns

Alvarado's small force of Spaniards, supported by thousands of Mexican (Tlaxcalan and other) allies, defeated the K'iche' kingdom at the battle of Quetzaltenango in 1524, where the K'iche' commander Tecun Uman was killed — later becoming Guatemala's national hero. The K'iche' capital Utatlán was burned, and Alvarado founded Santiago de los Caballeros, the first colonial capital. The Kaqchikel, initially allies against the K'iche', revolted and waged a guerrilla war until 1530. The Mam, Tz'utujil and other highland peoples were subdued over the following years, amid massacres, forced labour under the encomienda system, and catastrophic population loss from European diseases.

The long resistance of Petén

The lowland Maya of the Petén region resisted much longer. Cortés had marched through in 1525 and met the Itza king at the island capital Nojpetén (Tayasal) on Lake Petén Itzá, but no conquest followed. Franciscan and Dominican friars attempted peaceful conversion, sometimes with tragic results, while the Itza retained independence by playing on Spanish rivalries. Only in March 1697 did a Spanish force under Martín de Urzúa storm Nojpetén, ending the last independent Maya kingdom — 173 years after Alvarado's invasion.

Legacy

Guatemala became part of the Captaincy General of Guatemala within the Viceroyalty of New Spain. The conquest reshaped the region's demography, landholding and culture, and its violence and dispossession remain central to modern debates about indigenous rights; the indigenous account of the conquest survives in documents such as the Lienzo de Quauhquechollan.
