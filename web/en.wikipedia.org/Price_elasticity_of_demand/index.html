Price elasticity of demand

Elastic and inelastic demand curves

The price elasticity of demand is a measure used in economics to show the responsiveness, or elasticity, of the quantity demanded of a good or service to a change in its price when nothing else changes. More precisely, it gives the percentage change in quantity demanded in response to a one percent change in price. It is calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

Price elasticities are almost always negative because of the law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls. When the absolute value of the elasticity is greater than 1, demand is said to be elastic—consumers respond strongly to price changes; when it is less than 1, demand is inelastic. Demand for a good with no substitutes and few competitors, such as gasoline in the short run, tends to be inelastic, whereas demand for a specific brand among many alternatives tends to be highly elastic.

Determinants

Several factors influence elasticity: the availability of close substitutes (the most important factor), whether the good is a necessity or a luxury, how broadly the market is defined, the proportion of income spent on the good, and the time horizon—demand is generally more elastic in the long run, when consumers have time to adjust. Extreme cases include perfectly inelastic demand (elasticity of zero, a vertical demand curve) and perfectly elastic demand (infinite elasticity, a horizontal demand curve).

Applications

Elasticity determines how a price change affects total revenue: with inelastic demand, raising price increases revenue; with elastic demand, lowering price increases revenue. Governments use elasticity estimates when designing taxes—excise taxes are often levied on inelastic goods such as tobacco and fuel—and businesses use them in pricing strategy. The concept was developed by Alfred Marshall in his Principles of Economics (1890) and is related to income elasticity of demand and cross-price elasticity of demand, which measure responsiveness to income and to other goods' prices respectively.
