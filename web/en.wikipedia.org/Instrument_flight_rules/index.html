Instrument flight rules (IFR) are a set of regulations under which a pilot operates an aircraft in weather conditions generally not clear enough for visual flight — known as instrument meteorological conditions (IMC). The pilot flies primarily by reference to instruments in the flight deck, and separation from other aircraft and terrain is maintained with the help of air traffic control.

Overview

Under IFR, pilots must hold an instrument rating and the aircraft must be certified and equipped for instrument flight. Flights operate on filed IFR flight plans, follow published routes, standard instrument departures and arrivals, and fly instrument approach procedures to land at destination airports. IFR operations follow the semicircular cruising-level system, flying odd or even thousands of feet depending on direction of flight.

Instrument procedures

Approach procedures include non-precision approaches (e.g. VOR and RNAV/GPS approaches) and precision approaches such as the Instrument Landing System (ILS), which provides both lateral and vertical guidance to a decision height, often as low as 200 feet above the runway in Category I operations, and lower for Category II and III autoland operations.

History

Blind flying became practical with the development of the artificial horizon and directional gyro by Elmer Sperry and others; Jimmy Doolittle made the first complete blind take-off, flight and landing in 1929. Scheduled airline operations in all weathers made IFR the standard for commercial aviation.
