George L. Kelling

George Lee Kelling
Born: 21 August 1935, Milwaukee, Wisconsin, U.S.
Died: 15 May 2019, Hanover, New Hampshire, U.S.
Occupation: Criminologist
Known for: Broken windows theory

George Lee Kelling (August 21, 1935 – May 15, 2019) was an American criminologist, a professor in the School of Criminal Justice at Rutgers University–Newark, a senior fellow at the Manhattan Institute for Policy Research, and a fellow at the Kennedy School of Government at Harvard University. He is best known as the co-author, with James Q. Wilson, of the 1982 Atlantic Monthly article "Broken Windows," which introduced the broken windows theory of crime and policing.

Early life and career

Kelling was born in Milwaukee and trained originally in social work, earning degrees from St. Olaf College, the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee, and a PhD in social welfare from the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1973. His early research included directing the Police Foundation's Kansas City Preventive Patrol Experiment (1972–1973), whose findings — that varying the level of routine motorized patrol had little measurable effect on crime — challenged conventional assumptions about policing, and the Newark Foot Patrol Experiment, which found that foot patrol reduced citizens' fear of crime even where crime itself was little changed.

Broken windows theory

The "Broken Windows" article argued that visible signs of disorder and neglect — an unrepaired broken window, graffiti, litter, public drunkenness — signal that no one is in control and thereby invite more serious crime. Kelling and Wilson proposed that police should focus on maintaining order in public spaces and working with communities to address minor offenses. Kelling expanded the argument in Fixing Broken Windows (1996), written with his wife, Catherine M. Coles. The theory influenced order-maintenance and "quality of life" policing strategies adopted in New York City in the 1990s under Commissioner William Bratton, and was credited by supporters with contributing to sharp crime declines, while critics questioned the causal claims and warned against aggressive enforcement of minor infractions.

Later work and death

Kelling consulted for police departments and transit agencies across the United States, including on the New York City subway, and in his later writings cautioned against distorted versions of broken-windows policing that emphasized mass arrests over problem-solving and community partnership. He died of complications from cancer at his home in Hanover, New Hampshire, on 15 May 2019, at the age of 83.
