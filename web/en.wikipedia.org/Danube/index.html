The Danube is the second-longest river in Europe, after the Volga. Rising in the Black Forest of Germany, it flows southeast for 2,850 km (1,770 mi), passing through or bordering ten countries and four capitals — Vienna, Bratislava, Budapest, and Belgrade, more than any other river in the world — before emptying into the Black Sea through the Danube Delta in Romania and Ukraine. Its drainage basin, the largest in the European Union, covers some 800,000 km² across 19 countries and is home to more than 80 million people.

Course

The Danube begins at Donaueschingen, where the Brigach and Breg meet. It flows through Bavaria (Passau), Austria (Linz, the Wachau valley, Vienna), then forms borders between Slovakia and Hungary, turns south through Budapest, and continues through Croatia, Serbia (Novi Sad, Belgrade), and along the Bulgaria–Romania frontier. Notable features include the Iron Gate gorges between Serbia and Romania, now dammed for hydroelectricity and navigation, and the vast, wildlife-rich Danube Delta — a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of Europe's best-preserved deltas. Major tributaries include the Inn, Drava, Tisza, Sava, Morava, and Prut.

History

The Danube was a frontier (limes) of the Roman Empire, lined with forts and legionary camps, and later a boundary between empires — Habsburg, Ottoman, and Russian. It has been a trade artery since antiquity; the Congress of Paris (1856) internationalized navigation, and today the river is governed for navigation and environmental protection by international commissions (the Danube Commission and the ICPDR). The Rhine–Main–Danube Canal (completed 1992) links the Danube to the Rhine, creating a continuous waterway from the North Sea to the Black Sea.

Ecology

The basin supports rich biodiversity, including sturgeon species now critically endangered by dams, overfishing, and poaching for caviar. Floodplain forests, the delta's reed beds and pelican colonies, and protected areas along the river are the focus of international conservation efforts, while pollution and river engineering remain significant pressures.

Culture

The river has inspired enduring cultural works, most famously Johann Strauss II's waltz An der schönen blauen Donau ("The Blue Danube", 1866). It figures in the national histories and literatures of all the riparian peoples, and Danube cruises and cycling paths (the Danube Cycle Path) make it one of Europe's most popular tourist corridors.
