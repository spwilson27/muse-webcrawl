Hand sanitizer

Hand sanitizer is a liquid, gel or foam used to kill many viruses and bacteria on the hands. Most common types are alcohol-based, typically containing ethanol or isopropyl alcohol at 60–95%, often with glycerol and other emollients. It is widely used in healthcare and by the public when soap and water are unavailable.

Effectiveness and use

Alcohol denatures microbial proteins and disrupts membranes. Sanitizer should cover all hand surfaces and be rubbed until dry. It is less effective on visibly dirty or greasy hands and against some pathogens, including norovirus and Clostridioides difficile spores, for which washing is preferred. Health authorities promoted its use during the COVID-19 pandemic.

History

Alcohol has been used as an antiseptic since the 19th century. Modern gel sanitizers were popularised in the 1990s, with products such as Purell — originally developed in 1947 — reaching mass consumer markets and hospitals.

Safety

Alcohol sanitizers are flammable and poisonous if ingested in quantity; regulators have warned against products contaminated with methanol. Non-alcohol products based on benzalkonium chloride exist but are generally considered less broadly effective.
