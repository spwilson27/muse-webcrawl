The Iliad (Ancient Greek: Ἰλιάς, Iliás, "a poem about Ilium (Troy)") is one of two major surviving ancient Greek epic poems attributed to Homer. It is one of the oldest extant works of literature still widely read by modern audiences. As with the Odyssey, the poem is divided into 24 books and contains 15,693 lines in its most widely accepted version; it is written in Homeric Greek, a literary amalgam of Ionic Greek and other dialects.

Synopsis

Set towards the end of the Trojan War, a ten-year siege of the city of Troy by a coalition of Mycenaean Greek kingdoms, the poem depicts significant events in the war's final weeks. In particular, it traces the anger (mēnis) of the warrior Achilles after his commander Agamemnon seizes his war prize Briseis: Achilles withdraws from battle, the Trojans under Hector drive the Greeks back to their ships, and Achilles' beloved companion Patroclus, fighting in Achilles' armour, is killed by Hector. Enraged, Achilles returns to battle, kills Hector, and desecrates his body, but finally relents when Troy's king Priam comes by night to ransom his son's corpse; the poem ends with Hector's funeral.

Composition and the Homeric Question

Scholars generally date the poem's composition to the late 8th or early 7th century BC. It crystallised from a long oral tradition of heroic song; in the 20th century Milman Parry and Albert Lord demonstrated the formulaic, oral-derived character of its language. The "Homeric Question" — whether a single poet composed the epics — remains debated, but the Iliad is widely treated as the work of a monumental composer shaping traditional material.

Themes

Central themes include glory (kleos) and honour, fate, the intervention of the gods, the costs of rage, and the shared humanity of enemies, culminating in the meeting of Achilles and Priam, two bereaved men mourning together. Fate looms over all characters: even Zeus cannot save his son Sarpedon once his death is fated.

Influence

Together with the Odyssey, the Iliad stood at the foundation of Greek education and has profoundly shaped Western literature, from Virgil's Aeneid to modern retellings. Famous English translations include those of Chapman, Pope, Lattimore, Fagles, Wilson and others.
