The Routing Information Protocol (RIP) is one of the oldest distance-vector routing protocols used in computer networks. It employs hop count as its routing metric: each router a packet crosses counts as one hop, and routes with more than 15 hops are considered unreachable, which limits RIP to relatively small networks.

Operation

RIP routers periodically broadcast (RIPv1) or multicast (RIPv2) their entire routing tables to directly connected neighbours, by default every 30 seconds over UDP port 520. Each receiving router adds one to the advertised hop counts and installs better (lower-count) routes. The algorithm is a distributed implementation of the Bellman–Ford shortest-path computation.

Because information propagates slowly, RIP can suffer from slow convergence and counting-to-infinity problems after a failure. Standard mitigations include split horizon, route poisoning and hold-down timers, along with triggered updates sent immediately when a metric changes.

Versions

RIPv1 (1988, RFC 1058) is classful: updates carry no subnet masks, so all subnets of a network must be the same size. RIPv2 (1998, RFC 2453) supports classless addressing, route tags, next-hop addresses and simple password or MD5 authentication, sending updates to the multicast address 224.0.0.9. RIPng (RFC 2080) extends the protocol for IPv6.

History and use

RIP derives from the gateway information protocol used on the ARPANET and from the routing daemon routed distributed with BSD Unix in the early 1980s. Although largely superseded by link-state protocols such as OSPF in larger networks, RIP remains in use in small and legacy networks because of its simplicity and near-universal support.
