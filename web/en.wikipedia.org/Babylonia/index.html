Babylonia was an ancient Akkadian-speaking state and cultural region based in south-central Mesopotamia (present-day Iraq). It originated with the rise of the city of Babylon under an Amorite dynasty in the 19th century BC and endured, with interruptions under Kassite, Assyrian, and other rule, until its final absorption into the Hellenistic world. Babylonia, together with Assyria in the north, inherited the older Sumerian civilization and became a centre of learning — in law, astronomy, mathematics, and literature — whose achievements influenced the whole ancient Near East and beyond.

Old Babylonian period

The First Dynasty of Babylon reached its height under Hammurabi (reigned c. 1792–1750 BC), who conquered most of Mesopotamia and promulgated the famous Code of Hammurabi, one of the earliest and most complete written legal codes. After his death the empire contracted; in 1595 BC the Hittites sacked Babylon, and the Kassites took control.

Middle and Neo-Babylonian periods

Kassite rule lasted about four centuries, a period of stability and diplomatic correspondence with Egypt and Assyria. Following centuries in which Babylonia was frequently dominated by Assyria, the Neo-Babylonian (Chaldean) Empire was founded in 626 BC. Under Nebuchadnezzar II (605–562 BC) it conquered Judah, destroyed Jerusalem and its temple in 587 BC, deported much of its population (the Babylonian captivity), and rebuilt Babylon on a monumental scale, with the Ishtar Gate and great ziggurat Etemenanki.

Persian and Hellenistic rule

In 539 BC Cyrus the Great of Persia took Babylon, and the region became a satrapy of the Achaemenid Empire. Alexander the Great conquered it in 331 BC and died in Babylon in 323 BC; under his Seleucid successors the city's importance waned, though Babylonian astronomical scholarship continued for centuries. The last datable cuneiform texts are from the 1st century AD.

Culture and legacy

Babylonian scholars produced advanced mathematics in a sexagesimal (base-60) system — the origin of our 60-minute hour and 360-degree circle — and systematic astronomical records used by Greek astronomers. Babylonian literature includes the Epic of Gilgamesh in its Standard Babylonian version and the creation epic Enūma Eliš.
