Intercity rail

Intercity rail services are express passenger train services that cover longer distances than commuter or regional trains. There is no precise definition of intercity rail; its meaning varies from country to country. In most cases it refers to rail services whose primary purpose is to connect cities and towns over distances typically greater than about 100 km (60 mi), with fewer intermediate stops and higher average speeds than local services.

Characteristics

Intercity trains generally serve major population centres, offer amenities such as reserved seating, dining or buffet cars, first-class accommodation and at-seat power, and may achieve high commercial speeds, especially on dedicated high-speed lines. They contrast with commuter rail, which serves suburbs of a single metropolitan area with frequent stops, and with regional rail, which serves intermediate distances within a region.

By region

In the United Kingdom, InterCity was the brand of British Rail's long-distance sector from 1966 until privatization in the 1990s; routes such as the InterCity 125 high-speed trains on the East Coast and Great Western main lines remain emblematic. In Germany, Deutsche Bahn operates Intercity (IC) and the faster Intercity-Express (ICE) services; in France, SNCF runs TGV high-speed and Intercités services. In the United States, Amtrak provides intercity passenger rail across most states, including the Acela service in the Northeast Corridor. China has built the world's largest network of intercity and high-speed railways, with CRH trains linking all major cities, while Japan's Shinkansen, opened in 1964, pioneered modern high-speed intercity rail.
