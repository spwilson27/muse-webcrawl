Passacaglia

The passacaglia is a musical form that originated in early seventeenth-century Spain as a short, formulaic interlude between dances or songs, and later developed into a serious and expansive form built over a repeating bass line (ground bass or ostinato), usually in triple metre and a minor key. The related chaconne shares the same fundamental principle of continuous variation over a repeated harmonic pattern.

A Baroque passacaglia, variations unfolding over an unchanging ground bass.

History

The earliest passacaglias were simple chord progressions played on the guitar between verses of a song. By the later seventeenth century the form had spread across Europe and become a vehicle for virtuoso keyboard and ensemble writing. Girolamo Frescobaldi published early examples, and Dietrich Buxtehude wrote notable organ passacaglias, one of which influenced the young Johann Sebastian Bach.

Famous examples

The most celebrated passacaglia is Bach's Passacaglia and Fugue in C minor, BWV 582, for organ, whose eight-bar ostinato sustains twenty variations and a fugue. George Frideric Handel, Henry Purcell, and later composers including Max Reger, Dmitri Shostakovich, and Benjamin Britten all used the form; Britten employed passacaglias in operas such as Peter Grimes. The finale of Brahms's Fourth Symphony is a passacaglia-like set of variations over a bass derived from Bach.
