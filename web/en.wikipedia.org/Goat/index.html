The goat (Capra hircus) is a domesticated species of goat-antelope typically kept as livestock. It was domesticated from the wild goat (Capra aegagrus) of Southwest Asia and Eastern Europe. The goat is a member of the family Bovidae and the tribe Caprini, meaning it is closely related to the sheep. There are over 300 distinct breeds of goat, and it is one of the oldest domesticated species of animal.

Goats have been used for milk, meat, fur, and skins across much of the world. Goat milk is the primary form of milk consumed in many parts of the developing world, and goat cheese is a significant product in some cuisines.

History

Goats were among the first animals domesticated by humans; genetic evidence suggests domestication around 10,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent, in what is now Iran and Turkey. The bezoar ibex is regarded as the principal wild ancestor. Goats appear in the mythology and folklore of many cultures, and two goats, Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjóstr, pull the chariot of the Norse god Thor.

Anatomy and characteristics

Goats are ruminants with a four-chambered stomach. Most goats naturally have two horns; their pupils are horizontal and slit-shaped, giving them a wide field of vision useful for detecting predators. Males (bucks or billies) and females (does or nannies) both commonly grow beards. Goats are famously curious, agile, and able climbers, known for balancing in precarious places and for eating a wide variety of plant material.

Reproduction

Goats reach puberty between three and 15 months of age. Gestation lasts about 150 days, and twins are the usual outcome, though single kids and triplets also occur. Young goats are called kids.

Farming

Worldwide, more than a billion goats are kept, with the largest populations in Asia and Africa. Breeds specialise in milk (such as Saanen and Alpine), fibre (Angora, source of mohair; Cashmere), and meat (Boer). Goatskin has historically been used for water and wine bottles and for parchment.
