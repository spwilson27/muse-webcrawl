Tennis elbow

Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis)
SpecialtyOrthopedics, sports medicine
SymptomsPain at the outer elbow, weak grip
CausesRepetitive overuse of wrist extensors
TreatmentRest, physiotherapy, bracing; surgery rarely
Frequency~1–3% of adults

Tennis elbow, known medically as lateral epicondylitis or lateral epicondyle tendinopathy, is a condition in which the outer part of the elbow becomes painful and tender. The pain typically results from overuse of the forearm extensor muscles and tendons, particularly the extensor carpi radialis brevis, where they attach to the lateral epicondyle of the humerus.

Signs, symptoms, and causes

Symptoms include pain and tenderness over the outer elbow, pain on gripping or lifting, and weakened grip strength. Despite the name, most cases occur in people who do not play tennis: it is associated with repetitive wrist extension and gripping in occupations such as plumbing, painting, carpentry, and meat cutting, as well as other racquet sports. Histologically it is a degenerative tendinosis — disorganised collagen and angiofibroblastic change — rather than a true inflammation.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical, based on tenderness at the lateral epicondyle and pain on resisted wrist extension (Cozen's test); imaging is used mainly to exclude other conditions. Most cases resolve within 6–12 months with activity modification, ice, analgesics, forearm bracing, and eccentric strengthening exercises. Corticosteroid injections give short-term relief but worse long-term outcomes. Refractory cases may be treated with platelet-rich plasma or surgical release of the tendon origin. The mirror-image condition on the inner elbow is golfer's elbow (medial epicondylitis).
