Fissured tongue

Fissured tongue (also known as scrotal tongue, lingua plicata and plicated tongue) is a benign condition characterised by deep grooves, or fissures, on the dorsal surface of the tongue. The fissures may be single or multiple and usually run in a longitudinal or branching pattern. It is a common finding, with prevalence estimates in adults ranging from about 2 to 5 percent in many populations, and it generally requires no treatment.

Presentation

Fissures typically appear on the top and sides of the tongue and can be several millimetres deep. The condition is usually asymptomatic, although food debris and microorganisms may accumulate in the grooves and cause mild irritation or halitosis if oral hygiene is poor. Fissured tongue is frequently seen together with geographic tongue (benign migratory glossitis).

Causes and associations

The exact cause is unknown, but a hereditary component is suspected, and prevalence increases with age, suggesting wear-related changes may contribute. Fissured tongue is reported more often in people with Down syndrome and forms part of the classic triad of Melkersson–Rosenthal syndrome, along with recurrent facial swelling and facial nerve palsy. It is also associated with psoriasis in some studies.

Management

Because the condition is benign, reassurance is usually all that is needed. Patients may be advised to gently brush the tongue to clear debris from the fissures. Evaluation is warranted only if there is pain, ulceration or another change suggesting a different diagnosis.
