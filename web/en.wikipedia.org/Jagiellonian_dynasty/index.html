The Jagiellonian dynasty (Polish: dynastia Jagiellonów; Lithuanian: Jogailaičiai) was a royal dynasty originating from the Lithuanian House of Gediminas, which reigned in several Central and Eastern European states between the 14th and 16th centuries. Members of the dynasty were Kings of Poland and Grand Dukes of Lithuania (1386–1572), Kings of Bohemia (1471–1526), and Kings of Hungary and Croatia (1440–1444 and 1490–1526). At its height around 1500, the dynasty ruled a vast swathe of Europe from the Baltic to the Adriatic and Black Seas.

Origins

The dynasty's founder was Jogaila, Grand Duke of Lithuania, who by the Union of Krewo (1385) agreed to accept Roman Catholic baptism, marry the young Polish queen Jadwiga, and unite his lands with the Crown of Poland. Crowned as Władysław II Jagiełło in Kraków in 1386, he brought about the Christianisation of Lithuania, Europe's last pagan state. In 1410 Polish-Lithuanian forces under Jagiełło and his cousin Vytautas won a decisive victory over the Teutonic Order at the Battle of Grunwald, one of the largest battles of medieval Europe.

Expansion

Under Casimir IV Jagiellon (r. 1447–1492), Poland concluded the Thirteen Years' War with the Teutonic Order, recovering Royal Prussia. Casimir's sons spread the dynasty across the region: Vladislaus II became King of Bohemia (1471) and Hungary (1490), while John I Albert, Alexander, and Sigismund I succeeded in Poland-Lithuania. The Bohemian-Hungarian branch ended abruptly when Louis II was killed fighting the Ottomans at the Battle of Mohács in 1526, after which Bohemia and Hungary passed to the Habsburgs.

Golden Age in Poland

The reigns of Sigismund I the Old (1506–1548) and Sigismund II Augustus (1548–1572) are regarded as the Polish Golden Age: a period of territorial extent reaching nearly a million square kilometres, Renaissance cultural flowering centred on the Kraków court (shaped by Sigismund I's Italian queen, Bona Sforza), and remarkable religious tolerance formalised in the Warsaw Confederation of 1573. The Union of Lublin (1569) transformed the personal union of Poland and Lithuania into the real union of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.

End of the dynasty

Sigismund II Augustus died childless in 1572, extinguishing the male line in Poland-Lithuania. His sister Anna Jagiellon was later crowned alongside her husband Stephen Báthory, and the Commonwealth became an elective monarchy. The last Jagiellon, Anna, died in 1596.
