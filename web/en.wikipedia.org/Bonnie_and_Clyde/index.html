Bonnie Elizabeth Parker (October 1, 1910 – May 23, 1934) and Clyde Chestnut Barrow (March 24, 1909 – May 23, 1934) were American outlaws who, with their gang, traveled the Central United States during the Great Depression, robbing banks and small stores and killing at least nine police officers and four civilians. Their exploits captured the attention of the American public during the era of "public enemies," and they remain among the most famous criminals in American history.

Early lives

Bonnie Parker was born in Rowena, Texas, and raised in West Dallas; she was an honors student who wrote poetry and married Roy Thornton at sixteen, though the marriage soon ended. Clyde Barrow grew up in poverty in Telico and West Dallas, Texas, and was repeatedly arrested for theft and burglary from his teens; his brutal experiences at the Eastham Prison Farm, where he killed an inmate who had assaulted him, hardened his hatred of the Texas prison system. The two met in January 1930.

The Barrow Gang

Between 1932 and 1934, the gang — at various times including Clyde's brother Buck and his wife Blanche, W. D. Jones, and others — committed a string of robberies that netted relatively small sums, along with numerous shootouts and jailbreaks. In January 1934 Clyde staged the Eastham prison break, freeing Raymond Hamilton and others, an embarrassment that spurred Texas to hire former Texas Ranger Frank Hamer to hunt them down. The murders of two motorcycle patrolmen at Grapevine, Texas, on Easter Sunday 1934, and of a constable in Oklahoma days later, intensified public and official determination to end the gang's run.

Deaths

After a lengthy manhunt, Hamer's posse tracked the pair to Louisiana. On the morning of May 23, 1934, six lawmen ambushed Bonnie and Clyde on a rural road near Sailes, Bienville Parish, firing over a hundred rounds into their stolen Ford V-8. Each was killed instantly; their "death car" and effects, including Parker's poems and Barrow's saxophone, became enduring artifacts of the legend.

Legacy

The 1967 film Bonnie and Clyde, starring Warren Beatty and Faye Dunaway, revived their fame and is credited with helping launch the New Hollywood era. Historians note that the romanticized popular image contrasts sharply with the poverty, injury, and violence of the couple's actual lives on the run.
