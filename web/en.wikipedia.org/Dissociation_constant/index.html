Dissociation constant

In chemistry, biochemistry and pharmacology, a dissociation constant (Kd) is a specific type of equilibrium constant that measures the propensity of a larger object to separate (dissociate) reversibly into smaller components, as when a complex falls apart into its component molecules, or when a salt splits up into its component ions.

Definition

For a dissociation reaction AB ⇌ A + B, the dissociation constant is Kd = [A][B] / [AB], where the brackets denote equilibrium concentrations. Kd is the reciprocal of the association constant Ka. For acid–base reactions, the corresponding constants are the acid dissociation constant Ka and its logarithmic form pKa.

Interpretation

Kd has units of concentration. When the concentration of one binding partner equals Kd, half of the other partner is bound at equilibrium. A small Kd indicates tight binding (high affinity), while a large Kd indicates weak binding. In pharmacology, the Kd of a drug for its receptor is a standard measure of binding affinity, and values are often reported as pKd = −log10 Kd.
