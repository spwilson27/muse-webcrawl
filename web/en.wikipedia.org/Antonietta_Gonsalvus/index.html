Antonietta Gonsalvus

Bornc. 1581, France
Known forMember of the "hairy family" of Petrus Gonsalvus; subject of a celebrated portrait by Lavinia Fontana
ParentPetrus Gonsalvus

Antonietta Gonsalvus (born c. 1581), sometimes called Tognina, was a daughter of Petrus Gonsalvus, the famous "wild man" of Tenerife whose entire body was covered with hair. Like her father and several of her siblings, Antonietta had hypertrichosis — a condition that caused contemporaries to regard the family as marvels of nature, halfway between human and beast. She is best known from a portrait by Lavinia Fontana, painted when she was about eight to ten years old, which remains one of the most famous images of the family.

Background

Petrus Gonsalvus had been brought from Tenerife to the court of Henry II of France in 1547, educated as a courtier, and married to a Parisian woman, Catherine. Several of their children inherited his condition and were presented as gifts between European courts: members of the family passed to the households of Margaret of Parma in the Netherlands and of Ranuccio Farnese in Parma, among others. Physicians and naturalists, most famously Ulisse Aldrovandi, studied and described the family, and their portraits circulated in cabinets of curiosities across Europe.

The Fontana portrait

Lavinia Fontana's portrait of Antonietta (c. 1585, now at the Château de Blois) shows the young girl in a rich court dress of dark fabric with gold ornament, holding a paper inscribed with her story — an account of how her father had been taken from the Canary Islands and of her own life at court. The contrast between her hair-covered face and hands and her fine clothing and poised bearing made the image emblematic of early modern fascination with the boundary between nature and culture. The family's story is often cited as a possible source for the fairy tale of Beauty and the Beast.

Medical interpretation

Modern scholars generally identify the Gonsalvus family's condition as congenital generalised hypertrichosis, a form of the rare condition sometimes called Ambras syndrome, after portraits of the family kept at Ambras Castle in Austria. Antonietta's later life is poorly documented; members of the family settled in Italy, where some descendants are recorded at Capodimonte near Viterbo.
