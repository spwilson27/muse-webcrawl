The theatre of ancient Greece was a theatrical culture that flourished in ancient Greece from about 700 BC. The city-state of Athens, which became a significant cultural, political, and religious place during this period, was its centre, where the theatre was institutionalised as part of a festival called the Dionysia, which honoured the god Dionysus. Tragedy (late 6th century BC), comedy (486 BC), and the satyr play were the three dramatic genres to emerge there. Athens exported the festival to its many colonies. Western theatre as we know it derives fundamentally from Greek drama, as does much of its technical vocabulary.

Origins and festivals

Tradition credits Thespis (c. 534 BC) as the first actor to speak lines as a character separate from the chorus — hence "thespian." At the City Dionysia, three tragedians each presented a tetralogy of three tragedies and a satyr play, competing for prizes judged by lot-drawn citizens; comic poets presented one play each (five during wartime). Production costs were borne by wealthy citizens called chorēgoi as a public duty. Aristotle's Poetics remains the foundational critical account of tragedy, with its concepts of mimesis, catharsis, hamartia, and anagnorisis.

The theatre building

A Greek theatre comprised the orchēstra, a circular dancing place for the chorus; the theatron ("seeing place"), tiered seating carved into a hillside holding up to 14,000 or more spectators; and the skēnē, a backdrop building whose façade served as scenery. Machines included the ekkyklēma, a wheeled platform for displaying interior scenes, and the mēchanē, a crane that swung gods above the stage — origin of the term deus ex machina. The theatre at Epidaurus (4th century BC) is famed for its acoustics and symmetry.

Actors and masks

All performers were male citizens or professionals; actors wore masks with exaggerated expressions that identified character types and amplified the voice. The protagonist, deuteragonist, and tritagonist divided all speaking parts among just two or three actors, while the chorus of twelve to fifteen sang and danced in the orchestra, commenting on the action.

Playwrights

Of the hundreds of tragedies written, complete plays survive by Aeschylus (7), Sophocles (7), and Euripides (18–19) — including Oedipus Rex, Medea, and the Oresteia. Old Comedy is represented by eleven plays of Aristophanes, and New Comedy by Menander's Dyskolos and fragments.
