Hepatology

Hepatology is the branch of medicine that incorporates the study of the liver, gallbladder, biliary tree, and pancreas, as well as management of their disorders. Although traditionally considered a sub-specialty of gastroenterology, rapid expansion has led in some countries to doctors specialising solely on this area, who are called hepatologists.

Scope

Diseases and complications related to viral hepatitis and alcohol are the main reason for seeking specialist advice. More than two billion people have been infected with hepatitis B virus at some point in their life, and approximately 350 million have become persistent carriers. Up to 80% of liver cancers can be attributed to either hepatitis B or hepatitis C virus. In terms of mortality, the former stands second only to smoking among known agents causing cancer. With the widespread implementation of vaccination and strict screening of blood products, lower infection rates are expected in the future. In many countries, however, overall alcohol consumption and the numbers of people with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (fatty liver disease) are rising, making cirrhosis an increasingly common endpoint.

Conditions managed by hepatologists include:

  • Viral hepatitis (hepatitis A, B, C, D, and E)
  • Alcohol-related liver disease
  • Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and steatohepatitis (MASH)
  • Cirrhosis and its complications, including portal hypertension, ascites, and hepatic encephalopathy
  • Primary liver cancers, chiefly hepatocellular carcinoma
  • Autoimmune liver diseases: autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis
  • Genetic and metabolic disorders such as haemochromatosis, Wilson's disease, and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency
  • Drug-induced liver injury
  • Liver transplantation assessment and follow-up

History

The study of the liver dates to antiquity — the Babylonians and Etruscans examined the livers of sacrificed animals, and the liver was long regarded as the seat of the soul and of blood production. Modern hepatology emerged in the mid-20th century with the identification of the hepatitis viruses, the development of liver biopsy, and the first successful liver transplantations performed by Thomas Starzl in the 1960s. Sheila Sherlock's textbook Diseases of the Liver and Biliary System (1955) is often credited with founding the modern specialty in Britain.

Training

In most countries, hepatologists are trained within gastroenterology fellowships, with additional dedicated training in transplant hepatology. In the United States, transplant hepatology is an accredited subspecialty with board certification available through internal medicine and gastroenterology pathways.
