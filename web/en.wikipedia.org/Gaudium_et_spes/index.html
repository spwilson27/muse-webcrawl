Gaudium et spes

Image placeholder: the Second Vatican Council in session

Gaudium et spes (Ecclesiastical Latin: [ˈɡau̯di.um et ˈspes], "Joy and Hope"), the Pastoral Constitution on the Church in the Modern World, is one of the four constitutions resulting from the Second Vatican Council. It was promulgated by Pope Paul VI on 8 December 1965, the final day of the Council, after approval by a vote of 2,309 to 75. It is the longest document of Vatican II and the first conciliar document addressed not only to Catholics but to "the whole of humanity."

Overview

The constitution is named for its opening words: "The joys and the hopes, the griefs and the anxieties of the men of this age, especially those who are poor or in any way afflicted, these are the joys and hopes, the griefs and anxieties of the followers of Christ." Its first part develops the Church's teaching on human dignity, the human community, human activity, and the Church's role in the modern world; its second part addresses specific urgent problems: marriage and family, culture, economic and social life, politics, and the promotion of peace and avoidance of war.

Themes

Gaudium et spes called the Church to read "the signs of the times" and to engage the modern world in dialogue rather than condemnation. It affirmed the legitimate autonomy of earthly affairs, defended religious freedom and the dignity of conscience, condemned discrimination and the arms race, and described peace as "not merely the absence of war" but the fruit of justice and charity. Its teaching on the universal destination of goods and the preferential concern for the poor influenced later Catholic social teaching and liberation theology.

Drafting

The text originated in "Schema XIII" of the Council's preparations and passed through years of revision by a mixed commission of theologians and bishops; contributors included Karol Wojtyła (the future Pope John Paul II), whose anthropology left a mark on its personalist language. It remains one of the most cited and debated documents of Vatican II.
