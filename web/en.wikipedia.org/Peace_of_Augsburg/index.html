The Peace of Augsburg, also called the Augsburg Settlement, was a treaty passed on 25 September 1555 by the Imperial Diet at Augsburg between Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor, and the Lutheran princes of the Schmalkaldic League. It officially ended the religious struggle between the two groups and made the legal division of Christendom within the Holy Roman Empire permanent, allowing rulers to choose either Lutheranism or Roman Catholicism as the official confession of their state.

Terms

The settlement's central principle is summarised by the Latin phrase cuius regio, eius religio ("whose realm, his religion"): each prince could determine the religion of his own state. Subjects who dissented were granted the right to emigrate with their property (ius emigrandi). Lutheranism — defined by the Augsburg Confession of 1530 — was the only Protestant confession recognised; Calvinists, Anabaptists and other "sects" were excluded. Two controversial clauses were the reservatum ecclesiasticum, which barred prince-bishops who converted to Protestantism from taking their territories with them, and Ferdinand's side assurance (the Declaratio Ferdinandea) tolerating Protestant practice by knights and cities in ecclesiastical lands. Free and imperial cities could maintain both confessions side by side.

Background and consequences

The Peace followed decades of conflict: the Schmalkaldic War of 1546–47, Charles V's short-lived Augsburg Interim of 1548, and the princes' revolt of 1552, which forced the Treaty of Passau. The settlement was negotiated largely by Charles's brother Ferdinand, as Charles — disillusioned — prepared to abdicate. The Peace stabilised the Empire for more than sixty years, but its exclusions and ambiguities stored up grievances that contributed to the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War in 1618; the Peace of Westphalia (1648) extended the Augsburg principles to Calvinism and fixed confessional status as of 1624.
