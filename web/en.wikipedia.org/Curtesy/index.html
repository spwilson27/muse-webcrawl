Curtesy

Curtesy is a common law doctrine under which a widower is entitled, on the death of his wife, to a life interest in the lands she owned in fee simple, provided that the marriage produced a child born alive who was capable of inheriting the estate. It was the husband's counterpart to the widow's right of dower.

Requirements

Historically, an estate by the curtesy required a valid marriage, the wife's seisin of an inheritable estate during the marriage, the birth of issue alive, and the wife's death. The husband's interest was initially called tenancy by the curtesy initiate upon the birth of the child, and became consummate on the wife's death.

History and abolition

The doctrine, often called "curtesy of England" because English law allowed it over all the wife's inheritable lands rather than only a portion, spread through the common law world. Like dower, curtesy has been abolished or replaced by statutory elective-share and homestead provisions in most jurisdictions, including England and Wales, where it was ended by the property legislation of 1925, and most states of the United States, though remnants survive in a few.
