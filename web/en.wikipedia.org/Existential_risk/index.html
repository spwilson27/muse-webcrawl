Existential risk

An existential risk is a risk that threatens the destruction of humanity's long-term potential. The concept was defined and popularized by philosopher Nick Bostrom, who described an existential catastrophe as an event that would either annihilate Earth-originating intelligent life or permanently and drastically curtail its potential. Toby Ord's 2020 book The Precipice surveyed the field and estimated a roughly one-in-six chance of existential catastrophe within the next century.

Sources of risk

  • Natural risks — asteroid or comet impacts, supervolcanic eruptions, gamma-ray bursts, and runaway natural pandemics. Background rates are generally estimated as low, since humanity has survived such hazards so far.
  • Anthropogenic risks — nuclear war, anthropogenic climate change extremes, engineered pandemics from biotechnology, and misaligned artificial general intelligence, which Bostrom's Superintelligence (2014) argued could pose a singular threat if a powerful system's goals diverged from human values.
  • Future risks — technologies not yet invented, such as advanced nanotechnology, and risks arising from totalitarian lock-in or other permanent losses of human self-determination.

Research and ethics

Research institutes devoted to the topic include the Future of Humanity Institute (Oxford), the Centre for the Study of Existential Risk (Cambridge), and the Global Challenges Foundation. Arguments for prioritizing existential-risk reduction draw on the enormous expected value of the future: if humanity's potential future population is vast, even tiny reductions in extinction probability have large moral weight. Critics question the precision of probability estimates, the possibility of effective action, and the influence of longtermist philosophy on near-term priorities.
