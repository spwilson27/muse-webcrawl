Arius (Ancient Greek: Ἄρειος; c. 256 – 336 AD) was a Cyrenaic presbyter and ascetic from Alexandria, Egypt. He was the originator of Arianism, the doctrine that Jesus Christ was not of the same substance as God the Father, but was the Father's first and greatest creation. His teachings provoked the Arian controversy, one of the most consequential theological disputes in early Christianity.

Teaching and controversy

Around 318, Arius came into conflict with Bishop Alexander of Alexandria by preaching that "there was a time when the Son was not": the Son, though divine, was created and therefore subordinate to the Father. Arius popularised his views in songs and in his work the Thalia. Condemned by a local synod, he gained support from influential bishops in the eastern provinces, and the dispute spread through the empire.

Council of Nicaea and exile

The Emperor Constantine convened the First Council of Nicaea in 325, which adopted the Nicene Creed proclaiming the Son homoousios ("of one substance") with the Father, and condemned Arius, who was exiled to Illyricum. He was later recalled and apparently reconciled, but died suddenly in Constantinople in 336, shortly before a planned formal readmission to communion.

Legacy

Arian Christianity survived for centuries among several Germanic peoples, including the Goths, Vandals and Lombards, and debates over Arius's doctrine shaped Christian theology for generations. His opponents, above all Athanasius of Alexandria, defined the orthodox position that prevailed.
