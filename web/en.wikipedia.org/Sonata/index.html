Sonata

Sonata
OriginItalian sonare, "to sound" (as opposed to cantata, "to sing")
PeriodsBaroque, Classical, Romantic, modern
Typical formsMulti-movement work for solo instrument or small ensemble; first movement often in sonata form
Major exponentsCorelli, Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, Brahms
The piano sonata is a central sonata genre

A sonata is a composition for one or more solo instruments, typically in several contrasting movements, most characteristically for a keyboard instrument alone or for a solo melody instrument with keyboard accompaniment. In the Classical era the sonata became one of the most important genres of instrumental music, and its first-movement structure — sonata form — shaped symphonies, concertos, and chamber music as well.

History

Baroque sonata

In the Baroque period "sonata" simply meant an instrumental piece. Arcangelo Corelli codified the church sonata (sonata da chiesa, slow–fast–slow–fast) and chamber sonata (sonata da camera, a suite of dances), usually for one or two violins and basso continuo. Domenico Scarlatti wrote over 550 single-movement keyboard sonatas.

Classical sonata

Haydn, Mozart, and Clementi established the three- or four-movement Classical sonata: a fast opening movement in sonata form (exposition, development, recapitulation), a slow movement, a minuet or scherzo, and a lively finale. Beethoven expanded the genre's scale, drama, and emotional range in his 32 piano sonatas, including the "Pathétique", "Moonlight", "Waldstein", "Appassionata", and "Hammerklavier".

Later developments

Schubert, Chopin, Liszt, and Brahms adapted the sonata to Romantic expression; Liszt's single-movement B minor Sonata fused the movements into one span. Twentieth-century composers from Prokofiev and Scriabin to Carter continued the tradition while stretching its forms and tonality.
