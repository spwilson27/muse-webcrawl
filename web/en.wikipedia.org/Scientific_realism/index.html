Scientific realism

Scientific realism is the view that the universe described by science is real regardless of how it may be interpreted. A believer in scientific realism takes scientific theories to be approximately true descriptions of a mind-independent world, including the unobservable entities — such as electrons, genes, and tectonic plates — that theories posit. The position is central to the philosophy of science and stands in contrast to instrumentalism and other forms of scientific anti-realism.

Core commitments

Philosopher Stathis Psillos distinguishes three dimensions of realism: a metaphysical commitment that the world has a definite mind-independent structure; a semantic commitment that theoretical claims should be taken literally and are either true or false depending on whether posited entities exist; and an epistemic commitment that mature, predictively successful theories are well confirmed and approximately true.

Arguments

The leading argument for realism is the no-miracles argument, associated with Hilary Putnam: the predictive success of science would be miraculous if theories were not at least approximately true. The leading argument against realism is the pessimistic meta-induction, pressed by Larry Laudan: past successful theories (phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether) were later judged false and their central terms non-referring, so present theories are probably false too. Realists reply with selective strategies — such as structural realism (John Worrall), which holds that theories' mathematical structure is retained across theory change, and entity realism (Ian Hacking), which defends belief in entities that can be manipulated experimentally.

Variants and rivals

Rival positions include constructive empiricism (Bas van Fraassen), which recommends accepting theories as merely empirically adequate, and instrumentalism, which treats theories as tools for prediction. Internal variants of realism include structural, entity, and perspectival realism.
