Holocene extinction

The Holocene extinction, also referred to as the sixth mass extinction or Anthropocene extinction, is an ongoing extinction event caused predominantly by human activity. It comprises the disappearance of a large number of plant and animal species across the globe during the Holocene epoch, which began about 11,700 years ago at the end of the last glacial period. Many scientists consider the current rate of species loss to be comparable to the five major mass extinction events in Earth's history, including the one that eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs 66 million years ago.

Species lost during the Holocene include the dodo, the passenger pigeon and the woolly mammoth.

Background

The Holocene epoch followed the Pleistocene and the retreat of the great ice sheets. As human populations expanded across every continent, large animals — the megafauna — disappeared in waves that closely tracked human arrival. The woolly mammoth, giant ground sloths, the moa of New Zealand and the elephant birds of Madagascar all vanished after human settlement of their habitats.

Unlike earlier mass extinctions, which unfolded over hundreds of thousands or millions of years, the current event is proceeding over centuries to millennia. Estimates of the background extinction rate suggest that species are now being lost at 100 to 1,000 times the natural background rate.

Causes

The principal drivers identified by scientists include:

  • Habitat destruction — conversion of forests and wetlands to agriculture, logging and urban development.
  • Overexploitation — hunting, fishing and harvesting at unsustainable rates, as in the cases of the passenger pigeon and the great auk.
  • Climate change — human-driven warming altering habitats faster than species can adapt or migrate.
  • Invasive species — introduced predators, competitors and diseases, particularly devastating to island faunas.
  • Pollution and disease — chemical contamination, ocean acidification and emerging pathogens.

Notable extinct species

Well-documented losses include the dodo of Mauritius (extinct by about 1681), the passenger pigeon of North America (last individual, Martha, died in 1914), the thylacine of Tasmania (1936), the Caribbean monk seal (declared extinct 2008) and the baiji or Yangtze river dolphin (functionally extinct by 2006). Island species have been disproportionately affected: the majority of recorded bird extinctions since 1500 have occurred on islands.
