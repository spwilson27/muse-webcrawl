Salt marsh

A salt marsh (also saltmarsh) is a coastal ecosystem in the upper coastal intertidal zone between land and open saltwater or brackish water that is regularly flooded by the tides. It is dominated by dense stands of salt-tolerant plants (halophytes) such as herbs, grasses, and low shrubs. Salt marshes are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth and provide critical habitat, coastal protection, and carbon storage.

Formation and zonation

Salt marshes form in sheltered coastal settings — estuaries, lagoons, and behind barrier islands — where fine sediment accumulates. Plants colonize mudflats, trap more sediment, and build the marsh surface upward. Characteristic zonation develops with elevation and flooding frequency: low marsh, flooded daily, is often dominated by cordgrasses (Sporobolus alterniflorus, formerly Spartina alterniflora), while the higher marsh, flooded only at spring tides, supports species such as saltmeadow hay, glassworts (Salicornia), and rushes.

Ecology

Marsh plants tolerate salinity through salt excretion glands, succulence, and exclusion at the roots. The marsh supports rich food webs: detritus from decaying grasses feeds invertebrates, which in turn support fish, crabs, and birds. Tidal creeks and marsh edges serve as nurseries for many commercially important fish and shellfish, and marshes are vital feeding and breeding grounds for waterfowl and wading birds.

Ecosystem services

Salt marshes buffer coasts against storm surge and erosion, filter nutrients and pollutants from runoff, and sequester large amounts of "blue carbon" in their waterlogged soils, where slow decomposition can store carbon for centuries. They are valued for recreation, fisheries, and grazing in some regions.

Threats and conservation

Historically, marshes were widely drained, diked, and filled for agriculture and development. Today they face "coastal squeeze" from sea-level rise combined with shoreline hardening, which prevents inland migration. Restoration projects remove dikes and fill, reconnect tidal flow, and replant native vegetation; "managed realignment" is used in parts of Europe to recreate marsh habitat while maintaining flood defence.
