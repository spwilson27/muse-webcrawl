Animal consciousness

Animal consciousness, or animal awareness, is the quality or state of self-awareness within a non-human animal, or of being aware of an external object or something within itself. Consciousness has been defined as sentience — the capacity to feel pain and pleasure — as well as awareness, subjectivity, and the ability to experience and perceive.

Historical views

René Descartes famously argued that animals are automata lacking minds, a view challenged by later naturalists. Charles Darwin's work on the expression of emotions supported continuity between human and animal minds. Behaviourism in the early 20th century sidelined the question, but Donald Griffin's The Question of Animal Awareness (1976) helped revive it as a legitimate scientific topic, founding cognitive ethology.

Evidence

Researchers assess consciousness through behavioural and neural indicators: mirror self-recognition in great apes, dolphins, elephants, and magpies; metacognition (knowing what one knows) in primates and rats; episodic-like memory in scrub-jays; tool manufacture in New Caledonian crows; and neural correlates such as sleep patterns and brain structures analogous to those supporting consciousness in humans. In 2012 the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness stated that a wide range of animals, including mammals, birds, and octopuses, possess the neurological substrates of consciousness.

Sentience and welfare

The recognition of animal sentience has influenced law and policy: the European Union's Lisbon Treaty (2009) acknowledges animals as sentient beings, and the United Kingdom's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 extended recognition to cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans.

Debates

Scholars dispute which taxa are conscious, whether consciousness is gradual or thresholded, and whether self-awareness is required or whether phenomenal experience suffices. The problem of other minds applies with special force where language is absent.
