The Trail of Tears was the forced displacement of approximately 60,000 people of the "Five Civilized Tribes" — the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Seminole, Chickasaw, and Choctaw nations — from their ancestral homelands in the Southeastern United States to areas west of the Mississippi River designated as Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma), during the 1830s and afterward. The removals followed the Indian Removal Act of 1830, championed by President Andrew Jackson.

Background

The southeastern nations had adopted many European-American customs — farming, literacy, and constitutional governments (the Cherokee published a newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix). Nevertheless, settler demand for land, and gold discovered in Georgia, intensified pressure. In Worcester v. Georgia (1832) the U.S. Supreme Court affirmed tribal sovereignty, but Georgia and the federal government ignored the ruling. A small unauthorized Cherokee faction signed the Treaty of New Echota (1835), ceding all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi, which the Cherokee Nation overwhelmingly rejected.

The removals

The Choctaw were removed first (1831–1834), followed by the Seminole — whose resistance led to the Second Seminole War — the Creek (1836), the Chickasaw (1837–1838), and finally the Cherokee in 1838–1839 under military roundup directed by General Winfield Scott. Cherokee detachments traveled some 1,200 miles on foot and by wagon, boat, and rail through a harsh winter; disease, exposure, and starvation killed an estimated 4,000 or more of the roughly 16,000 removed. The Cherokee call the journey Nunna daul Isunyi — "the Trail Where They Cried."

Legacy

The removals are regarded as acts of ethnic cleansing and a defining injustice of U.S. Indian policy. The Trail of Tears National Historic Trail, established in 1987, commemorates the routes. The Cherokee Nation and the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians (descended from those who remained or returned) endure as sovereign tribal nations.
