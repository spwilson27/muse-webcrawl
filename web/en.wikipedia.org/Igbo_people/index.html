Igbo people

Igbo men in traditional isiagu attire.

The Igbo people (also spelled Ibo) are one of the largest ethnic groups in Africa, native to present-day south-eastern Nigeria, in the region historically known as Igboland. They speak the Igbo language, a member of the Volta–Niger branch of the Niger–Congo family. Numbering in the tens of millions, the Igbo are one of Nigeria's three largest ethnic groups, alongside the Hausa and the Yoruba.

Society and culture

Pre-colonial Igbo society was famously decentralised, organised into autonomous village groups governed by consensus assemblies, age grades, titled societies, and councils of elders rather than kings — a political culture sometimes summarised by the saying "the Igbo have no kings." Exceptions included the Kingdom of Nri, an influential ritual state, and the Aro Confederacy. Igbo culture is noted for its mbari art houses, elaborate masquerade (mmanwu) traditions, kola nut hospitality rituals, and a strong emphasis on individual achievement, commerce, and education.

History

Large numbers of Igbo people were taken across the Atlantic during the transatlantic slave trade, forming significant diaspora communities in the Americas. In 1967 the predominantly Igbo Eastern Region declared independence as the Republic of Biafra, precipitating the Nigerian Civil War (1967–1970), in which millions died, many from famine. Since then the Igbo have remained central to Nigerian commerce and public life, and the contemporary novelist Chinua Achebe, author of Things Fall Apart (1958), brought Igbo culture to a worldwide readership.
