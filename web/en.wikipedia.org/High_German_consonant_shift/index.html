High German consonant shift

Period: c. 3rd–9th centuries AD
Region: Southern and central German-speaking area
Also called: Second Germanic consonant shift

The High German consonant shift, also called the second Germanic consonant shift, was a phonological development that took place in the southern parts of the West Germanic dialect continuum in roughly the 3rd to 9th centuries AD. It is the defining change that distinguishes High German dialects (including Standard German) from Low German and the other Germanic languages such as English and Dutch.

The changes

In the shift, the Proto-Germanic voiceless stops *p, *t, *k became long (geminate) fricatives after vowels — compare English water / German Wasser, English eat / German essen, English make / German machen — and became affricates (pf, ts [written z], kx) at the beginnings of words and after consonants: English pound / German Pfund, English tin / German Zinn, English apple / German Apfel. Germanic *þ became d (English three / German drei), and *d became t (English day / German Tag). The shift parallels the earlier First Germanic consonant shift described by Grimm's law.

Geographical spread

The shift spread from south to north with decreasing completeness, producing the staggered bundle of isoglosses known as the Rhenish fan: southern dialects shifted all consonants in all positions, while progressively northern varieties shifted fewer. The Benrath line (the maken/machen isogloss) and the Uerdingen line (ik/ich) delimit High German from Low German and Low Franconian respectively.
