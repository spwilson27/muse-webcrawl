The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari


Original German release poster (1920).

The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (German: Das Cabinet des Dr. Caligari) is a 1920 German silent horror film directed by Robert Wiene and written by Hans Janowitz and Carl Mayer. Considered the quintessential work of German Expressionist cinema, it tells the story of an insane hypnotist (Werner Krauss) who uses a somnambulist (Conrad Veidt) to commit murders. The film features a dark, distorted visual style, with sharp-pointed forms, oblique lines, twisted landscapes, and sets built at unnatural angles.

Plot

Francis, the narrator, recounts how the mysterious Dr. Caligari arrived at a village fair exhibiting Cesare, a somnambulist kept in a coffin-like cabinet, who answers questions about the future. After a series of murders, Francis and his fiancée Jane investigate: Cesare is discovered to have been sent to kill Jane, and Caligari is exposed as the director of a mental asylum, obsessed with a medieval mountebank of the same name. In the framing device's twist, the final scene reveals Francis himself to be an inmate of the asylum and Caligari its director — leaving the truth of the tale deliberately ambiguous.

Production

The film was produced by Decla-Bioscop and designed by Hermann Warm, Walter Reimann, and Walter Röhrig. Its painted, deliberately artificial sets were partly a consequence of postwar shortages and partly a conscious artistic program. Janowitz and Mayer said they drew on experiences of military authority and wartime trauma, though scholars continue to debate the origins of the framing story and its effect on the script's anti-authoritarian message.

Legacy

The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari helped establish German cinema internationally after the First World War and influenced horror, film noir, and art cinema worldwide. The critic Siegfried Kracauer famously read it in From Caligari to Hitler (1947) as a premonition of the German turn toward tyranny. It is routinely ranked among the greatest films ever made.
