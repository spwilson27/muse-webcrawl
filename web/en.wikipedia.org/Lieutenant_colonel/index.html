Lieutenant colonel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lieutenant colonel is a rank of commissioned officer in the armies, and some air forces and marine forces, of many countries. It stands above major and below colonel, and follows its etymology: the lieutenant colonel was historically the deputy (lieutenant) of the colonel commanding a regiment. The rank corresponds to NATO code OF-4.

Role

In modern armies, a lieutenant colonel typically commands a battalion or regiment of 300 to 1,000 soldiers, or serves as a senior staff officer at brigade, division, or ministry level. In the United States armed forces the rank is grade O-5, with insignia of a silver oak leaf cluster; promotion is competitive and generally follows 16–22 years of service. In the British Army and Commonwealth forces, lieutenant colonels command battalions and hold senior staff appointments.

Equivalents

The equivalent naval rank is commander. In air forces using army-style ranks it remains lieutenant colonel; in the Royal Air Force the equivalent is wing commander. Many police services (for example in several U.S. state and municipal forces) and some fire services also use the rank of lieutenant colonel as a senior command grade.

Pronunciation and abbreviations

In British and Commonwealth usage the first word is pronounced "leftenant" (lieutenant colonel, abbreviated Lt Col); in American usage it is pronounced "loo-tenant" and abbreviated LTC (Army), LtCol (Marine Corps), or Lt. Col. Officers of this rank are commonly addressed simply as "Colonel", as is the corresponding French rank of lieutenant-colonel.

History

The rank arose in the 16th and 17th centuries as regiments passed from proprietary colonels to permanent state forces: the colonel was the regimental proprietor or commander, his lieutenant the deputy who exercised day-to-day command, and the major the third officer. By the 19th century the rank had been standardized across European and American armies above major and below colonel.
