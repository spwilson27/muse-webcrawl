Bereavement

Bereavement is the condition of having lost a close family member or friend through death. The period of grief and mourning after a death is often simply called bereavement, and the person experiencing it is described as bereaved. Reactions commonly include sadness, yearning, numbness, difficulty sleeping, changes in appetite, and difficulty concentrating.

Grief responses

Grief is a natural response to loss and varies widely between individuals and cultures. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross described stages of grief, though modern research emphasises that grief does not follow a fixed sequence; models such as the dual process model describe oscillation between confronting the loss and adapting to a changed life. Most people gradually adapt, but a minority develop prolonged grief disorder, a persistent and disabling condition now recognised in diagnostic manuals.

Social and cultural practices

Cultures and religions prescribe mourning rituals — funerals, wakes, shiva in Judaism, periods of formal mourning dress, and memorial anniversaries — that structure bereavement and mobilise social support for the bereaved.

Support

Support may come from family, community, bereavement counselling, and support groups. Research indicates that practical and emotional support, and the opportunity to talk about the deceased, are generally helpful, while most bereaved people do not require formal therapy.
