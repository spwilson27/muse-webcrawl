Polar bear

Scientific classification

Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Chordata
Class: Mammalia
Order: Carnivora
Family: Ursidae
Genus: Ursus
Species: U. maritimus

Binomial name: Ursus maritimus Phipps, 1774

The polar bear (Ursus maritimus) is a large bear native to the Arctic and nearby areas. It is closely related to the brown bear, and the two species can interbreed. The polar bear is the largest extant species of bear and land carnivore, with adult males weighing 300–800 kg (660–1,760 lb). The species is sexually dimorphic, as adult females are much smaller. The polar bear is white- or yellowish-furred with black skin and a thick layer of fat. It is more slender than the brown bear, with a narrower head, longer neck and lower shoulder hump. Its teeth are sharper and more adapted to cutting meat. The paws are large and allow the bear to walk on ice and paddle in the water.

Polar bears are both terrestrial and pagophilic (ice-living) and are considered marine mammals because of their dependence on marine ecosystems. They prefer the annual sea ice, but live on land when the ice melts in the summer. They are mostly carnivorous and specialized for preying on seals, particularly ringed seals. Such prey is typically taken by ambush; the bear may just sit by the seal's breathing hole for hours or days to wait for the seal to emerge. Polar bears are superb swimmers, and may travel long distances across open water.

Population and conservation

The estimated worldwide population is around 26,000 individuals, divided into 19 subpopulations around the circumpolar Arctic. The polar bear is classified as a vulnerable species on the IUCN Red List. The key threat is climate change, which is causing the sea ice on which the bears depend to decline in extent and duration. Hunting has been regulated internationally since the 1973 Agreement on the Conservation of Polar Bears.

Relationship with humans

Polar bears have long been important in the material, spiritual and cultural life of Arctic indigenous peoples, including the Inuit. The polar bear appears on the Canadian two-dollar coin and is the mascot of numerous organisations and sports teams.
