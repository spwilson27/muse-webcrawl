Hideki Tojo (Japanese: 東條 英機, 30 December 1884 – 23 December 1948) was a Japanese general and politician who served as Prime Minister of Japan and President of the Imperial Rule Assistance Association from 1941 to 1944, during most of the Pacific War. He was a leading advocate of Japan's expansionist and militarist policies and bore responsibility within the Tokyo Trials system for Japan's wars of aggression.

Early life and career

Tojo was born in Tokyo, the third son of Hidenori Tojo, a lieutenant general in the Imperial Japanese Army descended from a samurai family of the Iwaki Domain. He graduated from the Imperial Japanese Army Academy in 1905 and the Army War College in 1915, served as a military attaché in Germany after the First World War, and became known as an efficient, rigorous staff officer — earning the nickname "Razor" for his sharp mind. He rose through the ranks of the Kwantung Army in Manchuria, commanding the Kempeitai there in 1935 and serving as its chief of staff from 1937, leading forces in the invasion of Inner Mongolia and northern China.

Path to the premiership

As vice-minister and then Minister of War (from July 1940) in the cabinets of Fumimaro Konoe, Tojo pressed for the occupation of French Indochina and opposed concessions to the United States. When Konoe resigned in October 1941 amid deadlock over the American demand for Japanese withdrawal from China and Indochina, Emperor Hirohito appointed Tojo prime minister, largely to restrain the army; instead, Tojo's government authorized the attack on Pearl Harbor of 7 December 1941 and Japan's conquests across Southeast Asia and the Pacific.

Wartime leadership and fall

Tojo accumulated offices — prime minister, war minister, and at times home minister, foreign minister, and chief of the Army General Staff — concentrating power to a degree unusual even in wartime Japan. After early victories, Japan's position deteriorated from Midway onward. Following the fall of Saipan in July 1944, which brought the home islands within range of American B-29 bombers, Tojo and his cabinet resigned on 18 July 1944.

Trial and execution

After Japan's surrender, Tojo attempted suicide by shooting himself in September 1945 but survived after treatment by American doctors. He was tried by the International Military Tribunal for the Far East, convicted of waging aggressive war and of ordering or permitting inhumane treatment of prisoners of war, and hanged at Sugamo Prison on 23 December 1948. He is enshrined, controversially, at Yasukuni Shrine as a Class-A war criminal.
