Stop codon

In molecular biology (specifically protein biosynthesis), a stop codon (or termination codon) is a codon (nucleotide triplet within messenger RNA) that signals the termination of the translation process of the current protein. Most codons in messenger RNA correspond to the addition of an amino acid to a growing polypeptide chain, which may ultimately become a protein; stop codons signal the termination of this process by binding release factors, which cause the ribosomal subunits to disassemble, releasing the amino acid chain.

The three stop codons

In the standard genetic code, there are three stop codons:

CodonName
UAAochre
UAGamber
UGAopal (or umber)

In DNA these are written TAA, TAG and TGA. The names derive from the colour-based naming system used by the researchers who discovered the nonsense mutations: amber was named after Harris Bernstein (German for "amber"), a graduate student whose mutation was studied by Richard Epstein and Charles Steinberg; ochre and opal continue the colour theme.

Mechanism

Unlike sense codons, stop codons are not recognised by any transfer RNA. Instead, proteins called release factors bind the ribosome's A site when a stop codon enters it, causing the peptidyl-tRNA bond to be hydrolysed and the completed polypeptide to be released.

Variations and readthrough

Some organisms use alternative genetic codes in which one or more stop codons are reassigned to amino acids. In many ciliates, for example, UAA and UAG encode glutamine and only UGA functions as a stop. In some contexts, UGA is recoded to selenocysteine and UAG to pyrrolysine. Stop codon readthrough — in which a tRNA pairs with the stop codon and translation continues — occurs at low frequency naturally and can be induced by certain drugs, a phenomenon exploited in research into diseases caused by premature stop codons.
