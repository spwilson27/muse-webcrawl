Miles Davis

Miles Dewey Davis III (26 May 1926 – 28 September 1991) was an American jazz trumpeter, bandleader, and composer. He is among the most influential and acclaimed figures in the history of jazz and 20th-century music. Davis adopted a variety of musical directions in a five-decade career that kept him at the forefront of major stylistic developments, including bebop, cool jazz, hard bop, modal jazz, and jazz fusion.

Early career and bebop

Born in Illinois and raised in East St. Louis, Davis moved to New York in 1944, ostensibly to study at Juilliard, but spent his time in Harlem clubs playing with Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie. His Birth of the Cool sessions (1949–50) with arranger Gil Evans helped define cool jazz. After overcoming heroin addiction in the mid-1950s, he formed his first great quintet with John Coltrane, Red Garland, Paul Chambers, and Philly Joe Jones.

Modal jazz and the second quintet

Kind of Blue (1959), recorded with Coltrane, Cannonball Adderley, and Bill Evans, became the best-selling jazz album of all time and the defining statement of modal jazz. His 1960s "second great quintet" — Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter, and Tony Williams — pushed toward freer, more abstract improvisation on albums such as E.S.P. and Nefertiti.

Fusion and later years

With In a Silent Way (1969) and Bitches Brew (1970), Davis pioneered jazz fusion, adopting electric instruments and rock rhythms and launching the careers of Chick Corea, Joe Zawinul, John McLaughlin, and others. Health problems forced a five-year retirement in the late 1970s; he returned in 1981 and worked until his death in 1991, recording the pop-inflected Tutu (1986) and the hip-hop-influenced Doo-Bop (released posthumously).

Legacy

Davis was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2006. His muted trumpet tone, use of space, and constant reinvention left an enduring mark on jazz and popular music, and his bands served as a finishing school for generations of jazz innovators.
