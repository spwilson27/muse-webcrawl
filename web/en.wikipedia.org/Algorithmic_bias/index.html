Algorithmic bias

Algorithmic bias describes systematic and repeatable errors in a computer system that create unfair outcomes, such as privileging one arbitrary group of users over others. Bias can emerge from the design of an algorithm, from unrepresentative or historically skewed training data, from the choice of proxies for unmeasured quantities, or from the decisions of the people who build and deploy the system.

Biased training data can cause automated systems to reproduce and amplify discrimination.

Causes

Major sources include training data that reflects historical discrimination (for example, arrest records used as proxies for crime), sample bias that underrepresents some groups, label bias in what is chosen as the target variable, and feedback loops in which a system's outputs generate the data used to train its successors — as alleged in predictive policing.

Documented examples

Well-known cases include the COMPAS recidivism scores, which a 2016 ProPublica analysis found flagged Black defendants as future criminals at nearly twice the rate of white defendants; Amazon's experimental hiring tool, scrapped in 2018 after it was found to penalise résumés containing the word "women's"; and facial recognition systems whose error rates for darker-skinned women were shown by the Gender Shades study to be far higher than for lighter-skinned men.

Mitigation and regulation

Responses include fairness-aware machine learning, algorithmic auditing, impact assessments, and regulation such as the European Union's AI Act. A central difficulty is that formal fairness criteria (demographic parity, equalised odds, calibration) can be mutually incompatible except in special cases.
