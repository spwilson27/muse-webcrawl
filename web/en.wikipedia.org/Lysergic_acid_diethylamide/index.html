Lysergic acid diethylamide

Chemical structure of LSD

Common names: LSD, acid
Formula: C20H25N3O
First synthesised: 1938, Albert Hofmann
Legal status: Schedule I (US); prohibited in most countries

Lysergic acid diethylamide, commonly known as LSD (from German Lysergsäurediethylamid) and colloquially as acid, is a potent psychedelic drug. It is known for its effects on consciousness, perception, and thinking, which can include visual alterations, synesthesia, a distorted sense of time, and experiences often described as mystical. Effects typically last 8 to 12 hours and begin within an hour of ingestion; typical doses are measured in micrograms.

History

LSD was first synthesised in 1938 by Swiss chemist Albert Hofmann at Sandoz Laboratories in Basel, from lysergic acid derived from the ergot fungus. Its psychedelic properties were discovered accidentally in 1943 when Hofmann absorbed a small quantity through his skin; three days later, on 19 April 1943 — celebrated as "Bicycle Day" — he deliberately took 250 micrograms and recorded the effects. Sandoz marketed LSD under the name Delysid for psychiatric research, and it was investigated by psychiatrists, the military, and intelligence agencies, including in the CIA's MKUltra programme. In the 1960s LSD became emblematic of the counterculture; it was prohibited in the United States in 1968 and placed in Schedule I of the Controlled Substances Act.

Pharmacology

LSD acts primarily as an agonist at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors on cortical neurons, and also binds dopamine and other serotonin receptors. Physically it is considered non-addictive and of low toxicity, with no documented deaths from pharmacological overdose alone, though accidents and adverse psychological reactions ("bad trips"), and in rare cases persisting perception disorder (HPPD), occur.

Research revival

Since the 2000s, clinical research into LSD has resumed, with studies exploring its use in treating anxiety associated with life-threatening illness, depression, cluster headaches, and alcohol dependence.
