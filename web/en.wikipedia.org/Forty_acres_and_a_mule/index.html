Forty acres and a mule

Forty acres and a mule was part of Special Field Orders No. 15, a wartime order proclaimed by Union General William Tecumseh Sherman on January 16, 1865, during the American Civil War, to allot land to some freed families in plots of land no larger than 40 acres (16 ha), and also surplus army mules. The orders were issued after Sherman's meeting in Savannah, Georgia with twenty Black ministers, who identified land ownership as the key to freedom and self-sufficiency.

The order and its reversal

The order reserved a strip of coastal land from Charleston, South Carolina, to the St. Johns River in Florida — including the Sea Islands and abandoned rice fields — for settlement by freed families, who would receive possessory (not legal ownership) titles. By June 1865 some 40,000 freedpeople had settled on roughly 400,000 acres. After President Lincoln's assassination, Andrew Johnson reversed the policy in the autumn of 1865, ordering most of the land returned to its former Confederate owners; freed families were evicted or reduced to signing labour contracts. The phrase "forty acres and a mule" came to symbolise the broken promise of land reform and is frequently invoked in modern debates over reparations for slavery.

Other proposals

Related measures included the Port Royal Experiment, the Freedmen's Bureau bills, and Radical Republican proposals by Thaddeus Stevens for confiscation and redistribution of large estates, which Congress rejected. General Rufus Saxton's insistence on enforcing the order led to his removal in 1866.
