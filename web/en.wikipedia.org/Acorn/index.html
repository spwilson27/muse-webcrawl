Acorn

The acorn is the nut of the oak tree (genus Quercus). It usually contains a single seed enclosed in a tough, leathery shell, and is borne in a cup-shaped structure called a cupule. Acorns are 1–6 cm (0.4–2.4 in) long and take between 6 and 24 months to mature, depending on the species; oaks in the white oak group mature in one season, while red oaks take two.

Ecology

Acorns are a keystone food for wildlife: deer, wild boar, bears, mice, squirrels and birds such as jays and woodpeckers depend on them, particularly in "mast years" when oaks produce bumper crops synchronously. Scatter-hoarding animals disperse acorns by burying them, and forgotten caches germinate into new trees. Because acorns are rich in carbohydrates but often bitter with tannins, predators preferentially eat and cache different species, influencing oak regeneration.

Human use

Acorns have been eaten by many cultures — Indigenous peoples of North America, Koreans (who make acorn jelly, dotorimuk, and acorn noodles), and Mediterranean peoples — after leaching out the bitter tannins with water. In Iberia, acorns of the holm oak feed the pigs that produce jamón ibérico de bellota. The acorn is also a widespread heraldic charge and a symbol of strength, longevity and humble beginnings, summarised by the proverb "mighty oaks from little acorns grow".
