Stoicism

Zeno of Citium (c. 334 – c. 262 BC), founder of the Stoic school.

Stoicism is a school of Hellenistic philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium in Athens in the early 3rd century BC. Named for the Stoa Poikile (Painted Porch) where Zeno taught, Stoicism holds that the path to eudaimonia (flourishing) is found in accepting the present moment, living according to reason and nature, and practising the four cardinal virtues: wisdom, courage, justice, and temperance.

Philosophy

Stoic philosophy divides into logic, physics, and ethics. The Stoics taught a materialist, deterministic universe governed by a rational, divine Logos with which humans should align their will. A central doctrine is the dichotomy of control: some things are up to us (our judgements, impulses, desires) and others are not (body, property, reputation), and suffering arises from mistaking the second for the first. External things are "indifferents" — some preferred, some dispreferred — but only virtue is genuinely good and only vice genuinely bad.

History

After Zeno, the school was systematised by Cleanthes and Chrysippus, carried to Rome by Panaetius and Posidonius, and practised by the great Roman Stoics of the imperial period: Seneca the Younger, the former slave Epictetus, and the emperor Marcus Aurelius, whose Meditations remain widely read. Stoicism declined after the 3rd century AD but profoundly influenced Christian thought, Spinoza, Kant, and modern cognitive-behavioural therapy.

Practices

Stoic exercises include negative visualisation (premeditatio malorum), journalling, voluntary discomfort, the "view from above", and the evening review of one's actions. In the 21st century Stoicism has enjoyed a broad popular revival as a practical philosophy of resilience.
