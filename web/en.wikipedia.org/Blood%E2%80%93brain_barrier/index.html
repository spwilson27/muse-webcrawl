Blood–brain barrier

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a highly selective semipermeable border of endothelial cells that regulates the transfer of solutes and chemicals between the circulatory system and the central nervous system, protecting the brain from harmful or unwanted substances in the blood while allowing passage of essential nutrients.

Structure

The barrier is formed by endothelial cells of the capillary wall, connected by tight junctions, together with astrocyte end-feet, pericytes, and a basement membrane. Unlike capillaries elsewhere in the body, brain endothelial cells lack fenestrations and show very low rates of transcytosis. Efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein actively pump many foreign compounds back into the blood.

Function

The BBB shields the brain from circulating pathogens, toxins and fluctuations in blood composition (such as potassium concentrations) that could disturb neuronal signalling. Small lipophilic molecules, oxygen and carbon dioxide cross relatively freely; glucose and amino acids enter via specific transporters.

Clinical significance

The barrier frustrates drug delivery: most large-molecule drugs and more than 98% of small-molecule drugs cannot cross it, complicating treatment of brain tumours, infections and neurodegenerative diseases. The barrier breaks down in stroke, trauma, multiple sclerosis and meningitis, contributing to pathology. Circumventricular organs lack a full BBB so the brain can sample blood content.

History

The concept grew from Paul Ehrlich's dye experiments in the 1880s and Edwin Goldmann's confirmatory studies, which showed dyes injected into blood stained the body but not the brain, and vice versa.
