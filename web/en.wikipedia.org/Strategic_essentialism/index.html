Strategic essentialism

Strategic essentialism is a concept in postcolonial and feminist theory, coined by the literary critic and theorist Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak. It refers to a political strategy in which members of a group — such as women, colonised peoples, or ethnic minorities — temporarily adopt a simplified, essentialised identity of themselves and act as if their interests were unified, in order to achieve common political goals, even though they know their group is internally diverse and that identity is socially constructed.

Origin

Spivak developed the term in the context of the Subaltern Studies collective and her analysis of how subaltern groups can represent themselves politically. She described it as "a strategic use of positivist essentialism in a scrupulously visible political interest". The strategy acknowledges a tension: essentialism is theoretically suspect, because identities are heterogeneous and constructed, yet mobilising a shared identity may be practically necessary for political visibility and collective action.

Usage and critique

The concept has been widely taken up in feminist theory, critical race theory, and activism, where it is used to justify rallying under umbrella identities such as "women" or "Black" without denying internal differences. Spivak herself later expressed ambivalence about the term, noting that it had often been used as an alibi for the very essentialism it was meant to deploy cautiously, and she emphasised that the strategy should remain self-conscious and provisional rather than hardening into doctrine.
