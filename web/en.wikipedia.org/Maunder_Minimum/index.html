Maunder Minimum

[Image: Sunspot number over the last 400 years, showing the Maunder Minimum]

The Maunder Minimum, also known as the "prolonged sunspot minimum", was a period around 1645 to 1715 during which sunspots became exceedingly rare. During a 28-year period (1672–1699) within the minimum, observations revealed fewer than 50 sunspots, in contrast to roughly 40,000–50,000 sunspots in modern times. The period was first described and named by John A. Eddy in 1976, after the solar astronomers Edward Walter Maunder and his wife Annie Russell Maunder, who had earlier documented the scarcity of aurorae and sunspots during the period.

The Maunder Minimum roughly coincided with the coldest part of the Little Ice Age, during which Europe and North America experienced bitterly cold winters. Whether there is a causal connection between low sunspot activity and cold winters remains the subject of ongoing research. In addition to sunspot records, the minimum is corroborated by records of auroras, and by cosmogenic isotopes such as carbon-14 in tree rings and beryllium-10 in ice cores, which indicate low solar magnetic activity.

Other minima

Other historical prolonged minima include the Spörer Minimum (c. 1460–1550), the Dalton Minimum (1790–1830), and much earlier minima identified from isotope records. Some research suggests even during a grand minimum, an 11-year cycle can still be detected in indirect records.
