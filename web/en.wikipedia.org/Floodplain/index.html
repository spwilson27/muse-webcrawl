Floodplain

A floodplain or flood plain is an area of land adjacent to a river that is flooded during periods of high discharge. Floodplains are formed by the deposition of sediment (alluvium) carried over the river's banks during floods, and by the lateral migration of meanders. They are among the flattest and most fertile landscapes on Earth, and consequently among the most densely settled — despite the flood risk that defines them.

Formation

When a river overtops its banks, the water spreads out and slows, dropping the coarsest sediment near the channel to build low natural levees and laying finer silt and clay across the wider plain. Meanwhile the channel itself migrates sideways as erosion on outer bends and deposition on inner bends shift meanders across the valley, levelling the surface. Abandoned meanders remain as oxbow lakes. Over thousands of years these processes produce a wide, nearly flat valley floor.

Ecology and human use

Floodplain wetlands, forests and wet meadows are highly productive ecosystems supporting rich wildlife, and flood pulses renew their nutrients. The same fertility attracted farming from the earliest civilisations — Egyptian agriculture depended on the Nile flood — and floodplains remain prime agricultural land and favoured sites for cities and transport routes. Modern flood risk management includes levees and floodwalls, floodplain zoning, wetland restoration and "room for the river" strategies that reconnect rivers to their plains to store floodwater.
