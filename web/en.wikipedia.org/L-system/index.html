L-system

Plants modelled by L-systems, generated by repeatedly rewriting strings of symbols.

An L-system or Lindenmayer system is a parallel rewriting system and a type of formal grammar, introduced in 1968 by the Hungarian theoretical biologist and botanist Aristid Lindenmayer of Utrecht University. Lindenmayer used the systems to model the growth processes of plant development, originally the cellular patterns of algae and filamentous fungi. L-systems were later popularized for generating fractals and realistic computer models of plants, notably through Przemysław Prusinkiewicz's work and the book The Algorithmic Beauty of Plants (1990).

Definition

An L-system consists of an alphabet of symbols, an initial string called the axiom, and a set of production rules that expand each symbol into a larger string. Unlike ordinary grammars, all symbols are replaced simultaneously at each iteration, mirroring the simultaneous division of cells in a growing organism. A classic example models algae:

  • Variables: A, B
  • Axiom: A
  • Rules: (A → AB), (B → A)

This produces the sequence A, AB, ABA, ABAAB, ABAABABA, …, whose lengths are the Fibonacci numbers.

Graphical interpretation

By interpreting the symbols as drawing commands — for example F means "draw forward", + and − mean "turn left/right by a fixed angle", and brackets [ ] push and pop a state to create branches — L-systems generate fractal curves such as the Koch snowflake and Dragon curve, and lifelike branching structures of trees and flowers. Extensions include stochastic L-systems (rules with probabilities), context-sensitive systems, and parametric L-systems, which attach numerical parameters to symbols and are used in professional procedural-modelling software for film and games.
