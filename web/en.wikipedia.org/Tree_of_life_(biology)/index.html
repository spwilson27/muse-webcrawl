The tree of life is a metaphor, model and research tool used to explore the evolution of life and describe the relationships between organisms, both living and extinct, as a phylogenetic tree whose root is the last universal common ancestor (LUCA) of all life on Earth. The idea that species arise by branching descent from common ancestors was made famous by Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), whose only illustration is a branching diagram.

History

Branching diagrams of relationships predate Darwin in the work of naturalists such as Jean-Baptiste Lamarck and Augustin Augier, but Darwin gave the metaphor its evolutionary meaning: "The affinities of all the beings of the same class have sometimes been represented by a great tree. I believe this simile largely speaks the truth." Ernst Haeckel popularised elaborate trees of life in the later nineteenth century. In the twentieth century, classification relied mainly on morphology and the fossil record; then in 1977 Carl Woese and George Fox, comparing ribosomal RNA sequences, showed that the "prokaryotes" split into two fundamentally distinct groups, Bacteria and Archaea, leading Woese to propose a three-domain system (Bacteria, Archaea, Eucarya) in 1990, with the deepest branches of the tree dominated by microbes.

Modern reconstruction

Contemporary trees are built from molecular sequence data — small-subunit rRNA genes for deep relationships and whole genomes within groups. Large collaborative projects, notably the Open Tree of Life, have synthesised published phylogenies into a single tree covering over two million named species, and a widely reproduced 2016 tree by Hug and colleagues, built from ribosomal protein sequences of thousands of genomes, emphasised that the great majority of life's diversity is bacterial and that eukaryotes are a comparatively small branch.

Complications

The tree metaphor is strained by processes that are not purely branching. Horizontal gene transfer among bacteria and archaea moves genes across lineages, leading some researchers to describe the base of the tree as a web or network. Endosymbiosis — the engulfment that produced mitochondria and chloroplasts — merged lineages outright, and hybridisation and introgression blur branches in plants and animals. Nevertheless, a statistical central trend of vertical descent, especially in the "core" genes of cells, allows a meaningful tree to be reconstructed, and the tree remains the standard framework for taxonomy, comparative genomics and the search for LUCA's properties.
