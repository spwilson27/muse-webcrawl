Venatio (Latin: "hunting", plural venationes) was a type of entertainment in Roman amphitheatres involving the hunting and killing of wild animals. Together with gladiatorial combats, venationes formed the spectacle known as the munera.

A venator confronts a leopard, from a Roman mosaic.

Format

Venationes usually opened the day's programme in the morning. Professional beast-fighters (bestiarii) and hunters (venatores) fought lions, leopards, bears, elephants, crocodiles, and even ostriches; in other acts, animals were pitted against each other, or the hunts were staged in artificial woodland scenery. Condemned criminals (damnati ad bestias) could also be exposed to the beasts as a form of execution.

Scale

The scale was enormous: at the inauguration of the Colosseum in 80 AD, some 9,000 animals were killed across 100 days of games, and Trajan's triumph games reportedly consumed 11,000. Sourcing animals from across the empire drove some regional populations — such as the North African elephant and the Mesopotamian lion — to extinction or near-extinction.
