The Salic law (Latin: Lex Salica) was the civil law code of the Salian Franks, a Germanic people who established the Frankish kingdom in what is now France and western Germany. Written in Latin with Frankish glosses and first compiled around 500 AD under Clovis I, it is one of the oldest surviving Germanic law codes and influenced legal development across medieval Europe.

Contents

The code dealt principally with criminal law and compensation: it set out wergild — money payments for killing or injuring a person — scaled by the victim's rank, age, and sex, as well as fines for theft, insult, and property damage. Its procedures influenced the development of continental European law, and its provisions on inheritance of allodial land were later invoked in disputes over royal succession.

Succession and the exclusion of women

The Salic law is most famous for its later use to exclude women from inheriting the throne. A clause concerning inheritance of "Salic land" by male heirs only was reinterpreted in 14th-century France to bar the succession of Edward III of England — whose claim came through his mother Isabella — helping precipitate the Hundred Years' War, and to bar female rule generally. The rule influenced succession law in France, Spain (until 1830), and other European monarchies. Its application contributed to crises such as the Pragmatic Sanction disputes and the Carlist Wars in Spain.

Legacy

Because primogeniture and male-preference succession were justified by appeal to the Salic law, it became a byword for the exclusion of women from power. Most European monarchies have since moved to absolute primogeniture, under which the eldest child inherits regardless of sex.
