Ekman founded the mathematical theory of wind-driven ocean currents.

Vagn Waldo Ekman (3 May 1874 – 9 March 1954) was a Swedish oceanographer. Through his theory of the Ekman spiral — the spiral pattern of ocean currents with depth caused by the interplay of wind friction and the Coriolis effect — he laid the foundation of modern dynamical oceanography.

Biography

Born in Stockholm, the son of Fredrik Laurentz Ekman, himself an oceanographer, Ekman studied mathematics and physics at Uppsala University, taking his doctorate in 1902. His interest in ocean dynamics was awakened by Fridtjof Nansen's account of the Fram expedition: Nansen had observed that icebergs drifted 20–40 degrees to the right of the wind direction, and Vilhelm Bjerknes set Ekman the problem of explaining why.

Ekman worked at the International Laboratory for Oceanographic Research in Oslo and from 1903 to 1908 at the Swedish Hydrographic-Biological Commission. In 1910 he became professor of mechanics and mathematical physics at Lund University, a post he held until 1939.

Work

In his 1905 paper "On the influence of the Earth's rotation on ocean-currents", Ekman solved the equations of motion for a wind-driven ocean, showing that the surface current flows at 45° to the right of the wind (in the Northern Hemisphere), with the current vector rotating and weakening with depth — the Ekman spiral — so that the net transport of the Ekman layer is at 90° to the wind. This Ekman transport explains upwelling along coasts and at the equator, phenomena of central importance to ocean circulation and marine productivity.

Ekman also developed instrumentation, including the Ekman current meter and the Ekman water bottle, and studied internal waves, which he investigated in connection with the "dead water" phenomenon that had slowed the Fram. The dimensionless Ekman number, comparing viscous forces to the Coriolis force, bears his name, as does the atmospheric Ekman layer, the lowest part of the planetary boundary layer.

Legacy

Ekman's results remain the standard starting point for teaching wind-driven circulation, and his 1905 paper is among the most cited classics of physical oceanography.
