Peace treaty


Signing of a peace treaty

A peace treaty is an agreement between two or more hostile parties, usually countries or governments, which formally ends a state of war between the parties. It is different from an armistice, which is an agreement to stop the fighting, and from a ceasefire or a surrender, in which one party agrees to give up arms under conditions. Peace treaties are governed in modern international law by customary rules largely codified in the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties.

Contents

Peace treaties typically address the issues that caused the conflict and settle the parties' future relations. Common elements include the formal restoration of peace, definitions of borders and territorial cessions, reparations or indemnities, demilitarisation or arms limitations, prisoner exchanges and amnesties, trade and diplomatic relations, and mechanisms for resolving future disputes.

History

One of the earliest surviving peace treaties is the Egyptian–Hittite treaty of about 1259 BCE between Ramesses II and Ḫattušili III, following the Battle of Kadesh; a copy is displayed at the United Nations. Famous later examples include the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which reshaped the European state system; the Treaty of Utrecht (1713); the Congress of Vienna settlement (1815); the Treaty of Versailles (1919) after the First World War; and the treaties and armistices that closed the Second World War, including the Korean Armistice of 1953 — notable because no peace treaty followed, leaving the Korean states technically at war.

Peace process

Before a treaty, hostile parties usually pass through preliminary steps: a ceasefire, negotiations, and sometimes a preliminary or framework agreement. Ratification by each state is normally required before a treaty enters into force.
