Washington, D.C. hardcore

Washington, D.C. hardcore, often referred to simply as D.C. hardcore, is the hardcore punk scene of Washington, D.C., one of the most influential in the history of the genre. Emerging in the late 1970s and early 1980s around bands such as Bad Brains, Minor Threat and the Teen Idles, the scene was anchored by the independent label Dischord Records and a strong do-it-yourself ethic.

Origins

The Bad Brains, formed in 1977, pioneered the speed and intensity of hardcore in D.C., inspiring a generation of younger bands. Jeff Nelson and Ian MacKaye formed the Teen Idles in 1979 and founded Dischord Records in 1980 to release the band’s Minor Disturbance EP — among the first hardcore records on the East Coast. MacKaye’s next band, Minor Threat, wrote “Straight Edge” (1981), which gave its name to the abstinence-oriented straight edge movement.

Development

Bands such as State of Alert (fronted by Henry Rollins before he joined Black Flag), Government Issue, Marginal Man, Double-O and Scream carried the scene through the early 1980s, playing all-ages shows at venues like the 9:30 Club and community spaces, and keeping ticket and record prices deliberately low. A violent skinhead element and police attention strained the scene in the mid-1980s.

Revolution Summer and after

In 1985, activists in the scene proclaimed “Revolution Summer”, emphasising politics, community and a new musical direction. Bands such as Rites of Spring, Embrace and Beefeater expanded hardcore’s emotional and melodic range, laying groundwork later labelled post-hardcore and emo. MacKaye’s next band, Fugazi (formed 1987), became one of the most respected independent rock acts in the world while adhering to Dischord’s $5-show ethos. Later D.C. acts including Jawbox, Shudder to Think and Q and Not U continued the scene’s influence into the 1990s and 2000s.

Legacy

D.C. hardcore is credited with shaping straight edge, post-hardcore, emo and the economics of independent music. The scene is documented in the book Dance of Days and in the Dischord archives, and many of its recordings remain in print on the label.
