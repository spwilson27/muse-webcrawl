Calcite

Formula: CaCO3
Crystal system: Trigonal
Hardness: 3 (defining mineral)
Color: Colorless, white, or varied

Calcite is a carbonate mineral and the most stable polymorph of calcium carbonate (CaCO3). It is a very common mineral, a major constituent of sedimentary rocks such as limestone and chalk, and the primary mineral in metamorphic marble. Calcite defines hardness 3 on the Mohs scale.

Properties

Calcite crystallizes in the trigonal system, commonly as rhombohedral and scalenohedral crystals. It is strongly birefringent: a clear variety known as Iceland spar displays striking double refraction, so that text viewed through it appears doubled — a property used by Erasmus Bartholin's experiments in the seventeenth century and important in the development of optics. Calcite reacts vigorously with dilute hydrochloric acid, effervescing as carbon dioxide is released, a standard field test distinguishing it from dolomite.

Occurrence and formation

Calcite forms by chemical precipitation from calcium-rich waters and by the accumulation of the shells and skeletons of marine organisms. It is the material of stalactites and stalagmites in caves, of travertine deposits at hot springs, and of the tests of foraminifera and coccolithophores whose remains form chalk. Most limestones consist largely of calcite, as do carbonatite igneous rocks.

Uses

Calcite, in the form of limestone and marble, is used in construction, as the raw material for cement and lime, as a flux in steelmaking, as agricultural lime, and as a filler and pigment (whiting) in paper, paint, and plastics. Optical-grade Iceland spar was historically used in Nicol prisms for polarizing microscopes and optical instruments.
