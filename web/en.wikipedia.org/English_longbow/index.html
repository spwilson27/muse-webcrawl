The English longbow was a powerful medieval type of longbow — about 6 ft (1.8 m) long — used by the English and Welsh for hunting and as a weapon of war. Capable of driving an arrow through armour at range, it allowed English armies to defeat numerically superior opponents, most famously at the battles of Crécy (1346), Poitiers (1356) and Agincourt (1415) during the Hundred Years' War.

Design and use

The classic war bow was a self bow made from a single stave of yew, exploiting the natural composite of pale sapwood (strong in tension) on the back and dark heartwood (strong in compression) on the belly. Draw weights of surviving Mary Rose bows are estimated at 100–185 lb, demanding years of training; English law repeatedly required regular archery practice, and the skeletons of archers show characteristic deformities from a lifetime of shooting. A trained archer could loose ten or more aimed arrows per minute, and massed volleys darkened the sky in contemporary accounts.

History

Derived from Welsh bow traditions adopted by Edward I, the longbow became the signature weapon of English armies from the late 13th century. Its dominance waned as plate armour improved and firearms spread; the last major battlefield use by English forces is often given as the Battle of Flodden (1513), though bows were still mustered into the Tudor era, and the Mary Rose sank in 1545 carrying 137 longbows and thousands of arrows. The longbow's legend — the yeoman archer as national hero — endures in English culture.
