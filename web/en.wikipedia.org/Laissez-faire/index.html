Laissez-faire

Laissez-faire (French: [lɛse fɛːʁ], literally "let do") is a type of economic system in which transactions between private groups are free from any form of economic interventionism, such as regulation and subsidies. Derived from a maxim of the French physiocrats, the phrase expresses a preference for a minimal state, free markets, and individual liberty in economic affairs.

History

The phrase is traditionally attributed to the Marquis d'Argenson (c. 1751) and to a legendary exchange between the French finance minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert and a group of merchants led by a certain Legendre, who replied "Laissez-nous faire" ("Leave us to do it"). The physiocrat Vincent de Gournay popularised the maxim "laissez faire, laissez passer" ("let do, let pass").

The doctrine was given its most influential statement by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations (1776), which argued that individuals pursuing their own interest are led by an "invisible hand" to promote the public good. Smith nevertheless assigned the state essential duties: defence, justice, public works, and education.

Principles

Laissez-faire rests on the propositions that the natural economic order is one of freedom, that markets coordinate production and distribution through prices, and that government intervention generally distorts this order. Its classical components include low taxes, minimal regulation, free trade, private property rights, and freedom of contract. Nineteenth-century liberal governments in Britain and the United States approximated these ideals to varying degrees, though neither practised pure laissez-faire.

Criticism

Critics argue that unregulated markets produce monopolies, externalities such as pollution, inadequate public goods, business cycles, and severe inequality. The Great Depression and the writings of John Maynard Keynes led most Western governments toward mixed economies with active fiscal and regulatory policy. Defenders respond that many failures attributed to markets result from government intervention itself, and that voluntary exchange remains the most effective engine of prosperity.
