Active voice

The active voice is a grammatical voice in which the subject of a sentence performs the action expressed by the verb — the agent is the subject, and the patient (if any) is the object. It is the unmarked, default voice in English and in most nominative–accusative languages, in contrast to the passive voice, in which the patient becomes the subject.

"The cat chased the mouse." (active) — compare "The mouse was chased by the cat." (passive)

Characteristics

In an active clause, word order and case marking align the agent with the grammatical subject. Active constructions are generally shorter, more direct, and easier to process, which is why style guides frequently recommend them over passives. However, the passive has legitimate uses: when the agent is unknown, unimportant, or deliberately de-emphasised, and in much scientific writing.

Across languages

Many languages mark the active voice morphologically on the verb. Classical Greek and Latin distinguished active, middle, and passive endings. Some languages, such as the ergative languages, treat the subjects of intransitive and transitive verbs differently, and others possess additional voices — antipassive, applicative, causative — that rearrange the roles of agent and patient. English forms its active voice with the plain verb forms, reserving auxiliary constructions (be + past participle) for the passive.

Usage writers sometimes mislabel any sentence containing a form of be, or any weak or wordy sentence, as "passive"; grammarians caution that the true passive requires a participle construction promoting the object to subject.
