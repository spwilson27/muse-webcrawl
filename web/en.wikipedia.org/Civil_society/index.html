Civil society

Civil society can be understood as the "third sector" of society, distinct from government and business, and including the family and the private sphere. By other authors, civil society is used in the sense of (1) the aggregate of non-governmental organisations and institutions that manifest interests and will of citizens or (2) individuals and organisations in a society which are independent of the government. Sometimes the term civil society is used in the more general sense of "the elements such as freedom of speech, an independent judiciary, etc, that make up a democratic society" (Collins English Dictionary).

History of the concept

The idea descends from concepts of the polis and societas civilis in Greek and Roman thought. In the Enlightenment, thinkers such as John Locke and Adam Ferguson treated civil society as the sphere governed by civil law, distinct from the state of nature. Hegel placed it between the family and the state; Alexis de Tocqueville emphasised voluntary associations as schools of democracy in America; Antonio Gramsci analysed civil society as the terrain on which cultural hegemony is built and contested.

Contemporary use

The term surged after 1989, when dissident movements in Central and Eastern Europe — such as Poland's Solidarity — were described as civil society reasserting itself against the state. Development agencies and the World Bank now fund "civil society organisations" worldwide, and political scientists such as Robert Putnam link dense associational life to social capital and effective governance. Contemporary civil society includes charities, community groups, trade unions, faith groups, professional associations, foundations, and online networks.

Criticism

Critics note that civil society is not automatically democratic or benign — it can include exclusionary or extremist groups — and that reliance on NGOs may depoliticise social change or make organisations accountable to donors rather than members.
