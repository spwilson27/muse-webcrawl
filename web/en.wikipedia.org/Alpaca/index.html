Alpaca

The alpaca (Lama pacos, formerly Vicugna pacos) is a species of South American camelid mammal domesticated from the wild vicuña several thousand years ago. Alpacas are kept in herds on the high Altiplano of Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador and are bred primarily for their soft, warm fleece.

Characteristics

Adult alpacas stand about 81–99 cm at the withers and weigh 48–90 kg. There are two fleece types: the Huacaya, whose dense, crimped wool gives a teddy-bear appearance and accounts for the great majority of the world population, and the rarer Suri, whose fibre hangs in long, silky locks. Unlike llamas, which were bred as pack animals, alpacas were bred solely for fibre, which is hollow-cored, hypoallergenic (lacking lanolin), naturally water-resistant, and comes in more than twenty recognized natural colours.

History and husbandry

Alpacas were central to Andean cultures for millennia; the Inca valued their fibre above gold. The Spanish conquest devastated herds, but millions survive today, with Peru holding the great majority of the world population. Since the late twentieth century alpaca farming has spread to North America, Australia, and Europe. Female alpacas are induced ovulators with a gestation of about eleven and a half months, producing a single cria. Herds communicate with a soft humming sound and, like other camelids, may spit when distressed.
