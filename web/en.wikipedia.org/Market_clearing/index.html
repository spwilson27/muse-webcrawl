Market clearing

Market clearing is the process by which, in an economic market, the supply of whatever is traded is equated to the demand so that there is no leftover supply or unmet demand. It is an economic model based on the assumption that the market is in a state of equilibrium: prices adjust until the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded.

Definition

The market-clearing price is the price at which the quantity of a good or service demanded by buyers equals the quantity supplied by sellers. At any price above the clearing price, supply exceeds demand and a surplus (excess supply) results, putting downward pressure on price. At any price below it, demand exceeds supply and a shortage (excess demand) results, putting upward pressure on price. The concept is central to supply and demand analysis and to general equilibrium theory.

Mechanisms

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how markets move toward clearing. The Walrasian auction imagines an auctioneer who announces prices and adjusts them in response to excess demand, a process known as tâtonnement, with no trading taking place until equilibrium is reached. In real markets, adjustment may instead occur through inventories, queues, rationing, or changes in production. When prices are sticky or regulated — for example by price ceilings or floors — markets may fail to clear, producing persistent surpluses or shortages.

Applications

The clearing concept is applied in labour economics, where the market-clearing wage equates labour supply with labour demand; in financial markets, where exchanges match buy and sell orders; and in auction design, where a clearing price may be used to allocate many identical items at a single uniform price. Critics note that actual markets rarely reach perfect clearing and that frictions such as search costs, asymmetric information and menu costs can sustain disequilibrium.
