Free people of color

Free people of color (French: gens de couleur libres) were people of African descent in colonial and early-national America who were legally free — not enslaved — but who nonetheless faced extensive legal and social restrictions. The category existed throughout the Atlantic slave societies, and free people of color formed significant communities in places such as Charleston, New Orleans, and the Caribbean.

Origins and status

People became free through manumission, self-purchase, birth to a free mother (under the doctrine partus sequitur ventrem, the child's status followed the mother), military service, or successful freedom suits. By 1860 there were roughly 488,000 free African Americans in the United States, about 11 percent of the Black population; most lived in the Upper South and the North, but notable communities existed in the Lower South.

Restrictions

Free people of color were subject to discriminatory laws: limits on movement and assembly, curfews, requirements to register and carry freedom papers, exclusion from voting and office (with exceptions that narrowed over time), and bans on testifying against white people in some jurisdictions. After slave revolts and plots — such as Denmark Vesey's alleged conspiracy in Charleston in 1822 and Nat Turner's rebellion in 1831 — Southern legislatures further restricted manumission and free Black life.

Communities and achievements

Despite restrictions, free people of color built churches, schools, mutual-aid societies, and businesses; some became wealthy artisans, merchants, and, in Louisiana and South Carolina, slaveholders themselves. In New Orleans, the French-speaking Creole community of color produced notable educators, writers, and musicians. Free Black abolitionists were central to the movement against slavery, and over 180,000 Black men served in the Union forces during the American Civil War. The group's legal disabilities were formally ended by the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments, though Jim Crow laws soon imposed new forms of segregation.
