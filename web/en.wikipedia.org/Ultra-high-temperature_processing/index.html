Ultra-high-temperature processing

Ultra-high-temperature processing (UHT), also called ultra-heat treatment or ultra-pasteurization, is a food-processing technology that sterilizes liquid food by heating it above 135 °C (275 °F)—typically to between 135 and 150 °C—for a very short time, usually one to four seconds. The product is then packaged aseptically in sterile containers.

UHT is most commonly applied to milk, but is also used for cream, fruit juices, soy milk, wine, soups, and liquid egg. UHT milk can be stored unopened at room temperature for six to nine months. The process kills bacterial spores that survive ordinary pasteurization (HTST), at the cost of some alteration in flavour—often described as slightly "cooked" or sweeter—and small losses of some vitamins.

Two main methods exist: direct heating, in which steam is injected into or infused with the product, and indirect heating through plate or tubular heat exchangers. The technology was developed in the 1960s, with Tetra Pak a leading equipment maker, and UHT milk now dominates milk sales in much of Europe and Latin America.
