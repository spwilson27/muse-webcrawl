The Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 was a United States federal statute that created the territories of Kansas and Nebraska and opened them to settlement. Its most consequential provision allowed the settlers of each territory to decide for themselves whether to permit slavery — the doctrine of "popular sovereignty" — thereby repealing the Missouri Compromise of 1820, which had prohibited slavery north of latitude 36°30′ in the Louisiana Purchase lands.

Background and passage

The bill was driven by Illinois senator Stephen A. Douglas, who sought to organise the central plains for settlement and to secure a transcontinental railroad route favourable to Chicago; gaining Southern support required abandoning the Missouri Compromise restriction. After bitter debate, the act passed and was signed by President Franklin Pierce on 30 May 1854.

Consequences

The act detonated national politics. Pro-slavery and anti-slavery settlers rushed into Kansas, producing rival governments and the violence known as "Bleeding Kansas," including the sack of Lawrence and the Pottawatomie massacre. Outrage in the North destroyed the Whig Party and catalysed the creation of the Republican Party in 1854, dedicated to halting the expansion of slavery. The act is widely regarded as one of the pivotal steps on the road to the American Civil War.
