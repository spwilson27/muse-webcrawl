Übermensch

The Übermensch (German for "Overman", "Super-man" or "Above-human"; plural Übermenschen) is a concept in the philosophy of Friedrich Nietzsche, most prominently introduced in his book Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883–1885). In this work, Nietzsche's protagonist Zarathustra presents the Übermensch as a goal for humanity to set for itself: the human being is something to be overcome, "a rope, tied between beast and overman — a rope over an abyss".

The concept

Nietzsche developed the concept in the context of his diagnosis that traditional morality, grounded in Christianity and Platonism, had lost its authority — the "death of God". The Übermensch is the figure who responds to this crisis by creating his own values rather than submitting to inherited or "herd" morality. This act of self-overcoming (Selbstüberwindung) and value creation is associated with Nietzsche's ideas of the will to power, affirmation of life, and amor fati, the love of one's fate.

The Übermensch is contrasted with the "last man" (letzter Mensch), the comfortable, risk-averse product of modernity who seeks only security and contentment and whom Zarathustra's audience perversely prefers. Nietzsche never described the Übermensch as a literally existing person, and interpretations differ over whether it represents an attainable human type, a regulative ideal, or a symbol of self-transcendence.

Interpretations

Scholars debate the meaning of the concept. Some read it ethically, as a call to creative self-legislation and honesty about the sources of one's values. Others emphasise its aesthetic or cultural dimensions: the Übermensch as artist of his own character. Walter Kaufmann's influential mid-20th-century interpretation presented the Übermensch as a self-disciplined, creative individual who has overcome ressentiment, in contrast to crude biological or political readings.

Appropriation and misuse

The term was adopted, in distorted form, by Nazi ideology, which recast the Übermensch as a racial ideal; this appropriation was promoted by Nietzsche's sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, who edited his unpublished notes and aligned his legacy with nationalism and antisemitism — positions Nietzsche himself had despised. Most scholars regard the Nazi reading as a falsification, noting Nietzsche's contempt for antisemitism and German nationalism, and that his "overman" concerns individual self-overcoming, not racial supremacy.

In popular culture

The concept influenced literature and popular culture, including George Bernard Shaw's "Superman" in Man and Superman, and, indirectly, the naming of the comic-book character Superman, whose creators drew on the English translation of the term. "Übermensch" has also been used in music, film and video games, usually to suggest a being beyond ordinary human limits.
