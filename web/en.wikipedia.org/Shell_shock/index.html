Shell shock

Shell shock is a term coined in World War I by the British psychologist Charles Samuel Myers to describe the type of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) that many soldiers experienced during the war, before PTSD was officially recognised. It was initially thought to be caused by physical injury to the nerves from the shock of exploding shells.

Symptoms and history

Symptoms included trembling, confusion, nightmares, impaired sight and hearing, paralysis, and an inability to function, sometimes described as the "thousand-yard stare". Tens of thousands of British soldiers were treated for shell shock; many more cases went unrecognised, and some sufferers were accused of cowardice or malingering — hundreds of British soldiers were executed for desertion or cowardice, many now believed to have been suffering from the condition.

Treatment and legacy

Treatments ranged from rest and talk therapy — W. H. R. Rivers' work with officers such as Siegfried Sassoon at Craiglockhart War Hospital became famous — to disciplinary and physical methods. Analogous terms in later conflicts include "battle fatigue" and "combat stress reaction". Shell shock remains a powerful cultural symbol of the psychological cost of industrialised warfare; in 2006 the British government issued posthumous pardons to executed soldiers of the First World War.
