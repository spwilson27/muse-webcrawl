List of cuisines

A cuisine is a characteristic style of cooking practices and traditions, often associated with a specific region, nation or culture, shaped by available ingredients, climate, trade, religion and history. This article lists notable cuisines of the world, grouped by region. Cuisines evolve continually through migration and exchange ("fusion cuisine"), and national cuisines typically contain many regional variants.

Asia

  • Chinese — with regional schools including Cantonese, Sichuan, Hunan, Shandong and Jiangsu; dim sum, noodles, stir-frying and wok cookery.
  • Japanese — sushi, ramen, tempura, washoku traditions emphasising seasonality.
  • Korean — kimchi, barbecue, bibimbap, banchan side dishes.
  • Indian — highly regional: North Indian breads and tandoor dishes, South Indian dosas and rice dishes, Bengali fish cookery; extensive vegetarian traditions.
  • Thai, Vietnamese (pho, banh mi), Indonesian (rendang, satay), Malaysian, Filipino, Persian, Turkish, Levantine/Arab (mezze, kebabs), Georgian, Central Asian and Mongolian cuisines.

Europe

  • French — haute cuisine and regional cooking; Italian — pasta, pizza, regional diversity; Spanish — paella, tapas; Greek, Portuguese, German, British (full breakfast, roasts, fish and chips), Irish, Scandinavian/Nordic (including the New Nordic movement), Polish, Hungarian (goulash), Russian, Ukrainian (borscht), Balkan and Dutch cuisines.

Americas

  • Mexican — maize, beans, chillies; tacos, moles; recognised by UNESCO. Peruvian — ceviche, Andean potatoes, Nikkei fusion. Brazilian — feijoada, churrasco. Argentinian, Caribbean (Jamaican jerk, Cuban), and North American cuisines: United States regional styles (Southern and soul food, Cajun and Creole, Tex-Mex, New England, barbecue traditions), Canadian (poutine, tourtière) and Indigenous cuisines.

Africa and Oceania

  • Ethiopian (injera, wats), Moroccan (tagines, couscous), Egyptian, West African (jollof rice, groundnut stews), South African (braai, bobotie), Madagascan; in Oceania: Australian and New Zealand cuisines, Māori hāngī, and Pacific island traditions.

Other cross-cutting categories include Jewish cuisine, Mediterranean cuisine, vegetarian and vegan traditions, and historical cuisines such as ancient Roman and medieval European cookery.
