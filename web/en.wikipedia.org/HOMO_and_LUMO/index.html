HOMO and LUMO

In molecular orbital theory, the HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) and the LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) are the frontier orbitals of a molecule. The HOMO is the highest-energy orbital that contains electrons, and can be thought of as analogous to the valence band maximum in a solid; the LUMO is the lowest-energy orbital that is empty, analogous to the conduction band minimum.

Significance

The energy difference between them, the HOMO–LUMO gap, governs a molecule's optical absorption, chemical reactivity and electrical conductivity. Molecules with a small gap are generally softer, more polarisable and more reactive, and absorb longer-wavelength light; large-gap molecules are harder and more kinetically stable. In frontier molecular orbital theory, developed by Kenichi Fukui, chemical reactions are understood largely through the interaction of the HOMO of one reactant with the LUMO of another: the HOMO acts as an electron donor (nucleophilic character) and the LUMO as an electron acceptor (electrophilic character).

Applications

HOMO and LUMO energy levels are central to the design of organic semiconductors, OLEDs, dye-sensitised solar cells and photocatalysts, where alignment of a donor's HOMO with an acceptor's LUMO determines charge transfer. They are routinely estimated by cyclic voltammetry, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy and density functional theory calculations.

Related concepts for single-electron systems include the SOMO (singly occupied molecular orbital). The older terms NHOMO and SLUMO refer to the next orbitals below the HOMO and above the LUMO respectively.
