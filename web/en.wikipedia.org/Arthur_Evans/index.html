Arthur Evans

Sir Arthur John Evans FRS FBA FREng (8 July 1851 – 11 July 1941) was a British archaeologist and journalist, best known for excavating the palace of Knossos on the Greek island of Crete and for uncovering the civilization he named Minoan, after the legendary King Minos. His work transformed understanding of the Bronze Age Aegean.

Life

Evans was the son of the geologist and antiquary Sir John Evans. Educated at Harrow, Oxford and Göttingen, he worked as a journalist in the Balkans, where his reporting on uprisings against Ottoman rule led to his expulsion, and later became keeper of the Ashmolean Museum (1884–1908), which he greatly expanded.

Knossos

Following intriguing seal-stones and the earlier soundings of Minos Kalokairinos and Heinrich Schliemann, Evans bought the Kephala hill at Knossos and from 1900 excavated the vast Bronze Age palace complex. He uncovered storerooms, frescoes, and clay tablets inscribed in two scripts he named Linear A and Linear B, and structured his findings in the four-volume The Palace of Minos (1921–1935). Evans controversially restored parts of the palace in reinforced concrete, with repainted frescoes — reconstructions that still divide opinion. Linear B was deciphered as Greek by Michael Ventris in 1952, after Evans's death.
