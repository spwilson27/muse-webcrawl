Existentialism

Existentialism is a form of philosophical enquiry that explores the issue of human existence. Existentialist philosophers explore the relationship between the seeming arbitrariness, meaninglessness, or purpose of the individual's life and the individual's responsibility in creating meaning and living authentically. Its central claim is often summarised as "existence precedes essence": humans define their own meaning rather than discovering a pre-given nature.

Key figures

Antecedents include Søren Kierkegaard and Friedrich Nietzsche in the 19th century, and Fyodor Dostoevsky's novels. In the 20th century the movement is associated with Martin Heidegger, Karl Jaspers, Jean-Paul Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir, Albert Camus (though he rejected the label), and Maurice Merleau-Ponty. Sartre's 1946 lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism popularised the term.

Concepts

Recurring themes include absurdity (the conflict between the human desire for meaning and a silent universe), anguish and anxiety in the face of radical freedom, authenticity versus "bad faith" (mauvaise foi), facticity, and the confrontation with death and nothingness.

Influence

Existentialism influenced literature, theatre (the Theatre of the Absurd), film, theology, and existential psychotherapy, and remains a staple of introductory philosophy curricula.
