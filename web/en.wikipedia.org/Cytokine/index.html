Cytokine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cytokines are a broad and loose category of small proteins (~5–25 kDa) important in cell signalling. Cytokines are peptides and cannot cross the lipid bilayer of cells to enter the cytoplasm; they act by binding to specific receptors on cell surfaces. They are released by many cell types, especially immune cells, and regulate immunity, inflammation, and haematopoiesis.

Families

  • Interleukins (IL-1 through IL-38) — mediate communication between leukocytes.
  • Interferons (IFN-α, -β, -γ) — antiviral and immunomodulatory.
  • Tumour necrosis factors (TNF) — inflammation and apoptosis.
  • Chemokines — direct cell migration (chemotaxis).
  • Colony-stimulating factors — growth of blood cell progenitors.
  • Transforming growth factors (TGF-β) — regulation of proliferation and differentiation.

Function and clinical relevance

Cytokines act in cascades, with one cytokine stimulating cells to release others, and often display pleiotropy and redundancy. Excessive release can be pathological: the "cytokine storm" (cytokine release syndrome) is a dangerous hyperinflammatory state seen in severe infections and some immunotherapies. Cytokines and their receptors are major drug targets: recombinant interferons and interleukins are used therapeutically, and blocking agents such as anti-TNF antibodies and IL-6 receptor antagonists treat autoimmune disease.
