Sulfur

Sulfur (in British English, sulphur) is a chemical element with the symbol S and atomic number 16. It is an abundant, multivalent non-metal. Under normal conditions, sulfur atoms form cyclic octatomic molecules with the chemical formula S8. Elemental sulfur is a bright yellow, crystalline solid at room temperature.

Properties and occurrence

Sulfur is the fifth most common element by mass in the Earth. It occurs natively around hot springs and volcanic regions, and widely in sulfide and sulfate minerals such as pyrite, galena, cinnabar, and gypsum. It is essential to all life: the amino acids cysteine and methionine contain sulfur, as do the vitamins biotin and thiamine. In its allotropes, rhombic (α-) sulfur is the most stable form; it melts at 115.2 °C and burns with a blue flame, producing pungent sulfur dioxide.

Production and uses

Today almost all elemental sulfur is recovered from petroleum and natural gas via the Claus process, after early mining by the Frasch process. By far the largest use is conversion to sulfuric acid — the world's highest-volume industrial chemical — for fertiliser manufacture (phosphates). Sulfur is also used in vulcanisation of rubber, fungicides and insecticides, black gunpowder, matches, and pharmaceuticals.

History

Known since antiquity as "brimstone", sulfur was used in China in gunpowder and in Greece as a fumigant. Antoine Lavoisier recognised it as an element in 1777.
