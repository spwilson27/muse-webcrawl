Sclera

Part of: Eye

System: Visual system

Function: Protective outer coat of the eyeball

The sclera, also known as the white of the eye, is the opaque, fibrous, protective outer layer of the human eye. It covers roughly five-sixths of the eyeball's surface, extending from the cornea at the front to the optic nerve at the back.

Structure

The sclera is composed mainly of collagen and elastic fibres arranged in irregular bundles, which give it strength and help the eyeball keep its shape. It is continuous with the cornea at the front and with the dural sheath of the optic nerve at the back. The extraocular muscles that move the eye attach to the sclera.

Function

The sclera protects the inner structures of the eye, maintains the globe's shape against intraocular pressure, and provides attachment points for the muscles controlling eye movement. Its white colour comes from the scattering of light by its dense, disorganised collagen.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the sclera is called scleritis. In jaundice the sclera may appear yellow, and in some connective-tissue disorders such as osteogenesis imperfecta it can take on a blue tint because the underlying choroid shows through.
