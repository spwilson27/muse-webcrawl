Atrium (architecture)

Atrium
Original form: Central hall of a Roman domus
Features: Compluvium (open roof), impluvium (rain pool)
Modern form: Large open space, often glazed, within a building

In architecture, an atrium (plural: atria or atriums) is a large open-air or skylight-covered space surrounded by a building. The term originates in the central hall of the ancient Roman domus, and atria were a common feature of early Christian churches. In modern architecture, an atrium is a large open space, often several storeys high with a glazed roof, forming a social and circulation core for offices, hotels, and public buildings.

Roman atrium

The atrium was the most important reception room of a Roman house, where the owner received clients in the morning ritual of the salutatio. Its roof, the compluvium, sloped inward to an opening that admitted light and rain, which fell into a shallow pool, the impluvium, used to collect water. Types described by Vitruvius include the Tuscan, Corinthian, and tetrastyle atria, differing in how the roof was supported.

Modern atria

Revived in the 19th century in arcades and conservatories, the atrium became a signature of modern commercial architecture after John Portman's Hyatt Regency Atlanta (1967) popularised the dramatic multi-storey lobby. Atria bring daylight into deep floor plans but pose challenges for fire and smoke control, which is addressed by dedicated smoke-extraction and sprinkler systems.
