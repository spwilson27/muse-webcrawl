Parmalim

Parmalim, referred to as Malim or formally Ugamo Malim, is the modern form of the indigenous religion of the Batak people of North Sumatra, Indonesia. Practised chiefly by the Toba Batak around Lake Toba, it is a monotheistic faith centred on worship of Debata Mula Jadi Na Bolon, the creator god of traditional Batak belief, and is officially organised as the Agama Malim under Indonesia's system of recognised religions.

Origins

Traditional Batak religion combined worship of a high creator with veneration of ancestral spirits (sombaon) and the ministrations of priest-diviners (datu). In the late 19th century, as Christian missions and Dutch colonial rule expanded, the Batak priest-king Sisingamangaraja XII led armed and spiritual resistance until his death in 1907. In the following decades his followers, notably Guru Somalaing and later Raja Mulia Naipospos, reshaped the old faith into an organised, scripture- and congregation-based religion — Parmalim — partly in dialogue with the Christianity pressing in around it. Raja Mulia Naipospos is credited with codifying its doctrines and rites in the 1920s–1950s and with founding its central sanctuary at Huta Tinggi, Laguboti, near Lake Toba.

Beliefs and practice

Parmalim teaches belief in one supreme God, Debata Mula Jadi Na Bolon, and venerates Sisingamangaraja as God's messenger (ulubalang). Adherents hold weekly worship on Saturday in a prayer house called a bale pasogit, led by religious teachers (ulupunguan), with prayers, offerings, and sermons. Major festivals include Sipaha Sada, Sipaha Lima (a thanksgiving for the harvest involving sacrificial offerings), and the commemoration of Sisingamangaraja's birth and death. Moral teachings emphasise honesty, care for creation — the movement is noted for a strong ethic of environmental stewardship around Lake Toba — and communal solidarity.

Legal status

Under the New Order, indigenous faiths (aliran kepercayaan) were denied full status as "religions," and Parmalim adherents struggled for recognition on identity cards and marriage registration. Court rulings in the reform era — notably a 2017 Constitutional Court decision affirming that indigenous faiths may be recorded in the religion column of identity documents — have improved their formal standing, though Parmalim remains distinct from Indonesia's six officially promoted religions.
