Palladian architecture is a European architectural style derived from the work of the Venetian architect Andrea Palladio (1508–1580). What is recognised today as Palladian architecture is an evolution of Palladio's original concepts, defined by strict symmetry, adherence to classical temple architecture, and mathematical proportion, and disseminated through his treatise I quattro libri dell'architettura (The Four Books of Architecture, 1570).

Palladio's villas and churches

Working for the patricians of the Venetian Republic, Palladio designed country villas across the Veneto — such as Villa Barbaro at Maser and the perfectly symmetrical Villa Rotonda — combining working farm buildings with temple-fronted residences. In Venice he built great churches including San Giorgio Maggiore and Il Redentore. His designs were inscribed by UNESCO as the "City of Vicenza and the Palladian Villas of the Veneto" World Heritage Site.

Spread and revivals

Inigo Jones introduced Palladian principles to England in the early 17th century (Queen's House, Greenwich; the Banqueting House, Whitehall). A full Palladian revival in early-18th-century Britain, led by Lord Burlington and Colen Campbell, made the style dominant for country houses and influenced architecture across the British world, including Thomas Jefferson's Monticello and the University of Virginia in the United States. Later echoes include Neo-Palladianism and the style's use for civic and plantation buildings worldwide.

Characteristic features

Signature elements include rigorous bilateral symmetry, a central portico modelled on a classical temple front, the hierarchy of the classical orders, rusticated basements, and the "Palladian" (or Venetian) window — a central arched opening flanked by two narrower flat-topped ones. Interiors followed harmonic proportions derived from musical ratios.
