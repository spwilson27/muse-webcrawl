Wilderness first aid

Wilderness first aid is the provision of first aid under conditions where the arrival of emergency responders or the evacuation of an injured person may be delayed because of adverse weather, difficult terrain, distance, or lack of equipment. It differs from standard first aid in its emphasis on extended patient care, improvised equipment, and decisions about evacuation.

Scope and training

Courses in wilderness first aid (often abbreviated WFA) typically run 16 to 24 hours and cover patient assessment, management of bleeding and fractures, splinting with improvised materials, treatment of hypothermia, heat illness, altitude sickness, and anaphylaxis, and the construction of litters. More advanced qualifications include Wilderness Advanced First Aid (WAFA) and Wilderness First Responder (WFR), an approximately 80-hour course regarded as the standard for professional guides and outdoor leaders. Providers include national Red Cross societies and organisations such as NOLS Wilderness Medicine.

Principles

Because definitive care may be hours or days away, wilderness first aid extends the usual ABC (airway, breathing, circulation) assessment into long-term monitoring, wound cleaning to prevent infection, hydration and thermoregulation, and careful documentation. Decision-making about whether, how, and how urgently to evacuate a patient is a central skill.
