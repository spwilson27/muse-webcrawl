The Rub' al Khali (Arabic: الربع الخالي, "the Empty Quarter") is the largest continuous sand desert (erg) in the world, comprising most of the southern third of the Arabian Peninsula. Covering some 650,000 square kilometres, it stretches across parts of Saudi Arabia, Oman, the United Arab Emirates and Yemen — an area larger than France.

Geography and climate

The desert is roughly 1,000 kilometres long and 500 kilometres wide. Its surface is dominated by longitudinal and crescentic dunes, some rising more than 250 metres, interspersed with gravel plains and salt flats (sabkhas). Summer temperatures regularly exceed 50 °C, and annual rainfall is typically less than 35 millimetres, making it one of the most inhospitable environments on Earth. Even the Bedouin tribes historically skirted its edges rather than crossing its interior.

Exploration

The first documented crossing by a European was made by Bertram Thomas in 1930–31, followed in 1932 by St John Philby, who searched for the legendary lost city of Iram. Wilfred Thesiger crossed the Empty Quarter twice in 1946–48 with Bedouin companions, an experience recorded in his classic travel book Arabian Sands (1959).

Resources

Beneath the sands lie some of the world's richest petroleum deposits, including Saudi Arabia's Shaybah field and parts of the supergiant Ghawar field near its northern margin. Groundwater from ancient aquifers, and evidence of prehistoric lakes and river systems, show that the region was once far wetter, supporting human and animal life during humid phases of the late Pleistocene and early Holocene.
