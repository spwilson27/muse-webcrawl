Arboretum

An arboretum (plural: arboreta) is a botanical collection composed exclusively or principally of trees and other woody plants, intended at least partly for scientific study. Arboreta serve as living libraries for research in taxonomy, forestry and horticulture, as seed banks for conservation, and as public parks and educational resources. A specialist collection of conifers is called a pinetum; one of fruit trees, a fruticetum or pomarium.

History

The term "arboretum" was first used in English by John Claudius Loudon in 1833. Early examples include the Arboretum Trsteno near Dubrovnik, Croatia, dating to around 1492, and the arboretum planted at Westonbirt, England, from 1829 by Robert Stayner Holford. In North America, the Arnold Arboretum of Harvard University (established 1872, designed with Frederick Law Olmsted) and the United States National Arboretum in Washington, D.C. (1927) became leading centres of woody-plant research.

Functions

Modern arboreta maintain documented collections with accession records tracing each plant's provenance. They support breeding programmes, trials of species for changing climates, preservation of rare and endangered trees, and public engagement through displays that group trees by family, geography or season of interest. Many participate in international seed-exchange networks coordinated through Botanic Gardens Conservation International (BGCI).

Notable arboreta

Notable examples include Westonbirt National Arboretum and the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew's arboretum in England; the Arnold Arboretum in Boston; the Morton Arboretum in Illinois; and the Kalmthout Arboretum in Belgium.
