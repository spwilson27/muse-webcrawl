Flat cap

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A flat cap is a rounded cap with a small stiff brim in front, originating in the British Isles in the 16th century. The cap is known by many names, including ivyle cap, scally cap, newsboy cap (a fuller variant), paddy cap, bunnet (in Scotland), and dai cap (in Wales). It has long been associated with working-class dress in Britain and Ireland, and later with country pursuits, golf, and fashion.

History

In 1571 the English Parliament passed an act intended to stimulate the domestic wool trade requiring all non-noble males over the age of six to wear a woollen cap on Sundays and holidays, on pain of a fine — one of the forces that entrenched the flat cap in everyday dress. Although the law was repealed in 1597, the style endured. By the late 19th and early 20th centuries the flat cap was worn across the social spectrum in Britain, from labourers to gentlemen shooting or playing golf.

Construction

Traditional flat caps are sewn from tweed, wool, or cotton panels joined to form a soft, close-fitting crown that is pulled forward over a short peak. The peak is usually stiffened and may snap to the crown. The newsboy variant has a fuller, baggier crown, often made of eight panels topped with a button.

Regional and cultural associations

In Scotland the tweed bunnet remains a symbol of rural working dress. Irish immigrants carried the style to the United States, where the scally cap became associated with Boston's Irish-American community. In the 2010s the television series Peaky Blinders revived interest in the style worldwide. Flat caps are also part of classic golfing attire and of the "country gentleman" look associated with Barbour jackets.
