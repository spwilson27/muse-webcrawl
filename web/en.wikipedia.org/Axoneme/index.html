Axoneme

The axoneme is the core cytoskeletal structure inside cilia and flagella of eukaryotic cells. It consists of microtubules arranged in a characteristic pattern, together with associated motor and linker proteins, and is responsible for the beating motion of these organelles.

Structure

In motile cilia and flagella, the axoneme typically has a "9+2" arrangement: nine outer doublet microtubules surround two central single microtubules (the central pair). The outer doublets are connected by nexin links and bear rows of dynein arms — motor proteins whose ATP-driven sliding against adjacent doublets, constrained by the links, bends the axoneme and produces the whip-like beating motion. Radial spokes project from the outer doublets toward the central pair and help regulate dynein activity.

Primary (non-motile) cilia generally lack the central pair, having a "9+0" arrangement, and function mainly as sensory antennae.

Clinical significance

Defects in axonemal proteins cause diseases called ciliopathies. Primary ciliary dyskinesia results from defective dynein arms, leading to chronic respiratory infections and infertility; in about half of cases it is associated with situs inversus, as in Kartagener syndrome, because embryonic nodal cilia fail to establish normal left-right asymmetry.
