Caesium-137

Symbol: 137Cs

Half-life: 30.17 years

Decay mode: Beta emission to barium-137m, then gamma emission (662 keV)

Origin: Nuclear fission product

Caesium-137 (137Cs) is a radioactive isotope of caesium formed as one of the more common fission products of uranium-235 and other fissile isotopes in nuclear reactors and nuclear weapons. With a half-life of about 30 years, it is, together with strontium-90, one of the principal medium-lived fission products responsible for much of the radioactivity of spent nuclear fuel after the first few years of cooling.

Decay and uses

Caesium-137 decays by beta emission, mostly to the metastable isomer barium-137m, which in turn emits a characteristic gamma ray of 662 keV. This penetrating gamma emission makes 137Cs useful as a calibration and industrial radiation source: it is used in moisture and density gauges, flow meters, well logging, medical teletherapy (historically) and food irradiation, as well as in irradiators for blood products. Its ready detectability also makes it a standard marker by which fallout contamination is measured and dated in sediments.

Environmental releases

Atmospheric nuclear weapons testing dispersed caesium-137 worldwide, peaking in the early 1960s. The Chernobyl disaster (1986) and, to a lesser extent, the Fukushima accident (2011) released large quantities; 137Cs deposition defined exclusion zones and remains a long-term land-use constraint because the isotope binds to soils and enters food chains, notably mushrooms, game and freshwater fish. Caesium behaves chemically like potassium and is taken up by living tissue, making internal contamination a health concern addressed by measures such as Prussian blue therapy.
