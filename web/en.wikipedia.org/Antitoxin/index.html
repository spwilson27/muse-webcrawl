Antitoxin

An antitoxin is an antibody with the ability to neutralize a specific toxin. Antitoxins are produced by certain animals, plants, and bacteria in response to toxin exposure. Although they are most effective in neutralizing toxins, they can also kill bacteria and other microorganisms. Antitoxins are made within organisms and can be injected into other organisms, including humans, to treat infectious diseases.

History

In 1890, Emil von Behring and Kitasato Shibasaburō demonstrated that serum from animals immunized against diphtheria and tetanus toxins could cure infected animals and confer passive immunity. Diphtheria antitoxin, produced in horses, became the first effective therapy for the disease and earned Behring the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901.

Use

Therapeutic antitoxins remain important for diphtheria, tetanus, and botulism. Because most are derived from horse serum, recipients are tested for hypersensitivity, and serum sickness is a recognized adverse effect. Antivenoms for snake and arthropod envenomation are related products based on the same principle.
