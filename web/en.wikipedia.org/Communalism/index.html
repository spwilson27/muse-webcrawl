Communalism

Communalism is a term with several related meanings. In South Asian usage, it refers to hostility between religious or ethnic communities, particularly tensions between Hindus and Muslims in India, and to political movements organised around religious identity. In political philosophy, communalism is a theory developed by the American thinker Murray Bookchin, proposing a system of directly democratic, confederated municipalities as an alternative to both capitalism and the nation-state.

South Asian usage

In India, "communalism" describes the belief that religious communities have distinct and conflicting political and economic interests, and the mobilisation of people along religious lines. Historians link its development to colonial policies such as separate electorates under British rule and to the politics that led to the Partition of India in 1947. Communal violence—episodic riots between communities—remains a recurring concern in Indian public life, and scholars such as Bipan Chandra analysed communalism as an ideology with stages from communal consciousness to communal violence.

Bookchin's communalism

Murray Bookchin developed communalism (earlier called libertarian municipalism) as the political philosophy of his social ecology. It proposes that citizens govern themselves through popular assemblies in municipalities, which confederate into larger networks to administer policy, while elected delegates remain mandated and recallable. Bookchin drew on the traditions of the ancient Athenian polis, the Paris Commune, and anarchist and Marxist thought, while distinguishing his position from individualist anarchism. His ideas influenced Abdullah Öcalan's theory of democratic confederalism, applied in parts of northern Syria.
