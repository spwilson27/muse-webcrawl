Victorian literature
Period: c. 1837–1901 (reign of Queen Victoria)
Dominant form: the novel, often serialised
Major figures: Dickens, the Brontës, George Eliot, Hardy, Tennyson

Victorian literature is the body of English writing produced during the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901). It is dominated by the novel, which — issued in monthly serial parts and three-volume editions — became the era's great popular and artistic form, addressing industrialisation, class, faith, empire and the position of women.

The novel

Charles Dickens set the pattern with works such as Oliver Twist, Bleak House and Great Expectations, mixing social criticism with vivid characterisation. Charlotte and Emily Brontë brought psychological intensity in Jane Eyre and Wuthering Heights; George Eliot's Middlemarch is often judged the greatest Victorian novel. William Makepeace Thackeray, Anthony Trollope, Elizabeth Gaskell and, at the century's end, Thomas Hardy — whose Tess of the d'Urbervilles and Jude the Obscure scandalised readers — round out the major prose tradition. Sensation fiction, detective fiction (Wilkie Collins, Arthur Conan Doyle) and children's literature (Lewis Carroll, Robert Louis Stevenson) also flourished.

Poetry and drama

Alfred, Lord Tennyson, Poet Laureate for much of the period, and Robert Browning, master of the dramatic monologue, led Victorian poetry, alongside Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Matthew Arnold and Gerard Manley Hopkins. The stage was dominated by melodrama and, late in the century, the comedies of Oscar Wilde.
