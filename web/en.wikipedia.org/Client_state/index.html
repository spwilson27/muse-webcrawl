Client state

A client state depends on a more powerful patron state for political, economic or military support

In international relations, a client state is a state that is economically, politically, and militarily subordinated to a more powerful patron state in international politics. A client state may variously be described as a satellite state, an associated state, a dominion, a self-governing colony, a neo-colony, a protectorate, a vassal state, or a tributary state, though these terms differ in their precise implications.

Characteristics

A client state retains nominal sovereignty but in practice its foreign policy, and often aspects of its domestic policy, are aligned with or directed by the patron. In exchange, the client receives benefits such as economic aid, military protection, weapons, or diplomatic backing. The relationship is unequal: the patron is the dominant partner, and the client's leadership typically depends on the patron's support to remain in power.

Client states are usually contrasted with fully independent allies on the one hand, and with directly administered territories or puppet states on the other. A puppet state is generally understood to be a regime installed and controlled by a foreign power with little or no indigenous legitimacy, whereas a client state may retain a substantial degree of internal autonomy and its own political structures.

History

The concept descends from the client kingdoms of the ancient world. The Roman Republic and Empire maintained numerous client kingdoms on their frontiers, ruled by local kings who acknowledged Roman supremacy and supplied troops in return for protection. Similar arrangements appeared in imperial China, where surrounding states paid tribute to the emperor.

During the Cold War, both the United States and the Soviet Union maintained networks of client states: the USSR's Eastern European allies and Cuba, for example, were often described as Soviet clients, while South Vietnam, and at various times states such as South Korea, the Philippines and several Latin American regimes, were described as American clients. In the post-Cold War era the term has been applied to relationships such as that between Belarus and Russia, or between North Korea and China.

See also

Related concepts include the satellite state, the puppet state, the protectorate, the vassal state, and the tributary state. The patron's control may be exercised through economic leverage, as in relationships centred on minerals such as cobalt and other strategic resources.
