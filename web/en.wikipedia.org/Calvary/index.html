Calvary

Calvary (Latin: Calvariae Locus) or Golgotha (Greek: Γολγοθᾶ, Golgothâ) was a site within the walls of Jerusalem where, according to the canonical Gospels, Jesus was crucified.

Name

The Gospels describe the place as "the place of a skull": the Aramaic Golgotha and its Greek rendering Kranion were translated into Latin as Calvaria. Explanations for the name include that the hill resembled a skull, that it was a place of executions where skulls were left, or a tradition that the skull of Adam was buried there.

Location

Since at least the 4th century, tradition has located Calvary at the site of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in the Christian Quarter of the Old City of Jerusalem, built under Constantine the Great after his mother Helena's pilgrimage. The location lay outside the city wall of Jesus' time, as the New Testament describes, near a garden with tombs. A rock outcrop within the church is venerated as the site of the Crucifixion, and the church also encloses the traditional site of Jesus's tomb. An alternative location proposed in the 19th century is the "Garden Tomb" near a skull-like escarpment north of the Damascus Gate, favoured by some Protestants.

Use of the term

By extension, a "calvary" is an outdoor sculptural representation of the Crucifixion, common in Catholic regions of Europe such as Brittany and Belgium, often forming part of a parish close or roadside shrine, and the name of numerous chapels, hills, and cemeteries.
