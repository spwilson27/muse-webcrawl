Grand Teton National Park is a national park in northwestern Wyoming, immediately south of Yellowstone National Park. It preserves the Teton Range — a dramatic, glacier-carved fault-block mountain front rising nearly 7,000 feet above the valley of Jackson Hole with no foothills — together with the Snake River, piedmont lakes such as Jenny and Jackson Lakes, and rich wildlife habitat.

History

The park was established in 1929 to protect the peaks and lakes; the valley floor was added in 1950 after John D. Rockefeller Jr. secretly bought up ranch land through the Snake River Land Company and donated some 35,000 acres, overcoming fierce local opposition. The John D. Rockefeller Jr. Memorial Parkway links the park to Yellowstone.

Geology and ecology

The Tetons are among the youngest mountain ranges in North America, uplifted along the Teton fault beginning about nine million years ago, while some of their granite and gneiss rocks are among the oldest on the continent. The park supports grizzly bears, moose, elk, bison, pronghorn, and trumpeter swans, and is a world-renowned destination for mountaineering — the Grand Teton was first climbed in 1898 (or possibly 1872) — hiking, and wildlife watching, drawing several million visitors a year.
