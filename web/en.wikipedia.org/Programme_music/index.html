Programme music

Programme music or program music is a type of instrumental art music that attempts to depict in musical form an extra-musical narrative — a story, poem, landscape, painting, or idea — so that the listener can follow the programme with the aid of a written outline or title. The term was popularised in the 19th century, notably by Franz Liszt, who coined the term symphonic poem for single-movement orchestral works on programmatic subjects. Programme music stands in opposition to absolute music, which is intended to be appreciated without reference to anything outside itself.

History

Imitative and descriptive music is ancient, but programmatic composition flourished in the Romantic era. Beethoven's Pastoral Symphony (1808), with its bird calls and storm, and Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique (1830), which narrates an artist's opium dream with the aid of a printed programme, are landmark works. Liszt's symphonic poems, Richard Strauss's tone poems such as Don Juan, Till Eulenspiegel, and Also sprach Zarathustra, and works by Smetana (Má vlast), Mussorgsky (Pictures at an Exhibition), and Saint-Saëns extended the genre. Beethoven himself insisted his Pastoral was "more expression of feeling than painting," and the balance between depiction and pure musical structure was debated throughout the century.

Debate

Critics led by Eduard Hanslick championed absolute music and argued that music's aesthetic value lies in its form, not in representing external content. The so-called "War of the Romantics" opposed the programmatic New German School of Liszt and Wagner to the more conservative followers of Brahms and Hanslick. In the 20th century the sharp distinction faded, though programmatic thinking survives prominently in film scores, where music is composed to accompany narrative images.

Examples

Famous programme works include Vivaldi's The Four Seasons (with sonnets), Beethoven's Pastoral Symphony, Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique, Liszt's Les préludes, Rimsky-Korsakov's Scheherazade, Strauss's Ein Heldenleben, Debussy's La mer, and Respighi's Pines of Rome.
