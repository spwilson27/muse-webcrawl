Verb

A verb is a word that, in syntax, generally conveys an action (bring, read, walk, run, learn), an occurrence (happen, become), or a state of being (be, exist, stand). In the usual description of English, the basic form of a verb, with or without the particle to, is the infinitive. Verbs are often the nucleus of a predicate, agreeing with the subject in person and number in many languages.

Types

Verbs vary by valency — the number of arguments they take. Intransitive verbs take one argument (the subject), transitive verbs take two (subject and object), and ditransitive verbs take three. Other classifications distinguish action verbs from stative verbs, main verbs from auxiliaries, and regular from irregular verbs. Copular verbs (such as be, seem) link the subject to a complement rather than expressing action.

Inflection

In many languages verbs are inflected (modified in form) to encode tense, aspect, mood, voice, and agreement with person, number and sometimes gender of their arguments. English retains comparatively little verb inflection: third-person singular present -s, past tense forms, and participles are the main remnants. Languages such as Spanish, Russian and Arabic have far richer conjugation systems.

Agreement and auxiliaries

Auxiliary verbs (English be, have, do and the modals) combine with main verbs to express perfect and progressive aspect, passive voice, questions, negation and modality. See the general article on acoustics for an unrelated field that also studies how language is produced and perceived.
