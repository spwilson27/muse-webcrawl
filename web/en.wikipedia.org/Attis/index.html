Attis

Attis (Ancient Greek: Ἄττις or Ἄττης) was a Phrygian deity of vegetation, the consort of the great mother goddess Cybele. In his self-mutilation, death and (in later tradition) rebirth he personified the fruits of the earth, which die in winter and rise again in spring. His myth and cult spread from Anatolia through the Greek world and became prominent in the Roman Empire, where the festival of Attis formed part of the March celebrations of the Magna Mater.

Myth

Versions of the myth vary, notably in Ovid's Fasti, Pausanias, and the account preserved by Arnobius (drawing on Timotheus). In the most widespread telling, the beautiful youth Attis, beloved of Cybele, was driven mad by the goddess (in jealousy over his intended marriage, or, in the Anatolian version, by the hermaphroditic monster Agdistis) and castrated himself beneath a pine tree, dying of his wound; violets sprang from his blood. In another version he was killed by a boar, like Adonis. Zeus, moved by Cybele's grief, granted that Attis's body should not decay — his hair would keep growing and his little finger would keep moving. Later Roman-era tradition developed the motif into a fuller resurrection celebrated at the spring festival.

Cult

Attis's priests, the galli, imitated their god through ritual self-castration, performed in ecstatic frenzy on the "Day of Blood" (Dies Sanguinis, 24 March) during the March festival cycle of Cybele in Rome: the cycle ran from the entry of the reed (15 March) through the cutting of the pine tree sacred to Attis (22 March), the Day of Blood, and the day of rejoicing, the Hilaria (25 March), to ritual washing of the goddess's image in the Almo. Attis is typically depicted in art wearing a Phrygian cap and trousers, often in the mournful pose of the "capitoline" type statue, leaning on a shepherd's crook. The emperor Claudius is credited with organising the Roman festival in its developed form, and the cult remained important into late antiquity, its death-and-renewal symbolism inviting comparison — already by ancient Christian writers such as Firmicus Maternus, who attacked it — with pagan mystery religions and Easter.
