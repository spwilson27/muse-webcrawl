Nemi ships

The Nemi ships were two enormous Roman vessels built in the 1st century AD on Lake Nemi, a small volcanic lake in the Alban Hills south of Rome. Commissioned by the emperor Caligula (reigned 37–41 AD), they were among the largest ships of the ancient world: the larger of the two measured roughly 73 metres (240 ft) long and 24 metres (79 ft) in the beam. They served as floating palaces or as temple barges connected with the nearby sanctuary of Diana at Nemi.

Construction and features

The ships were built with sophisticated technology, including a hull sheathed in lead over tarred wool, held by copper nails, and fitted with advanced plumbing in lead pipes stamped with Caligula's name. The superstructures carried marble temples and palatial rooms with mosaic floors, heating, and flowing water. Remarkably, the larger ship appears to have carried rotating platforms mounted on bronze spheres — an early form of ball bearing — possibly supporting statues or cranes.

Loss and recovery

After Caligula's assassination in 41 AD, the ships were stripped and sank, or were deliberately sunk, in the lake. Local fishermen knew of the wrecks for centuries and recovered small artefacts; Renaissance figures including Leon Battista Alberti attempted salvage in the 15th century. The ships were finally recovered between 1928 and 1932 under the Fascist government of Benito Mussolini, which drained much of the lake using ancient Roman emissary tunnels and pumps. The hulls were housed in a purpose-built museum on the lakeshore.

Destruction

On the night of 31 May – 1 June 1944, during the Second World War, the museum and both ships were destroyed by fire, amid disputed circumstances involving retreating German forces and Allied artillery fire. Only smaller artefacts, bronze fittings, and models survived; a modern museum on the site displays reconstructions and surviving pieces.
