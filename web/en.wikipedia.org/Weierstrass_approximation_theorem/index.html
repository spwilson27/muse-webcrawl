Weierstrass approximation theorem

In mathematical analysis, the Weierstrass approximation theorem states that every continuous function defined on a closed interval [a, b] can be uniformly approximated as closely as desired by a polynomial function. That is, for every continuous f and every ε > 0, there exists a polynomial p such that |f(x) − p(x)| < ε for all x in [a, b].

History and proofs

Karl Weierstrass published the theorem in 1885. A famous constructive proof was given by Sergei Bernstein in 1912, using the Bernstein polynomials as an approximation device; the argument relies on probabilistic reasoning about the binomial distribution. Marshall Stone generalized the result in 1937 to the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, which characterizes dense subalgebras of the continuous functions on a compact Hausdorff space.

Significance

The theorem shows that polynomials are dense in the space of continuous functions under the uniform norm, a foundational result for approximation theory. Although real-analytic results guarantee uniform convergence, the approximating polynomials furnished by the theorem need not converge in derivative, and Weierstrass himself exhibited a continuous function that is nowhere differentiable.
