Hercules

Roman name of: Heracles
Parents: Zeus (Jupiter) and Alcmene
Symbols: Club, lion skin, bow

Hercules is the Roman name for the Greek divine hero Heracles (Greek: Ἡρακλῆς), son of Zeus (Roman Jupiter) and the mortal Alcmene. In classical mythology he is famous for his extraordinary strength and for performing the Twelve Labours imposed on him by King Eurystheus. The Romans adopted him as a god of victory and a protector of travellers and athletes, and his cult was among the most widespread in the ancient world.

The Twelve Labours

Driven mad by Hera, Heracles killed his wife Megara and their children. To atone, the Delphic oracle ordered him to serve Eurystheus for twelve years and perform the tasks set for him: slaying the Nemean Lion and the Lernaean Hydra; capturing the Ceryneian Hind and the Erymanthian Boar; cleaning the Augean stables in a day; driving off the Stymphalian birds; fetching the Cretan Bull and the mares of Diomedes; taking the girdle of Hippolyta; seizing the cattle of Geryon; stealing the apples of the Hesperides; and bringing Cerberus up from the underworld.

Death and apotheosis

Heracles died from a shirt poisoned (unknowingly) with the blood of the centaur Nessus, given to him by his wife Deianira. Rather than die slowly, he built his own funeral pyre on Mount Oeta; as it burned, he was taken up to Olympus and granted immortality, reconciling with Hera and marrying her daughter Hebe.

Roman cult and legacy

In Rome, Hercules had an altar in the Forum Boarium (the Ara Maxima) and was invoked by generals such as Sulla and Mark Antony, who claimed descent from him. The pillars at the Strait of Gibraltar were called the Pillars of Hercules. He remains a ubiquitous figure in Western art, literature, and place names.
