The Transatlantic Trade and Investment Partnership (TTIP) was a proposed trade agreement between the European Union and the United States, intended to promote trade and multilateral economic growth. Negotiations were launched in 2013 and were halted at the end of 2016, after fifteen rounds. In April 2019, the Council of the European Union declared the 2013 negotiating directives obsolete.

Had it been concluded, TTIP would have created the world's largest free-trade area, covering economies accounting for roughly a third of global GDP. The agreement aimed to reduce tariffs, align regulations and standards, and liberalise trade in services and public procurement.

Contents of the proposal

The negotiations were organised around three pillars: market access (tariffs, services, procurement), regulatory cooperation (technical barriers to trade and sectoral rules such as automobiles, pharmaceuticals, and chemicals), and rules (sustainable development, energy, customs, intellectual property, and small and medium-sized enterprises).

Controversy

TTIP provoked one of the largest trade-related protest movements in European history. Critics focused on the proposed investor–state dispute settlement (ISDS) mechanism, which they argued would allow corporations to challenge public-interest regulation; on fears of lowered food safety, environmental, and labour standards; and on the secrecy of the negotiations. A European Citizens' Initiative against TTIP gathered more than three million signatures, and large demonstrations took place in Germany and elsewhere. Supporters argued the deal would boost growth and jobs and set global standards.

End of negotiations

Progress stalled in 2016 amid slowing talks and public opposition, and the election of Donald Trump as US president ended the process; the incoming administration prioritised other trade actions. The EU and US instead pursued narrower agreements under the later Trade and Technology Council framework.
