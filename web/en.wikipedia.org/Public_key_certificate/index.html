Public key certificate

In cryptography, a public key certificate, also known as a digital certificate or identity certificate, is an electronic document used to prove the validity of a public key. The certificate includes the public key, information about the identity of its owner (called the subject), and the digital signature of an entity that has verified the certificate's contents (called the issuer). If the issuer is trusted and the signature is valid, a verifier can be confident that the public key belongs to the named subject. The most common format for public key certificates is defined by the X.509 standard.

Role in TLS

Certificates are central to Transport Layer Security (TLS), the protocol behind HTTPS. When a browser connects to a website, the server presents a certificate (usually a chain of certificates leading to a root certificate authority pre-installed in the browser or operating system). The browser verifies the signatures, checks that the certificate is unexpired, has not been revoked, and matches the hostname requested, then uses the public key — or keys exchanged via signed parameters — to establish an encrypted session.

Certificate authorities

Certificates are issued by certificate authorities (CAs), which validate the applicant's control of a domain (domain-validated certificates) or legal identity (organisation- and extended-validation certificates). The system depends on the trustworthiness of hundreds of root CAs; mis-issuance incidents have led to tighter auditing, Certificate Transparency logs that publicly record issued certificates, and the rise of automated free issuance through the ACME protocol, notably by Let's Encrypt.

Revocation

If a private key is compromised or a certificate is issued in error, the CA publishes its serial number on a certificate revocation list (CRL) or answers status queries via OCSP. Modern browsers increasingly rely on short-lived certificates and compressed revocation mechanisms because traditional revocation checking proved slow and unreliable.
