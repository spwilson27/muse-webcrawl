Right to repair

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The right to repair is a legal right for owners of devices and equipment to freely modify and repair products such as automobiles, electronics, and farm equipment. It refers to the concept that end users, business users, and repair businesses should be able to fix the products they own, or have access to the services of others to do so, without being constrained by manufacturers' technical, legal, or contractual restrictions.

The movement behind the concept seeks laws requiring manufacturers to provide spare parts, tools, manuals, diagnostics, and firmware, and to design products that can be disassembled and repaired rather than discarded.

History

Independent repair was long the norm for automobiles and appliances, but increasingly software-controlled products — from tractors to smartphones — became repairable only by authorised networks. A 2012 Massachusetts ballot measure opened access to vehicle diagnostic data and became a national template. In the 2020s, several U.S. states including New York, Minnesota, Colorado, and California enacted digital fair repair legislation, while the European Union adopted ecodesign rules requiring spare-part availability and, later, a common repair framework. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission's 2021 "Nixing the Fix" report found little evidence for manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions.

Arguments

Supporters cite consumer savings, reduced electronic waste, resilience for farmers and hospitals, and competition for independent shops. Opponents, mainly manufacturers, raise concerns about safety, cybersecurity, intellectual property, and product liability.
