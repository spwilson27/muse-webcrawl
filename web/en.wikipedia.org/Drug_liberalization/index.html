Drug liberalization

Drug liberalization is a drug policy approach that seeks to reduce or eliminate the criminal prohibition of drugs. It spans a spectrum of reforms, from decriminalization — removing criminal penalties for possession of small amounts while keeping supply illegal — to full legalization, in which production and sale are permitted under regulation.

Arguments

Supporters argue that prohibition creates profitable black markets, enriches organized crime, produces violence, and imposes heavy costs of incarceration, while failing to eliminate drug use. Liberalization is presented as a way to bring production under quality control, tax the trade, and treat addiction as a public-health rather than criminal matter. Opponents argue that easier availability would increase consumption and associated harms, and that regulation of substances such as opioids or methamphetamine is impracticable.

Examples

Portugal decriminalized possession of all drugs in 2001, referring users to dissuasion commissions and treatment; the reform is widely studied in debates on drug policy. Uruguay (2013), Canada (2018) and numerous US states have legalized recreational cannabis under regulated markets, while the Netherlands has long tolerated retail cannabis sale under its policy of gedoogbeleid. Several jurisdictions have also decriminalized or created medical exemptions for psychedelic substances.

International context

Liberalization sits in tension with the international drug control treaties, particularly the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs of 1961, which oblige signatories to limit drug use to medical and scientific purposes.
