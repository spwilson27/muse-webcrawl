In mathematics, an ellipse is a plane curve surrounding two focal points, such that for all points on the curve, the sum of the two distances to the focal points is a constant. It generalizes a circle, which is the special type of ellipse in which the two focal points are the same. The elongation of an ellipse is measured by its eccentricity, a number ranging from 0 (a circle) to values approaching 1.

Ellipses are one of the types of conic section, formed by the intersection of a cone with a plane. They arise in many physical contexts: most famously, Johannes Kepler discovered that the orbit of each planet around the Sun is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus, a result stated in his first law of planetary motion.

Definitions and properties

An ellipse may be defined as the locus of points whose distances to two fixed points (the foci) sum to a constant 2a, where a is the semi-major axis. The line segment through the foci is the major axis; perpendicular to it, through the centre, is the minor axis with semi-axis b. The area enclosed by the ellipse is πab.

Equations

In Cartesian coordinates with the centre at the origin and axes aligned with the coordinate axes, the equation is x²/a² + y²/b² = 1. In parametric form, x = a cos t and y = b sin t. Unlike the perimeter of a circle, the circumference of an ellipse has no elementary closed form and is given by an elliptic integral.

Applications

Beyond planetary orbits, ellipses appear in optics (a ray from one focus reflects to the other, a property used in whispering galleries and lithotripsy), in engineering (elliptical arches and gears), and in statistics (confidence ellipses).
