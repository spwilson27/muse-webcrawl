Mexican-American War


The war ended with Mexico ceding its northern territories to the United States.

LocationTexas, northern and central Mexico, California

The Mexican-American War, also known in the United States as the Mexican War and in Mexico as the Intervención estadounidense en México (United States intervention in Mexico), was an invasion of Mexico by the United States Army from 1846 to 1848. It followed the 1845 American annexation of Texas, which Mexico still considered its territory.

Background

Texas had won de facto independence from Mexico in 1836, but Mexico never recognised the Republic of Texas and disputed its southern boundary: Texas claimed the Rio Grande, Mexico the Nueces River further north. President James K. Polk, an expansionist committed to Manifest Destiny, sought not only Texas but also California and New Mexico. When Mexico rejected the Slidell mission to negotiate a purchase, Polk ordered General Zachary Taylor's troops into the disputed strip between the rivers, where they clashed with Mexican forces in the Thornton Affair of April 1846. Congress declared war on 13 May 1846.

Course of the war

American forces won a rapid series of victories. Taylor defeated Mexican armies at Palo Alto, Resaca de la Palma, Monterrey, and — heavily outnumbered — Buena Vista in February 1847. In the west, Stephen Kearny's Army of the West took Santa Fe and moved into California, where, with John C. Frémont's California Battalion and naval forces, the United States secured the province after the Bear Flag Revolt. General Winfield Scott then landed at Veracruz in March 1847 and fought his way inland through Cerro Gordo, Contreras, Churubusco, and Molino del Rey to storm Chapultepec and occupy Mexico City in September 1847. The defence of Chapultepec by young cadets — the Niños Héroes — became a lasting symbol in Mexico.

Treaty and consequences

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed on 2 February 1848, ended the war. Mexico recognised the Rio Grande as the Texas boundary and ceded the vast Mexican Cession — California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming — in return for $15 million and the assumption of certain claims. Mexico lost roughly half its pre-war territory. In the United States, the new lands reignited the slavery-extension controversy, contributing to the Compromise of 1850 and, ultimately, the Civil War. Many later-famous officers — including Ulysses S. Grant, who later judged the war unjust, and Robert E. Lee — gained experience in the conflict.
