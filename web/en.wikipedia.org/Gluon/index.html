Gluon

Gluons (from glue) are elementary particles that mediate the strong interaction between quarks, acting as the exchange particles of the strong nuclear force. They are the gauge bosons of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), analogous to photons in electromagnetism, but with a crucial difference: gluons themselves carry the "colour charge" of the strong force, so gluons interact with each other as well as with quarks.

Properties

There are eight independent types of gluon, corresponding to combinations of the three colour charges (red, green, blue) and their anticolours. Because gluons carry colour, the strong force between quarks does not weaken with distance in the way electromagnetism does; instead the force stays roughly constant, which leads to colour confinement — quarks and gluons are never observed in isolation, only bound inside colour-neutral hadrons such as protons and neutrons. At very short distances, conversely, quarks behave almost as free particles, a property called asymptotic freedom, recognised by the 2004 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Discovery

Gluons were first directly observed in 1979 at the PETRA electron–positron collider at DESY in Hamburg, in events where a quark-antiquark pair radiated a gluon, producing three jets of hadrons. Exotic bound states of gluons, called glueballs, are predicted by QCD but have not been conclusively identified.
