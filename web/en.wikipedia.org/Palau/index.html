Palau, officially the Republic of Palau, is an island country in the western Pacific Ocean, part of the Micronesia region of Oceania. The archipelago of more than 340 coral and volcanic islands — of which only a handful are inhabited — lies southeast of the Philippines and north of Indonesia. Palau is famed for its marine environment: the mushroom-shaped Rock Islands, Jellyfish Lake, and some of the world's richest coral reefs make it a premier diving destination and a global leader in ocean conservation.

History

Palau was settled some 3,000 years ago by seafarers from Island Southeast Asia, who developed a matrilineal chiefdom society noted for its stone money platforms, meeting houses (bai) and terraced hills. Administered by Spain from the 19th century, the islands were sold to Germany in 1899, seized by Japan in 1914, and mandated to Japan after the First World War. The Second World War brought the savage Battle of Peleliu (1944). Thereafter Palau became part of the US-administered Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands. Voters repeatedly rejected inclusion in the Federated States of Micronesia, largely over Palau's constitutionally mandated nuclear-free status, before approving a Compact of Free Association with the United States, under which Palau became independent in 1994 while the US retains defence responsibility.

Geography and environment

The largest island, Babeldaob, hosts the capital, Ngerulmud (one of the world's least populous capitals), while most Palauans live in Koror. In 2009 Palau created the world's first shark sanctuary, and in 2015 it designated the Palau National Marine Sanctuary, covering some 500,000 km² — among the largest fully protected marine areas on Earth. Palau was also the first country to require visitors to sign an eco-pledge (the "Palau Pledge", 2017) stamped into their passports.

Economy and culture

Tourism (especially diving and snorkelling), subsistence agriculture, fishing and US assistance underpin the economy. Palauan society is traditionally matrilineal, with clan identity, land and titles passing through women; the Palauan language, unusual among Austronesian languages of the region in its structure, is spoken by the majority. Climate change and sea-level rise are existential concerns, and Palau has been a prominent voice for small island states in international climate diplomacy.
