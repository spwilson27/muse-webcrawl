John Langshaw Austin (26 March 1911 – 8 February 1960), usually cited as J. L. Austin, was a British philosopher of language and a leading proponent of ordinary language philosophy, best known for developing the theory of speech acts. His posthumously published book How to Do Things with Words (1962), based on lectures he gave at Harvard University in 1955, is one of the most influential works in twentieth-century philosophy of language.

Life and career

Austin was born in Lancaster and educated at Shrewsbury School and Balliol College, Oxford, where he read Literae Humaniores (classics and philosophy). He became a fellow of All Souls College and later of Magdalen College, Oxford, and in 1952 was appointed White's Professor of Moral Philosophy at Oxford. During the Second World War he served in British military intelligence, working on the intelligence preparations for the Normandy landings. He died of lung cancer in 1960, aged only 48.

Philosophy

Austin argued that philosophers should attend closely to the distinctions drawn in ordinary language, on the ground that everyday speech embodies the inherited experience and resourcefulness of many generations. His famous Saturday morning discussion group at Oxford, attended by figures such as P. F. Strawson and H. P. Grice, was central to the development of ordinary language philosophy.

Speech acts

Austin distinguished performative utterances — sentences that do something rather than merely describe, such as "I promise" or "I name this ship" — from constative statements that are true or false. He went on to generalise the insight into a threefold distinction between the locutionary act (saying something), the illocutionary act (what one does in saying it, such as promising or warning), and the perlocutionary act (what one brings about by saying it, such as persuading). His student and later critics, notably John Searle, developed this into modern speech act theory.

Other works

His papers, collected as Philosophical Papers (1961), include "A Plea for Excuses" and "Ifs and Cans". Sense and Sensibilia (1962), reconstructed from his lecture notes, attacks A. J. Ayer's sense-data theory of perception. Austin also translated Gottlob Frege's The Foundations of Arithmetic into English.
