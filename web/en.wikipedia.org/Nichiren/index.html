Nichiren (日蓮; 1222–1282) was a Japanese Buddhist priest of the Kamakura period who founded Nichiren Buddhism, a major branch of Japanese Mahayana Buddhism. He taught that the Lotus Sutra alone contains the complete truth of Shakyamuni Buddha's enlightenment, and that chanting its title — Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō (the daimoku) — enables all people to attain Buddhahood in this lifetime.

Life

Born the son of a fisherman in Awa Province, Nichiren was ordained at Seichō-ji temple and studied at Mount Hiei, the centre of Tendai Buddhism. In 1253 he publicly proclaimed his teaching and adopted the name Nichiren ("Sun-Lotus"). His strident denunciation of rival schools — Pure Land, Zen, Shingon and Ritsu — and of government reliance on them brought repeated persecution. In the Risshō Ankoku Ron (1260) he blamed Japan's disasters on false teachings. He narrowly escaped execution at Tatsunokuchi in 1271 and was exiled to Sado Island, where he inscribed the first Gohonzon mandala; after a pardon he retired to Mount Minobu, where he taught until his death in 1282.

Teaching and legacy

Nichiren's doctrine centres on the daimoku, the Gohonzon (a calligraphic mandala embodying the Lotus Sutra), and doctrines inherited from Chinese Tiantai such as ichinen sanzen ("three thousand realms in a single moment of life"). After his death his six senior disciples founded distinct lineages. Modern Nichiren movements include Nichiren Shū, Nichiren Shōshū, and the lay organization Soka Gakkai, one of Japan's largest religious movements.
