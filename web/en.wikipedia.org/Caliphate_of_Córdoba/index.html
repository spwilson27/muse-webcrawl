The Caliphate of Córdoba was an Islamic state in the Iberian Peninsula ruled by the Umayyad dynasty from 929 to 1031. Its capital, Córdoba, became one of the largest and most advanced cities in the world, a leading centre of science, medicine, philosophy and the arts.

From emirate to caliphate

The Umayyad presence in al-Andalus began in 756, when Abd al-Rahman I, a survivor of the Abbasid overthrow of the Umayyad caliphate in Damascus, established an independent emirate. In 929 his descendant Abd al-Rahman III assumed the title of caliph, partly in response to the rival Fatimid caliphate in North Africa. Under him and his son al-Hakam II, the state reached its greatest power and cultural brilliance; al-Hakam's library in Córdoba was said to hold some 400,000 volumes.

Decline and collapse

After 976 real power passed to the chamberlain Almanzor (al-Mansur), whose military campaigns kept the caliphate dominant but hollowed out caliphal authority. Following his death in 1002, succession struggles plunged al-Andalus into civil war (the Fitna of al-Andalus). Córdoba itself was sacked, and in 1031 the caliphate was abolished, fragmenting into the smaller taifa kingdoms.

Culture

The caliphate's court patronised scholars such as the surgeon al-Zahrawi (Albucasis) and maintained diplomatic and cultural exchange with Byzantium and the Christian kingdoms. Its most famous monument is the Great Mosque of Córdoba, expanded repeatedly under the emirs and caliphs.
