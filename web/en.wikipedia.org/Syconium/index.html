Syconium

Syconium
Type: Multiple fruit / inflorescence
Genus: Ficus
Pollinators: Fig wasps
Opening: Ostiole

A syconium is the specialized inflorescence and fruit structure characteristic of figs (genus Ficus). It consists of a hollow, fleshy receptacle whose inner surface is lined with hundreds of tiny flowers, accessible from outside only through a small opening called the ostiole. What is commonly called a fig "fruit" is therefore a multiple fruit containing many small true fruits (achenes).

Structure

The syconium develops from an inverted flower head: the receptacle folds inward, enclosing male, female, and sterile gall flowers inside a flask-shaped cavity. As it ripens, the walls thicken and become sweet and fleshy, forming the edible fig of species such as Ficus carica.

Pollination

Figs are pollinated by minute fig wasps that enter through the ostiole in an obligate mutualism evolved over tens of millions of years; most fig species depend on a single specific wasp species. The wasps lay eggs in gall flowers while pollinating the female flowers, and their offspring mate inside the syconium before the winged females disperse.
