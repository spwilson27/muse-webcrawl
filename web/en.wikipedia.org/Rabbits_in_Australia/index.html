Rabbits in Australia

The European rabbit is one of Australia's most destructive introduced species.

European rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus) were introduced to Australia in the 18th century with the First Fleet, and were first released into the wild in 1859 in southern Victoria. They are one of the most destructive invasive species in the country, causing millions of dollars of damage to crops and rangelands, ring-barking trees and shrubs, and contributing to soil erosion and the decline of native species through competition and habitat destruction.

Introduction and spread

Domestic rabbits arrived with the First Fleet in 1788 and were kept in captivity for decades. The explosion began in 1859, when settler Thomas Austin released 24 wild rabbits on his property near Geelong, Victoria, so he could hunt them. In the absence of natural predators and in a favourable climate where rabbits could breed year-round, numbers grew explosively: within decades rabbits had spread across the continent at one of the fastest rates recorded for any mammal, reaching an estimated 10 billion animals by the 1920s.

Control efforts

Colonial and state governments responded with bounties, trapping, poisoning, warren destruction and enormous fences, most famously the rabbit-proof fences of Western Australia, which failed to halt the spread. The great turning point was biological control: the release of the myxoma virus in 1950 caused the disease myxomatosis, which initially killed more than 99% of infected rabbits and cut the population from about 600 million to 100 million. Rabbits and virus then co-evolved toward resistance and attenuation. Rabbit haemorrhagic disease virus (calicivirus) escaped from a trial on Wardang Island in 1995 and spread across the mainland with similar initial effect; a Korean strain, RHDV1 K5, was deliberately released in 2017. Feral rabbit numbers are now commonly estimated in the hundreds of millions, and control remains an ongoing land-management burden.

Ecological impact

Rabbits compete with native herbivores such as bilbies and wombats, prevent regeneration of native shrubs and trees by eating seedlings, and support elevated populations of introduced predators such as foxes and feral cats, which then prey more heavily on native fauna. Their burrowing and overgrazing accelerate erosion of fragile Australian soils.
