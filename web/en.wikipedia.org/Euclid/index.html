Euclid (/ˈjuːklɪd/; Greek: Εὐκλείδης – Eukleídēs, pronounced [eu̯kleːdɛːs]; fl. 300 BC), sometimes called Euclid of Alexandria, was a Greek mathematician active in Alexandria during the reign of Ptolemy I (323–283 BC). His Elements is one of the most influential works in the history of mathematics, serving as the main textbook for teaching geometry from its publication until the late 19th or early 20th century. In the Elements, Euclid deduced the theorems of what is now called Euclidean geometry from a small set of axioms, applying a rigorous deductive and axiomatic method for the first time.

Life

Little is known about Euclid's life; most of the information comes from Proclus and Pappus, writing centuries later. He likely studied at Plato's Academy in Athens and moved to Alexandria, which Ptolemy was building up as a centre of learning. Anecdotes depict him teaching Ptolemy with the remark that there is "no royal road to geometry" and reproaching students who asked what profit proofs would bring.

Works

The Elements comprises 13 books covering plane geometry, proportion, number theory (including the proof of the infinitude of primes), incommensurable magnitudes, and solid geometry (ending with the five Platonic solids). Other attributed works include Data, On Divisions of Figures, Phaenomena (on astronomy), and Optics. Euclid also gave the Euclidean algorithm for the greatest common divisor and the first known proof that there are infinitely many prime numbers.

Legacy

Euclid's name is attached to Euclidean space, Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometry, Euclid's lemma, and the Euclidean distance. His works were translated into Arabic and Latin, defining the framework of Western mathematics education for two thousand years.
