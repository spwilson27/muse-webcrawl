The European rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus) is a species of rabbit native to the Iberian Peninsula and southwestern France. It is the ancestor of all domestic rabbit breeds and has been widely introduced elsewhere — most notoriously to Australia, where it became a devastating invasive pest. Despite its abundance in introduced ranges, populations in its native range have declined sharply due to disease, and the species is classified as Endangered on the IUCN Red List within its native range.

Description and behaviour

European rabbits are grey-brown lagomorphs 34–50 cm long, with long ears tipped with black and a white underside to the tail visible as they run. They are highly social, living in groups in extensive burrow systems called warrens, and are most active at dawn and dusk. Females can produce several litters of 3–7 kits per year after a gestation of about 30 days; young are born blind and hairless in a fur-lined nest chamber.

Introduction and impact

Rabbits were spread by the Romans and Normans for food and fur across Europe. In Australia, rabbits released in Victoria in 1859 multiplied explosively, competing with livestock and native wildlife and causing severe erosion; control measures included the vast rabbit-proof fences, the deliberate release of the myxoma virus in 1950 (myxomatosis), and later rabbit haemorrhagic disease. In their native Iberia, by contrast, rabbits are a keystone prey species for predators such as the Iberian lynx and Spanish imperial eagle, so their decline threatens those species.

Domestication

Domestic rabbits were bred from this species, probably by monks in southern France in the early Middle Ages, and now number over 300 recognised breeds kept for meat, wool, fur, research, and as pets.
