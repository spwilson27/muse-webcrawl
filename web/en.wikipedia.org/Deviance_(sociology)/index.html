Deviance (sociology)

Deviance
FieldSociology, criminology
Key theoristsÉmile Durkheim, Robert K. Merton, Howard Becker, Erving Goffman
Key conceptsAnomie, strain, labelling, stigma, social control

In sociology, deviance describes an action, belief, or condition that violates the social norms of a society or group. Deviance can be as minor as dressing unusually or breaching etiquette, or as serious as crime; it is defined not by any intrinsic property of the act but by the reactions of audiences and by the norms of the particular time, place, and culture. The same behaviour may be deviant in one context and celebrated in another.

Norms and sanctions

Societies maintain norms through sanctions ranging from informal responses (frowns, gossip, ostracism) to formal ones (fines, imprisonment). Sociologists distinguish mores, folkways, laws, and taboos by the severity of the sanction attached to their violation.

Major theories

Functionalism: Émile Durkheim argued deviance is a normal and even necessary feature of society: it clarifies moral boundaries, unites groups in reaction, and can promote social change. Robert K. Merton's strain theory explained deviance as the product of a gap between culturally approved goals and the legitimate means available to achieve them, yielding adaptations such as innovation, ritualism, retreatism, and rebellion.

Labelling theory: Howard Becker (Outsiders, 1963) held that "deviance is not a quality of the act... but a consequence of the application by others of rules and sanctions" — the label itself can produce secondary deviance and a deviant career. Erving Goffman analysed the management of stigma.

Conflict and control theories: Conflict approaches stress that norms and their enforcement reflect power, so the powerful define the behaviour of the powerless as deviant. Social control theories, such as Travis Hirschi's social bond theory, ask instead why most people conform.

Types

Deviance may be formal (violating enacted law) or informal (violating unwritten norms), and behaviour once condemned may be normalised (and vice versa) as norms shift — as with tattooing, divorce, or the criminalisation and later legalisation of various drugs.
