Declaration of Arbroath

The Declaration of Arbroath is the name usually given to a letter, dated 6 April 1320 at Arbroath, sent by the barons and freeholders of Scotland to Pope John XXII. Written in Latin, it sought to confirm Scotland's status as an independent, sovereign state and defended its right to use military action when unjustly attacked.

Background

Scotland had been at war with England since 1296, when Edward I invaded. Robert the Bruce's victory at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314 secured much of the kingdom, but the Pope had excommunicated Bruce and his supporters, and England continued to press its claim of overlordship. The declaration was part of a diplomatic campaign to have the excommunication lifted and Scottish independence recognised.

Contents

The letter recounts the mythical origins of the Scots, praises Robert the Bruce as a deliverer of his people, and complains of English aggression. Its most famous passage declares that the Scots fight "not for glory, nor riches, nor honours, but for freedom — for that alone, which no honest man gives up but with life itself." It also contains a striking conditional loyalty: if Bruce were to submit Scotland to England, the nobles say they would drive him out and make another king.

Legacy

The only surviving copy is held by the National Archives of Scotland. The declaration has become an iconic text of Scottish national identity, and 6 April is marked as Tartan Day in North America. Some historians have suggested it influenced later declarations of independence.
