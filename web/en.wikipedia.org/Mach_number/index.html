In fluid dynamics, the Mach number (M or Ma), often only Mach, is a dimensionless quantity representing the ratio of flow velocity past a boundary to the local speed of sound. It is named after the Austrian physicist Ernst Mach. By definition, Mach 1 equals the speed of sound in the surrounding medium, which varies with temperature and pressure; at sea level in standard conditions this is about 343 metres per second (1,235 km/h).

A vapour cone forms around an aircraft in transonic flight near Mach 1.

Classification of regimes

Flow regimes are conventionally classified by Mach number: subsonic (below about Mach 0.8), transonic (roughly 0.8–1.2), supersonic (1.2–5), hypersonic (5–10), and high-hypersonic (above 10). The regimes differ qualitatively: in subsonic flow, pressure disturbances propagate upstream, while in supersonic flow they are confined within a Mach cone, and shock waves form around the body.

Calculation

The Mach number is computed as M = v/a, where v is the velocity of the flow (or of the object through the medium) and a is the speed of sound in that medium. In an ideal gas, a depends on the square root of absolute temperature, so a given true airspeed corresponds to a higher Mach number at high altitude, where the air is colder.
