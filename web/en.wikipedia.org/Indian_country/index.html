Indian country is a legal term in United States law describing lands within the jurisdiction of American Indian tribes. Under 18 U.S.C. § 1151, Indian country includes all land within the limits of any Indian reservation under the jurisdiction of the United States government, all dependent Indian communities, and all Indian allotments whose Indian titles have not been extinguished.

Historical usage

The term originally referred to the frontier lands west of the Appalachian Mountains reserved for Native American peoples under British and early American policy, notably the Royal Proclamation of 1763 and the Indian Intercourse Acts. As the frontier moved west, "Indian Territory" in present-day Oklahoma became the best-known remnant of this usage before Oklahoma statehood in 1907.

Modern legal significance

The designation determines criminal and civil jurisdiction. Generally, tribal and federal governments exercise criminal jurisdiction over Native Americans in Indian country, while states have jurisdiction over crimes by non-Indians against non-Indians. Major federal statutes such as the Major Crimes Act and the Indian Civil Rights Act operate within this framework. In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), the Supreme Court held that the Muscogee (Creek) Reservation in Oklahoma had never been disestablished and thus remained Indian country for purposes of federal criminal law.

Extent

Indian country comprises roughly 56 million acres of reservation land held in trust, along with allotments and dependent communities such as the New Mexico Pueblos. Alaska Native lands are mostly excluded from the definition following the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act, with the exception recognized in some contexts such as the Annette Islands Reserve.
