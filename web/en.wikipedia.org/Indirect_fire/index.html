Indirect fire

Indirect fire is aiming and firing a projectile without relying on a direct line of sight between the gun and its target, as in the case of direct fire. Aiming is performed by calculating azimuth and elevation angles, and may be corrected by a forward observer or by sensors. Indirect fire emerged with modern artillery in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, and became the dominant form of artillery fire in the First World War, when trench systems made direct-fire guns vulnerable and observation from balloons, aircraft and forward positions made unseen targets engageable.

Technique

The firing unit needs to know its own position precisely, the direction of a reference point, and the target's location, computed on a map or by survey. Sights such as the dial sight, panoramic telescope and later inertial and GPS-based systems allow the gun to be laid (aimed) on an aiming point rather than the target. Fire is adjusted by observers who report the fall of shot in terms of corrections ("add 200, left 50"), and fire-control centres compute firing data using range tables accounting for charge, projectile, wind, air temperature and the rotation of the Earth.

Weapons

Howitzers and mortars are designed principally for indirect fire, firing at high angles so shells clear intervening terrain; multiple launch rocket systems and naval gunfire also employ it extensively. Indirect fire allows batteries to remain concealed in defilade, mass fires from dispersed positions onto one target, and engage targets behind hills, in trenches or in urban areas that direct fire cannot reach.
