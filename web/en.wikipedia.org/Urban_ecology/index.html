Urban ecology

Urban ecology is the scientific study of the relation of living organisms to each other and their surroundings in the context of an urban environment. It examines how plants, animals, microorganisms, people and ecological processes interact within cities and their peri-urban fringes, and it draws on ecology, geography, sociology, urban planning and public health. The field emerged as a distinct area of research in the late twentieth century, shifting from an early view of cities as biological deserts to a recognition that urban areas are complex ecosystems in their own right.

Urban ecosystems

Cities create distinctive environmental conditions: the urban heat island effect raises temperatures by several degrees relative to surrounding countryside; impervious surfaces such as asphalt and concrete alter runoff and groundwater recharge; soils are often compacted, contaminated or imported; and light and noise pollution change animal behaviour. Habitat patches — parks, cemeteries, railway embankments, allotments, brownfield sites, green roofs and even walls — are embedded in a matrix of built land, and their connectivity determines which species can disperse through the city. Despite these stresses, some cities harbour surprisingly rich biodiversity, including rare species on post-industrial land.

Species in cities

Urban wildlife includes generalist "urban exploiters" such as pigeons, rats, raccoons, coyotes and red foxes; "adapters" that use urban resources while retaining natural behaviours; and "avoiders" sensitive to disturbance. Urban populations often evolve rapidly: documented examples include changes in beak shape in urban birds, altered timing of flowering, reduced dispersal structures in plants growing in pavement cracks, and behavioural shifts in song frequency to overcome traffic noise. Non-native and ornamental species make up a large share of urban floras, and cities can act as beachheads for invasive species.

History of the field

Early urban ecology grew out of the Chicago School of sociology in the 1920s, which applied ecological metaphors to human communities. Biological urban ecology developed later, with pioneering studies of Berlin's flora and fauna after the Second World War by Herbert Sukopp and colleagues, who showed that bombed sites and the strip along the Berlin Wall supported distinctive communities. In the United States, the National Science Foundation's Long Term Ecological Research sites in Baltimore (1997) and Phoenix (1997) institutionalised the study of cities as ecosystems, framing research as both "ecology in the city" and "ecology of the city" — the latter treating the whole metropolitan area, including its human inhabitants and infrastructure, as the ecosystem.

Applications

Urban ecology informs green infrastructure planning, stormwater management, urban forestry, biodiversity conservation, climate adaptation and environmental justice research. Practitioners evaluate ecosystem services provided by urban nature — cooling, air filtration, flood mitigation, pollination and recreation — and the unequal distribution of those services between neighbourhoods. Urban rewilding, daylighting of buried streams, wildlife crossings and dark-sky initiatives are applied outcomes of the discipline.
