Torture Memos

The Office of Legal Counsel memoranda, released in 2009

The Torture Memos, also known as the Bybee–Yoo memos, are a series of legal memoranda prepared by the United States Department of Justice's Office of Legal Counsel (OLC) in 2002–2005 that advised the Central Intelligence Agency and the Department of Defense on the use of "enhanced interrogation techniques" on detainees in the war on terror.

Contents

The most famous of the memos, dated 1 August 2002 and signed by Assistant Attorney General Jay Bybee (and largely drafted by John Yoo), adopted a narrow definition of torture, reasoning that physical pain must be "equivalent in intensity to the pain accompanying serious physical injury, such as organ failure" to violate the federal torture statute. Companion memos approved specific techniques including waterboarding, stress positions, sleep deprivation and confinement with insects.

Controversy and withdrawal

The memos became public after the Abu Ghraib scandal in 2004. The August 2002 memo was withdrawn later that year by Jack Goldsmith, who considered it legally flawed. Further memos, written by Steven Bradbury in 2005, were released by the Obama administration in April 2009, when President Barack Obama stated that the techniques constituted torture and banned them, while declining to prosecute officials who had relied on the advice.

Aftermath

The memos remain central to debates over executive power, the legal definition of torture, and the ethics of government lawyers. A 2010 Justice Department review criticised the authors' professional judgement but found no professional misconduct warranting discipline.
