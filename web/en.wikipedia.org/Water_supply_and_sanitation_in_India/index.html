Water supply and sanitation in India

Water supply and sanitation in India has improved substantially since independence, but challenges of water quality, intermittent supply, groundwater depletion, and sanitation coverage remain significant for the country's population of more than 1.4 billion. Responsibility for water supply and sanitation is shared between the central government, state governments, and urban local bodies and village panchayats.

Access

Access to improved drinking-water sources is now widespread, though piped water on premises is less common and supply in cities is often intermittent — only a few hours per day — leading households to rely on storage tanks and groundwater borewells. The Jal Jeevan Mission, launched in 2019, aims to provide tap water connections to all rural households. Groundwater, extracted with millions of wells, supplies most irrigation and much drinking water, and aquifers in northwestern India are being depleted.

Sanitation

The Swachh Bharat Mission (Clean India Mission), launched in 2014, financed the construction of over 100 million household toilets and declared rural India open-defecation free in 2019, though independent surveys found continued gaps in usage and waste management. Sewerage coverage in cities remains partial, and a large share of urban wastewater is discharged untreated into rivers such as the Ganges, which the Namami Gange programme seeks to clean.

Challenges

Key challenges include water contamination (including fluoride, arsenic, and salinity in some aquifers), non-revenue water losses in utilities, low cost recovery, monsoon dependence, and the impacts of climate change on Himalayan glaciers and rainfall.
