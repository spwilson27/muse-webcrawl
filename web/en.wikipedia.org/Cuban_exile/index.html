The Cuban exile refers to the emigration of Cubans from the island following the 1959 Cuban Revolution, largely in opposition to the government of Fidel Castro. More than a million Cubans left over the following decades, most settling in the United States, where they formed an influential diaspora centred on Miami's Little Havana and shaped U.S. politics and policy toward Cuba.

Waves of emigration

The first "golden exile" of 1959–1962 brought professionals, business owners and some 14,000 unaccompanied children sent by their parents under Operation Peter Pan. The Camarioca boatlift of 1965 and the twice-daily Freedom Flights that followed carried hundreds of thousands more. In 1980 the Mariel boatlift brought roughly 125,000 Cubans to Florida in a few months, and in 1994 tens of thousands of balseros took to sea on rafts during the economic crisis of the Special Period, leading to the "wet foot, dry foot" policy. The Cuban Adjustment Act of 1966 gave Cubans a uniquely fast path to U.S. permanent residency.

Influence

Exile organisations such as the Cuban American National Foundation long lobbied for a hard line against Havana, and the exile community became a decisive voting bloc in Florida. Later generations and newer arrivals have shown more diverse views, including support for engagement and travel to the island.
