The Maghreb (from Arabic al-Maghrib, "the west"), also known as Northwest Africa, is the western part of the Arab world and of North Africa. In its core sense it comprises Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia; in the broader definition used by the Arab Maghreb Union it also includes Libya and Mauritania. The region is separated from the rest of Africa by the Sahara and is bounded by the Mediterranean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean.

Geography

The Atlas Mountains run through Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia, sheltering a fertile coastal plain — the historic heartland of settlement — from the Sahara to the south. The region's climate is Mediterranean along the coast, becoming arid inland. Major cities include Casablanca, Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, and Marrakech.

History

The Maghreb's indigenous peoples are the Amazigh (Berbers). The coast was settled by Phoenicians and Carthaginians, then ruled by Rome, Vandals, and Byzantines before the Arab conquests of the 7th–8th centuries brought Islam and Arabic. Medieval dynasties such as the Almoravids and Almohads at times united the region and extended into Iberia. From the 16th century much of the coast fell under Ottoman influence, and in the 19th–20th centuries France colonised Algeria, Tunisia, and most of Morocco, with Spain and Italy holding other territories. Independence came between 1951 (Libya) and 1962 (Algeria).

Culture

Maghrebi culture blends Amazigh, Arab, Mediterranean, and sub-Saharan elements. Spoken varieties of Maghrebi Arabic (Darija) differ markedly from eastern Arabic, French remains widely used in administration and commerce, and Amazigh languages have gained official recognition in Morocco and Algeria. The cuisine — couscous, tagines, harissa, mint tea — is internationally known.
