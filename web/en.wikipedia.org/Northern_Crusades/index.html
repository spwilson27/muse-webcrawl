Northern Crusades

The Northern Crusades, also called the Baltic Crusades, were a series of Christianisation and colonisation campaigns undertaken by Catholic Christian military orders and kingdoms, primarily against the pagan Finnic, Baltic and West Slavic peoples around the southern and eastern shores of the Baltic Sea, from the 12th to the 15th century.

Background

The campaigns are conventionally dated from the Wendish Crusade of 1147 against the Polabian Slavs, preached in the context of the Second Crusade. The Livonian Crusade followed from the 1190s, when German missionaries and crusaders under Bishop Albert of Riga (founder of Riga in 1201) and the Order of the Brothers of the Sword conquered and converted Livonia and Estonia, with Danish participation in northern Estonia. The Prussian Crusade, conducted from the 1230s by the Teutonic Order, subjugated the Old Prussians and established the monastic state of the Teutonic Knights.

Course and outcome

Crusading in the region was accompanied by the founding of fortified towns, the immigration of German settlers (the Ostsiedlung), and the imposition of feudal and ecclesiastical structures. Resistance was prolonged: the Prussians mounted great uprisings, and pagan Lithuania remained independent, becoming a grand duchy that accepted Christianity only in 1387 through its union with Poland. The Teutonic Order's power was broken by Poland–Lithuania at the Battle of Grunwald (Tannenberg) in 1410.

The Swedish crusades into Finland, traditionally dated to the 12th and 13th centuries, integrated Finland into the Swedish realm. The Northern Crusades reshaped the ethnic, religious and political map of northeastern Europe, and their memory remains contested in the region.
