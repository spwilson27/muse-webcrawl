Aeschylus (c. 525/524 – c. 456/455 BC) was an ancient Greek tragedian often described as the father of tragedy. Academics' knowledge of the genre begins with his work, and according to Aristotle, he expanded the number of characters in a play to allow for conflict among them, whereas previously characters interacted only with the chorus. Aeschylus also introduced the second actor (deuteragonist) to the stage and is credited with innovations in costume and stage decoration.

Life

Aeschylus was born around 525 BC in Eleusis, a small town northwest of Athens. His family was wealthy and well established; his father, Euphorion, was a member of the Eupatridae, the ancient nobility of Attica. As a youth, Aeschylus worked at a vineyard until, according to tradition, the god Dionysus visited him in his sleep and ordered him to turn his attention to the nascent art of tragedy.

Aeschylus fought in the Greco-Persian Wars, taking part in the Battle of Marathon (490 BC), where his brother Cynegeirus was killed, and he may also have fought at Salamis (480 BC) and Plataea (479 BC). His experience of war deeply informed his plays, most directly The Persians (472 BC), the only surviving Greek tragedy based on contemporary events.

Works

Aeschylus is said to have written between 70 and 90 plays, of which only seven survive in complete form:

  • The Persians (472 BC)
  • Seven Against Thebes (467 BC)
  • The Suppliants (c. 463 BC)
  • Agamemnon (458 BC)
  • The Libation Bearers (458 BC)
  • The Eumenides (458 BC)
  • Prometheus Bound (attributed; date disputed)

The Oresteia trilogy — Agamemnon, The Libation Bearers and The Eumenides — is the only complete trilogy of Greek tragedies to survive and is considered his greatest work. It traces the curse on the House of Atreus from the murder of Agamemnon to the establishment of justice through the law court of the Areopagus in Athens.

Death and legacy

Aeschylus travelled to Sicily several times and died at Gela around 456/455 BC. A later legend held that he was killed when an eagle dropped a tortoise on his bald head, mistaking it for a rock. His epitaph, said to have been composed by himself, commemorated his valour at Marathon rather than his poetry. Aeschylus' plays continued to be performed after his death, and later tragedians such as Sophocles and Euripides were influenced by his work.
