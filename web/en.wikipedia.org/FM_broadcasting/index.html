FM broadcasting

FM broadcasting is a method of radio broadcasting that uses frequency modulation (FM) of the radio carrier wave to carry audio. Invented in 1933 by American engineer Edwin Armstrong, wide-band FM offered dramatically better sound quality and resistance to static and interference than the amplitude modulation (AM) broadcasting then dominant. In most of the world, FM broadcasting uses the VHF band from 87.5 to 108.0 MHz, divided into channels 200 kHz wide (in the Americas) with a maximum frequency deviation of ±75 kHz.

Technology

An FM broadcast signal carries a baseband composite signal: the sum of left and right audio channels up to 15 kHz, a 19 kHz pilot tone used to decode the stereo difference signal on a 38 kHz suppressed subcarrier, and auxiliary services such as the Radio Data System (RDS) at 57 kHz, which conveys station identification and text. Pre-emphasis boosts high frequencies before transmission to improve signal-to-noise ratio, with a matching de-emphasis at the receiver. FM exhibits a "capture effect," in which the receiver locks onto the stronger of two signals on the same frequency.

History

Armstrong demonstrated FM to RCA in the 1930s and built an experimental station, W2XMN in Alpine, New Jersey. Commercial FM stations began broadcasting in the United States in 1941 in a band at 42–50 MHz; in 1945 the FCC controversially moved FM to its present band, obsoleting early receivers — a decision Armstrong fought until his death in 1954. FM grew slowly against entrenched AM, but superior fidelity drove rapid adoption for music formats from the 1960s and 1970s; stereo broadcasting (the Zenith-GE system) was standardized in 1961. By the late 1970s FM surpassed AM in audience in the United States, a pattern repeated worldwide.

FM today

FM remains the principal terrestrial music radio medium in most countries, though it faces migration pressure: Norway completed a national FM switch-off in favour of DAB in 2017, while other countries have delayed or rejected similar plans, and HD Radio provides digital sidebands in North America. Low-power FM (LPFM) services license community stations in several countries.
