Hippolytus (mythology)

In Greek mythology, Hippolytus (Ancient Greek: Ἱππόλυτος, "let loose of horses") was the son of Theseus, king of Athens, and an Amazon — variously named Antiope, Hippolyta or Melanippe. A devoted hunter and worshipper of the virgin goddess Artemis, Hippolytus scorned love and the goddess Aphrodite, whose revenge drives his tragedy, one of the most retold stories of antiquity.

The myth

Spurned by Hippolytus's contempt, Aphrodite caused his stepmother Phaedra to fall desperately in love with him. When Hippolytus rejected Phaedra's advances — conveyed in Euripides' surviving play by her nurse — Phaedra hanged herself, leaving a letter falsely accusing Hippolytus of violating her. Theseus, believing the letter, used one of three curses granted him by Poseidon to call destruction on his son. As Hippolytus drove his chariot along the shore, a monstrous bull rose from the sea; his horses panicked, the chariot overturned, and Hippolytus, tangled in the reins, was dragged to his death. Artemis then revealed the truth to Theseus, and father and son were reconciled before Hippolytus died.

Sources and adaptations

The story is best known from Euripides' tragedy Hippolytus (428 BCE), his second treatment of the theme — the first, lost, version portrayed a shameless Phaedra who propositioned Hippolytus directly. Seneca's Phaedra influenced later treatments, including Racine's Phèdre (1677), widely considered the summit of French classical tragedy. In Roman tradition Hippolytus was said to have been resurrected by Asclepius and carried to Italy, where he was worshipped as the god Virbius at Nemi, alongside the nymph Egeria.

Cult

At Troezen, where the fatal chariot ride was set, Hippolytus received heroic cult: girls before marriage offered locks of their hair at his shrine, and his tomb and the myrtle tree Phaedra was said to have stabbed with a hairpin were shown to visitors.
