Necroptosis is a programmed form of necrosis, or inflammatory cell death. Conventionally, necrosis is associated with unprogrammed cell death resulting from cellular damage or infiltration by pathogens, in contrast to orderly, programmed cell death via apoptosis. The discovery of necroptosis showed that cells can execute necrosis in a programmed fashion, and that this pathway acts as a backup defence — particularly against pathogens — when apoptosis is blocked.

Mechanism

Necroptosis is best understood downstream of death receptors such as TNFR1. When caspase-8 is inhibited or absent, receptor-interacting protein kinases RIPK1 and RIPK3 assemble into a complex called the necrosome. RIPK3 phosphorylates the pseudokinase MLKL, which oligomerises and translocates to the plasma membrane, where it disrupts membrane integrity. The cell swells and ruptures, releasing its contents, including damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that provoke inflammation.

Medical significance

Necroptosis has been implicated in inflammatory and degenerative conditions including inflammatory bowel disease, ischaemia–reperfusion injury, pancreatitis, neurodegenerative diseases, and retinal degeneration. Viruses encode caspase inhibitors in some cases, and necroptosis via the sensor ZBP1 (which detects viral Z-form nucleic acids) provides an antiviral countermeasure. Pharmacological inhibition of RIPK1 — pioneered by the discovery of necrostatin-1 in 2005, which also coined the term — is an active area of drug development.
