The Samnite Wars were three conflicts (343–341, 326–304 and 298–290 BC) between the Roman Republic and the Samnites, a federation of Oscan-speaking hill peoples of the Apennines. Rome's eventual victory made it the dominant power of the Italian peninsula and set the pattern for its conquest of the Mediterranean world.

First Samnite War (343–341 BC)

War began when the Campanian city of Capua appealed to Rome against Samnite attack and surrendered itself into Roman protection. Roman victories at Mount Gaurus and Suessula forced a peace restoring the status quo; the war flowed into the Latin War (340–338), after which Rome reorganised its Italian alliances.

Second Samnite War (326–304 BC)

The long second war began over Naples. In 321 BC a Roman army was trapped in the defile of the Caudine Forks and forced to pass "under the yoke" in humiliation — one of Rome's most infamous disasters, which the Senate refused to accept as binding. Rome adapted: it built military colonies, cut the Appian Way toward Capua (begun 312 BC), reorganised the legion into flexible maniples suited to hill warfare, and ground Samnium down until peace in 304 BC.

Third Samnite War (298–290 BC)

The Samnites assembled a grand coalition with Etruscans, Umbrians and Gauls. At the Battle of Sentinum (295 BC) — among the largest battles of the era — Rome defeated the combined Samnite-Gallic army, the consul Decius Mus famously devoting himself to death for victory. Manius Curius Dentatus completed the submission of Samnium by 290 BC.

Consequences

The wars hardened Rome's army and its system of graded alliances and colonies, binding Italy into a military confederation. Samnite resistance flickered again in the Pyrrhic and Punic wars and in the Social War, but the outcome of 290 BC was never reversed.
