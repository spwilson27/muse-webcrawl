French Third Republic

The French Third Republic (French: Troisième République) was the system of government adopted in France from 4 September 1870, when the Second French Empire collapsed during the Franco-Prussian War, until 10 July 1940, when France's defeat by Nazi Germany in World War II led to the formation of the Vichy government. At nearly 70 years, it was the longest-lasting republican regime in France since 1789.

Consolidation and crises

Born of defeat — Napoleon III was captured at Sedan and Paris endured siege and the Paris Commune of 1871 — the republic was given constitutional laws in 1875 and consolidated under republicans like Jules Ferry, whose laws made primary education free, compulsory, and secular. The regime survived the Boulanger crisis, the Panama scandals, and the Dreyfus affair (1894–1906), which divided the nation over the wrongful treason conviction of a Jewish artillery captain, and it separated church and state in 1905. Overseas it built the second-largest colonial empire of the era.

War and interwar years

The republic bore the main Western Front burden of World War I and emerged victorious under Clemenceau, but lost 1.4 million soldiers. Interwar governments faced inflation, frequent cabinet turnover (over 100 governments in 70 years), the Stavisky riots of 1934, and then the left-wing Popular Front of Léon Blum in 1936, which enacted paid holidays and the 40-hour week. Defeat in the Battle of France brought the National Assembly to vote full powers to Marshal Pétain on 10 July 1940, ending the republic in practice.

Character

The Third Republic combined strong parliamentary sovereignty with a weak presidency (a reaction against both Napoleon III and MacMahon), ministerial instability with remarkable administrative continuity, universal male suffrage (women voted only from 1944), and a laïque civic culture centred on the schoolteacher and the town hall.
