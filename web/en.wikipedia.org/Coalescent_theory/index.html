Coalescent theory

Coalescent theory is a framework in population genetics that models the ancestry of a sample of gene copies backward in time. Rather than following all the individuals in a population forward through generations, the coalescent traces only the lineages of the sampled copies, which merge—coalesce—whenever two lineages descend from the same parent copy in the previous generation, until a single most recent common ancestor of the whole sample is reached.

The standard coalescent

The theory was developed in the early 1980s, principally by John Kingman, building on the Wright–Fisher model of genetic drift. In a population of constant size, each pair of lineages coalesces at a rate inversely proportional to population size, so the expected time back to the most recent common ancestor of a sample can be computed, as can the expected pattern of genetic variation if mutations are placed on the branches of the resulting genealogy. The total length of the coalescent tree determines how much variation a neutral sample is expected to contain.

Extensions and applications

The basic model has been extended to incorporate recombination, natural selection, population growth and subdivision, and migration. Coalescent methods underpin much of modern statistical population genetics: they are used to estimate population sizes and divergence times, to infer demographic history from genome data (as in studies of human origins), to analyze samples of pathogen genomes through time, and to simulate genetic data under complex evolutionary scenarios. Approximate Bayesian computation and likelihood-based inference frequently rely on coalescent simulations.
