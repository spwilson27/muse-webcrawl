Degenerate art (German: Entartete Kunst) was a term adopted in the 1920s by the Nazi Party in Germany to describe modern art. During the dictatorship of Adolf Hitler, German modernist art, including many works by internationally renowned artists, was removed from state-owned museums and banned in Nazi Germany on the grounds that such art was an "insult to German feeling", un-German, Freemasonic, Jewish, or Communist in nature.

Background

The concept of artistic "degeneracy" drew on earlier writing, notably Max Nordau's Entartung (Degeneration, 1892), and on racial theories that framed modernism — Expressionism, Cubism, Dada, Surrealism, abstraction — as symptoms of racial and moral decay. After 1933 the regime, through the Reich Chamber of Culture under Joseph Goebbels, purged museums, dismissed directors and artists, and restricted artistic production to heroic, racially idealized styles.

The 1937 exhibition

The Entartete Kunst exhibition opened in Munich on 19 July 1937, displaying some 650 confiscated works by 112 artists — among them Kandinsky, Klee, Kirchner, Beckmann, Dix, Chagall, Mondrian, and Picasso — hung chaotically with derisive slogans. It drew over two million visitors in Munich and millions more on tour, far outstripping the officially approved Great German Art Exhibition nearby. Ironically, it became many visitors' last chance to see the modern masters.

Confiscation and fate of works

A commission under Adolf Ziegler confiscated more than 16,000 works from German public collections. Many were sold abroad for hard currency (notably at the Fischer Gallery auction in Lucerne in 1939), exchanged through dealers such as Hildebrand Gurlitt, or destroyed — thousands were burned in Berlin in 1939. Artists were forbidden to work, dismissed from teaching posts, and in some cases driven into exile; a few died by suicide.

Legacy

The campaign is a central case study in state censorship of art. Post-war research and exhibitions, including the 1991 "Degenerate Art" exhibition in Los Angeles and ongoing provenance research (highlighted by the 2012 discovery of the Gurlitt trove in Munich), have sought to trace confiscated works and restore them to owners or document their loss.
