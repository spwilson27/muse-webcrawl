T. H. Green

Born: 7 April 1836, Birkin, Yorkshire, England
Died: 26 March 1882 (aged 45), Oxford, England
Alma mater: Balliol College, Oxford
Era: 19th-century philosophy
School: British idealism

Thomas Hill Green (7 April 1836 – 26 March 1882), known as T. H. Green, was an English philosopher, political radical and temperance reformer, and a member of the British idealism movement. Like all British idealists, Green was influenced by the metaphysical historicism of G. W. F. Hegel. He was one of the thinkers behind the brand of philosophy known as British idealism, which reacted against the doctrines of empiricism and utilitarianism that had dominated British philosophy for much of the nineteenth century.

Life

Green was born at Birkin in Yorkshire, where his father was rector. He was educated at Rugby School and Balliol College, Oxford, where he became a fellow in 1860 and later Whyte's Professor of Moral Philosophy (1878). He was also active in local politics in Oxford, serving on the city council, and campaigned for temperance, university extension and the widening of educational opportunity. He died of blood poisoning in 1882, aged 45.

Philosophy

Green's most important works were published posthumously, including the Prolegomena to Ethics (1883) and the Lectures on the Principles of Political Obligation. He argued that human consciousness constitutes experience through relations, and that reality is a single eternal spiritual whole. In ethics, he developed the idea of the "common good": rights and moral duties arise not from nature or mere contract but from social recognition directed toward the common good. This doctrine made him an intellectual founder of the British welfare-state tradition and an influence on the "new liberalism" of L. T. Hobhouse and others. His critique of Herbert Spencer and of utilitarian hedonism helped to redirect British moral philosophy toward idealism for a generation.
