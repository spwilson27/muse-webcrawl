Electrical synapse

Electrical synapse
StructureGap junction
Key proteinsConnexins (connexin 36 in mammals)
TransmissionBidirectional, near-instantaneous

An electrical synapse is a mechanical and electrically conducting link between two neurons, formed by a gap junction through which ions and small molecules pass directly from one cell to the next. Unlike chemical synapses, electrical synapses conduct signals almost without delay and often in both directions.

Structure

Gap junctions are clusters of intercellular channels called connexons, each made of six connexin proteins. When two connexons in adjacent membranes align, they form a continuous pore of about 1.2 to 2 nanometres that connects the cytoplasm of the two cells.

Function

Electrical synapses synchronise the activity of connected neurons, which is valuable in circuits requiring rapid, coordinated firing, such as escape reflexes in invertebrates and fish, and in networks of inhibitory interneurons in the mammalian cortex and retina. They also allow metabolic and developmental signalling between cells.

Comparison with chemical synapses

Electrical transmission is faster but less modifiable than chemical transmission; most electrical synapses cannot amplify signals or change sign from excitatory to inhibitory, though their conductance can be regulated by voltage, pH and calcium.
