Abstract art

Abstract art uses visual language of shape, form, color and line to create a composition which may exist with a degree of independence from visual references in the world. Western art had been, from the Renaissance up to the middle of the 19th century, underpinned by the logic of perspective and an attempt to reproduce an illusion of visible reality. By the end of the 19th century many artists felt a need to create a new kind of art which would encompass the fundamental changes taking place in technology, science and philosophy.

Early abstraction

The sources from which individual artists drew their theoretical arguments were diverse, and reflected the social and intellectual preoccupations in all areas of Western culture at that time. The Swedish artist Hilma af Klint (1862–1944) created abstract paintings as early as 1906, working with a spiritual and theosophical framework. Wassily Kandinsky's treatise Concerning the Spiritual in Art (1911) became a foundational text, arguing that art should express inner necessity rather than depict the material world.

Geometric abstraction

Between 1915 and 1925, Kazimir Malevich founded Suprematism in Russia, reducing painting to basic geometric forms such as the square of his Black Square (1915). In the Netherlands, Piet Mondrian and Theo van Doesburg developed De Stijl, restricting compositions to straight lines, primary colors, and black, white and gray. These movements influenced the Bauhaus school in Germany, where Kandinsky and Paul Klee taught.

Abstract expressionism

After the Second World War, New York became the center of abstract art. Abstract expressionists such as Jackson Pollock, with his poured "drip" paintings, and Mark Rothko, with his floating rectangles of color, pursued spontaneous, gestural or meditative modes of abstraction on monumental canvases. The critic Clement Greenberg championed this work as the culmination of modernist painting's drive toward flatness and medium specificity.

Later developments

Post-painterly abstraction, Color Field painting, Op art and Minimalism extended abstraction through the 1960s, while later generations repeatedly returned to non-representational strategies in painting, sculpture and digital media. Abstraction remains one of the central modes of contemporary art practice worldwide.
