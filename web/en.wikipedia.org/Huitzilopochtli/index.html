Huitzilopochtli

Huitzilopochtli
Nahuatl: Huītzilōpōchtli
Role: God of the sun and war; patron of Tenochtitlan
Cult centre: Templo Mayor, Tenochtitlan
Feast: Panquetzaliztli
Symbols: Hummingbird feathers, xiuhcoatl (fire serpent)

Huitzilopochtli (Classical Nahuatl: Huītzilōpōchtli, often glossed "hummingbird of the south/left") is the Aztec (Mexica) god of the sun and war and the patron deity of the Mexica people and their capital, Tenochtitlan. His shrine shared the summit of the Templo Mayor with that of the rain god Tlaloc.

Mythology

According to Mexica myth, Huitzilopochtli was born fully armed on the mountain Coatepec to the goddess Coatlicue, who had miraculously conceived him from a ball of feathers. He immediately slew his sister Coyolxauhqui, goddess of the moon, and routed his 400 brothers, the Centzon Huitznahua — a myth read as a daily allegory of the sun defeating the moon and stars. Huitzilopochtli also guided the Mexica on their legendary migration from Aztlan, commanding them to found their city where an eagle perched on a cactus — the image now on the flag of Mexico.

Worship

The god's cult was central to the Mexica state: war supplied captives whose hearts were offered so that the sun would continue its course and not fall into darkness. His great festival, Panquetzaliztli, fell in December. Priests fashioned a dough image of the god (tzoalli) which was ritually consumed, and the Templo Mayor was repeatedly enlarged in his honour.
