Colossi of Memnon

The Colossi of Memnon (Arabic: el-Colossat) are two massive stone statues of the Pharaoh Amenhotep III, who reigned in Egypt during the Eighteenth Dynasty. They have stood since 1350 BCE in the Theban Necropolis, west of the River Nile from the modern city of Luxor. The statues flanked the entrance to the pharaoh's mortuary temple, which was once the largest and most opulent in Egypt but was largely destroyed by floods and later quarrying.

Description

Each statue, carved from single blocks of quartzite sandstone, depicts Amenhotep III seated on a throne, and rises about 18 metres (59 feet) high; with its pedestal the total height approaches 21.3 metres. Smaller figures of the king's wife Tiye and his mother Mutemwiya are carved beside his legs. The stone was transported some 675 kilometres (420 miles) from quarries near modern Cairo to Thebes.

The "singing" statue

After an earthquake in 27 BCE damaged the northern colossus, it became famous in the Roman world for producing a musical sound at dawn, attributed at the time to the statue greeting the goddess Eos. Greek and Roman visitors identified the figure with the hero Memnon and left graffiti recording their visits. The sounds, probably caused by dew evaporating in cracks as the stone warmed, ceased after repairs ordered by the emperor Septimius Severus around 199 CE.
