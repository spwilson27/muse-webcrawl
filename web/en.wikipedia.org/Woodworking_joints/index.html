Woodworking joints

A woodworking joint is a connection between two or more pieces of wood, typically arranged at an angle, that fastens the pieces together in furniture, cabinetry, timber framing and other wooden structures. Joints may rely on the mechanical strength of the shaped wood itself, on adhesives, or on mechanical fasteners such as nails, screws, dowels or bolts. The choice of joint depends on the strength required, the appearance desired, the materials available and the skill and tools of the woodworker.

Types of joints

Butt joint

The butt joint is the simplest joint, in which the end of one piece of wood is simply butted against another and fixed with glue, nails or screws. Because end grain glues poorly, the plain butt joint is weak and is often reinforced with dowels, biscuits, screws driven at an angle ("toe-nailing") or metal plates.

Mitre joint

A mitre joint joins two pieces cut at an angle, usually 45 degrees, so that they meet at a corner — commonly 90 degrees in total. Mitres are widely used in picture frames and mouldings because they hide end grain and present a clean appearance, though they are relatively weak without reinforcement such as splines.

Dovetail joint

The dovetail joint consists of interlocking fan-shaped "tails" on one board fitting into corresponding "pins" on another. It is prized for its resistance to being pulled apart and is a hallmark of fine drawer and box construction. Variants include through dovetails, half-blind dovetails and sliding dovetails.

Mortise and tenon

In a mortise and tenon joint, a projecting tongue (the tenon) on one piece fits into a matching hole (the mortise) in another. One of the oldest and strongest joints, it is fundamental to frame-and-panel construction and timber framing. It may be glued, pinned with a drawbore peg, or wedged.

Finger and box joints

Finger joints interlock rectangular projections along the edges of two boards, giving a large gluing area and good strength; box joints are a related form used at corners, especially for drawers and boxes.

Lap joints

Lap joints overlap the two pieces, with material removed from one or both so the surfaces sit flush. Half-lap, cross-lap and dovetail-lap joints are common in framing and shelving.

Biscuit and dowel joints

Modern methods use inserted elements: compressed beech "biscuits" (plate joinery) that swell in oval slots, or cylindrical dowels glued into aligned holes, to register and strengthen butt and edge joints.

Properties and selection

Joint strength comes from long-grain-to-long-grain glue surface, mechanical interlock, and resistance to racking and withdrawal. Wood movement — expansion and contraction across the grain with humidity changes — must also be accommodated, which is why frame-and-panel construction allows panels to float within their frames.
