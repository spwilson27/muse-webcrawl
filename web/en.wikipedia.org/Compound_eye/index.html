Compound eye

A compound eye is a visual organ found in arthropods such as insects and crustaceans. It consists of many individual photoreception units called ommatidia (from a few in some primitive insects to around 30,000 in dragonflies), each with its own cornea, lens, and photoreceptor cells. The combined input forms a mosaic image.

Types

In apposition eyes, typical of day-active insects, each ommatidium is optically isolated by pigment, producing a relatively sharp but dim image. In superposition eyes, found in many nocturnal insects and deep-water crustaceans, light from many facets is combined onto a single rhabdom, greatly increasing sensitivity at the cost of acuity.

Properties

Compound eyes have a very wide field of view and are excellent at detecting movement, with flicker-fusion rates far exceeding those of humans, but their spatial resolution is limited by facet size and diffraction. Many compound eyes detect polarised light and ultraviolet wavelengths invisible to humans; mantis shrimps possess exceptionally complex colour and polarisation vision. Compound eyes evolved over 500 million years ago and are seen in trilobite fossils.
