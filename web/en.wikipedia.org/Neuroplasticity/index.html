Neuroplasticity

Plasticity includes growth of new synapses and reorganization of cortical maps.

Neuroplasticity, also known as neural plasticity or brain plasticity, is the ability of neural networks in the brain to change through growth and reorganization. These changes range from individual neuron pathways making new connections, to systematic adjustments like cortical remapping. Examples of neuroplasticity include circuit and network changes that result from learning a new ability, environmental influences, practice, and psychological stress.

Mechanisms

Synaptic plasticity — described by Donald Hebb's postulate that "cells that fire together wire together" — is implemented by long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD), in which synaptic strengths are adjusted in a calcium- and NMDA receptor-dependent manner. Structural plasticity includes growth and retraction of dendritic spines, axonal sprouting, and, in restricted regions such as the hippocampal dentate gyrus, adult neurogenesis. Homeostatic mechanisms stabilize network activity while permitting change.

Development and experience

Plasticity is greatest during early critical periods, when sensory experience sculpts circuits such as the visual cortex, as shown by the work of David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel. In adults, plasticity underlies skill learning, recovery after stroke (exploited by constraint-induced movement therapy), phantom limb phenomena, and the remapping of sensory cortices after amputation or blindness.

Clinical relevance

Maladaptive plasticity contributes to chronic pain, tinnitus, focal dystonia, and addiction, while harnessing plasticity is a goal of rehabilitation and of interventions such as neuromodulation.
