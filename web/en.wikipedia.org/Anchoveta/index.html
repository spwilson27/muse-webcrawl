The anchoveta (Engraulis ringens), also known as the Peruvian anchoveta, is a species of fish of the anchovy family, Engraulidae, found in the southeast Pacific Ocean. It is one of the most commercially important fish species in the world, and in many years the Peruvian anchoveta fishery has been the largest single-species fishery on Earth by tonnage.

Description

The anchoveta is a small schooling fish, typically reaching a maximum length of about 20 cm (8 in). It has a silvery, elongated body with a bluish-green back. Like other anchovies, it feeds primarily on plankton, filtering phytoplankton and zooplankton — especially large diatoms and copepods — from the water.

Distribution and habitat

The species inhabits the cold, nutrient-rich waters of the Humboldt Current system off the coasts of Peru and northern Chile. Upwelling in this region brings deep, nutrient-laden water to the surface, supporting enormous phytoplankton blooms that in turn sustain vast schools of anchoveta. The fish are typically found within about 80 km of the coast, in waters down to depths of roughly 180 m.

Fishery

The Peruvian fishery for anchoveta expanded dramatically in the 1950s and 1960s with the growth of the fishmeal industry. Most of the catch is not eaten directly but reduced to fishmeal and fish oil, used in aquaculture and livestock feed. Catches have exceeded 12 million tonnes in peak years, though the fishery is highly vulnerable to El Niño events: warm water suppresses upwelling, and during the strong El Niño of 1972–73 the stock collapsed, an event compounded by heavy overfishing. The fishery has since been managed with seasonal closures, quotas, and real-time monitoring of juvenile abundance.

Ecological role

Anchoveta are a keystone forage species. They are the principal food of guano-producing seabirds such as the Guanay cormorant and Peruvian booby, of Humboldt penguins, sea lions, and larger predatory fish including bonito and mackerel. Fluctuations in anchoveta abundance therefore ripple through the entire Humboldt Current ecosystem.
