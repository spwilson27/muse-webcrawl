Canadian Indian residential school system

The Canadian Indian residential school system was a network of boarding schools for Indigenous peoples of Canada, funded by the federal government and administered largely by Christian churches from the 19th century until the last school closed in 1997. Its purpose was forced assimilation — in the words of its architects, to remove Indigenous children from their families' influence and absorb them into Euro-Canadian culture. About 150,000 First Nations, Inuit, and Métis children were taken from their homes; thousands died, and many suffered abuse and the loss of their languages.

History

Precursors included church-run schools in New France and the Mohawk Institute (1831) in Brantford. Following the Bagot Commission (1842–44) and the Davin Report (1879), the federal government expanded the system; attendance became compulsory for status Indian children in 1894 and again under the 1920 Indian Act amendments. Children were forbidden to speak their languages, given inadequate food and medical care, and subjected to physical, emotional, and sexual abuse. Tuberculosis and other diseases caused catastrophic mortality, long documented by officials such as medical inspector Peter Bryce, whose warnings were suppressed.

Closure and apology

The government began closing schools in the 1970s; the last, Gordon's Indian Residential School in Saskatchewan, closed in 1996 (some accounts date the final closure to 1997). Churches issued apologies beginning in 1986. The 2006 Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement — the largest class-action settlement in Canadian history — established compensation and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC).

Truth and Reconciliation Commission

The TRC (2008–2015) gathered testimony from thousands of survivors and concluded that the system amounted to "cultural genocide," issuing 94 Calls to Action. On 11 June 2008 Prime Minister Stephen Harper formally apologized on behalf of the Government of Canada. Pope Francis apologized on Canadian soil in 2022, asking forgiveness for the Church's role.

Unmarked graves

In 2021, ground-penetrating radar surveys at former school sites — beginning with 215 potential burials at Kamloops — prompted a national reckoning and an ongoing search of many sites. The National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (30 September) is now a federal statutory holiday.
