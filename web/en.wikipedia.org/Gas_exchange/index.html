Gas exchange

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gas exchange is the physical process by which gases move passively by diffusion across a surface. For example, this surface might be the air/water interface, the surface of a gas bubble in a liquid, a gas-permeable membrane, or a biological membrane that forms the boundary between an organism and its extracellular environment.

Gases are constantly consumed and produced by aerobic and anaerobic cells and tissues throughout the body, and gas exchange is therefore a vital process: organisms must obtain oxygen (O2) from the environment and dispose of carbon dioxide (CO2), a waste product of cellular respiration. In single-celled organisms, exchange occurs directly across the cell membrane. Multicellular organisms have evolved a wide variety of exchange surfaces and transport systems: the lungs and alveoli of mammals, the gills of fish, the tracheal systems of insects, and the stomata and spongy mesophyll of plant leaves are all gas exchange surfaces.

Principles of diffusion

Gas exchange surfaces share several properties that maximize the rate of diffusion, as summarized by Fick's law: a large surface area relative to volume, a thin diffusion pathway, a steep concentration gradient maintained by ventilation and blood flow, and (in many animals) a moist surface in which gases dissolve before diffusing. The rate of diffusion is proportional to surface area and the difference in partial pressure, and inversely proportional to the thickness of the barrier and the square root of the gas's molecular mass (Graham's law).

Ventilation — the movement of the respiratory medium (air or water) over the exchange surface — and perfusion — the flow of blood on the other side — keep the partial-pressure gradients steep. The matching of ventilation to perfusion is critical: regions of the lung that are ventilated but not perfused (or vice versa) contribute to physiological dead space or shunt.

Gas exchange in mammals

In mammals, air is inhaled through the nose and mouth, passes down the trachea and bronchi, and reaches the alveoli — tiny air sacs, some 300 million per human lung, providing a total surface area of roughly 70 square metres. Each alveolus is wrapped in a dense network of pulmonary capillaries. The diffusion barrier — alveolar epithelium, a fused basement membrane, and capillary endothelium — is as thin as 0.2–0.6 micrometres in places.

Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air (partial pressure about 13.3 kPa) into the deoxygenated blood arriving in the pulmonary capillaries (about 5.3 kPa), while carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction. Gas is carried in the blood mostly bound to haemoglobin (oxygen) and as bicarbonate ions (carbon dioxide). At the tissues, gradients reverse: oxygen is released where metabolic demand is high, aided by the Bohr effect, in which acidity and CO2 promote oxygen unloading.

Gas exchange in fish

Fish extract oxygen from water using gills — feathery structures of filaments and lamellae that provide a huge surface area. Water is pumped or rammed over the gills while blood flows through the lamellae in the opposite direction, a countercurrent arrangement. Countercurrent exchange keeps a favourable gradient along the whole length of the exchange surface, allowing fish to extract a large proportion (often over 80 percent) of the oxygen dissolved in the water passing the gills — far more efficient than the concurrent arrangement found in some other systems.

Gas exchange in plants

Plants exchange gases through stomata — adjustable pores, mostly on the undersides of leaves, flanked by guard cells that open and close in response to light, CO2 concentration, and water status. Carbon dioxide is taken up for photosynthesis while oxygen and water vapour leave; the loss of water by transpiration is the unavoidable cost of opening stomata. Inside the leaf, the loosely packed spongy mesophyll provides a large moist internal surface across which gases diffuse to and from the photosynthesizing cells. Some plants use crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM), opening stomata at night to reduce water loss in arid conditions.

Gas exchange in insects

Insects breathe through a network of tubes called tracheae, which open to the outside through valve-like spiracles. The tracheae branch into fine tracheoles that reach individual cells, delivering oxygen by diffusion (supplemented in larger or more active insects by pumping movements of the abdomen). Because the system bypasses the blood, insects can meet high metabolic demands during flight; but diffusion limits their maximum body size, a constraint that was relaxed in the high-oxygen atmosphere of the Carboniferous period, when giant dragonfly-like insects existed.
