Alveolus

A pulmonary alveolus (plural: alveoli) is a tiny hollow air sac found in the lungs of mammals, at the terminal ends of the respiratory tree. Alveoli are the primary sites of gas exchange: oxygen diffuses from inhaled air into the blood of surrounding capillaries, and carbon dioxide diffuses out to be exhaled.

Structure

Human lungs contain an estimated 300 to 500 million alveoli, providing a total surface area of roughly 70 square metres for gas exchange. Each alveolus is about 0.2–0.3 mm in diameter and is wrapped in a dense network of pulmonary capillaries.

The alveolar wall consists of a single layer of epithelial cells: flattened type I pneumocytes, across which gases diffuse, and cuboidal type II pneumocytes, which secrete pulmonary surfactant — a substance that reduces surface tension and prevents the alveoli from collapsing at the end of exhalation. Alveolar macrophages patrol the air spaces and clear inhaled particles and pathogens.

Function

Gas exchange across the blood–air barrier follows Fick's laws of diffusion, driven by partial-pressure gradients. Diseases such as emphysema destroy alveolar walls and reduce surface area, while pneumonia and pulmonary oedema can fill or thicken the air spaces, impairing diffusion.
