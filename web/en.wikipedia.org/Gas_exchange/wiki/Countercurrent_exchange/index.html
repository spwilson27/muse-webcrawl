Countercurrent exchange

Countercurrent exchange is the transfer of a property (heat, gases, or solutes) between two fluids flowing in opposite directions through adjacent channels. Because a gradient is maintained along the whole length of the exchanger, countercurrent arrangements can transfer far more of the property than cocurrent (same-direction) flow, in which the two streams merely approach equilibrium.

In biology

Fish gills

In fish gills, water flows over the lamellae in the opposite direction to the blood within them. Blood always meets water with a higher oxygen concentration, so oxygen continues to diffuse into the blood along the entire lamella; extraction efficiencies can exceed 80%, far above what cocurrent flow could achieve. This is essential because water contains much less oxygen per volume than air.

Heat conservation

Countercurrent heat exchange in the limbs of wading birds, the flippers of dolphins, and the retia mirabilia of tuna and some sharks conserves body heat in cold water. In the kidney, the countercurrent multiplier of the loop of Henle concentrates urine; the vasa recta act as countercurrent exchangers preserving the medullary gradient.

In engineering

Countercurrent flow is standard in industrial heat exchangers, distillation columns, and chemical extraction, wherever maximal transfer of heat or mass between streams is desired.
