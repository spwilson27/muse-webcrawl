Fick's laws of diffusion

Fick's laws of diffusion, formulated by Adolf Fick in 1855, describe the movement of particles from regions of high concentration to regions of low concentration. They are foundational to the study of mass transport in physics, chemistry, and physiology — including the diffusion of respiratory gases across the alveolar membrane.

First law

Fick's first law relates the diffusive flux to the concentration gradient under steady-state conditions:

J = −D ∂φ/∂x

where J is the diffusion flux, D is the diffusion coefficient, and ∂φ/∂x is the concentration gradient. The negative sign indicates flow down the gradient.

Second law

Fick's second law describes how diffusion changes concentration over time:

∂φ/∂t = D ∂²φ/∂x²

It can be derived from the first law together with conservation of mass.

Physiological application

In the lungs, the rate of gas transfer across the blood–air barrier is proportional to the surface area and the partial-pressure gradient and inversely proportional to membrane thickness — the practical form of Fick's law used in pulmonary medicine. Diseases that thicken the barrier or reduce surface area lower the diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), a standard clinical test.
