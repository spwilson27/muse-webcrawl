Gill

A gill is a respiratory organ found in many aquatic organisms that extracts dissolved oxygen from water and excretes carbon dioxide. Gills are typically highly branched and folded filamentous structures richly supplied with blood, presenting a large surface area for gas exchange.

Structure and function

In bony fish, four pairs of gills lie beneath a protective operculum on each side of the head. Each gill consists of an arch bearing rows of filaments, which in turn carry thin plate-like lamellae. Water is drawn in through the mouth and pumped over the lamellae. Blood in the lamellae flows opposite to the water current — a countercurrent exchange that maximises oxygen uptake.

Gills in other animals

Many invertebrates possess gills: molluscs use feathery ctenidia in the mantle cavity, crustaceans bear gills attached to their appendages, and aquatic insect larvae may have tracheal gills. Some amphibians, such as the axolotl, retain external gills throughout life. External gills, exposed directly to the water, are vulnerable to damage and pollution and are usually protected or internalised in more active animals.

Other functions

Beyond respiration, gills participate in osmoregulation — the control of salt and water balance — acid–base regulation, and the excretion of nitrogenous waste, largely as ammonia.
