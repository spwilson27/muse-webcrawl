Stoma

In botany, a stoma (plural: stomata) is a pore found in the epidermis of leaves, stems, and other plant organs that controls the rate of gas exchange. The pore is bordered by a pair of specialised cells called guard cells, which change shape to open or close it.

Function

Stomata admit carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, which the plant uses for photosynthesis, and allow oxygen and water vapour to escape. The loss of water through open stomata — transpiration — is the unavoidable cost of CO2 uptake, so plants must balance carbon gain against dehydration.

Mechanism

Guard cells open the pore by accumulating potassium ions (and associated anions), drawing in water by osmosis and swelling. Because their inner walls are thicker and their cellulose microfibrils are oriented radially, the cells bow outward as they swell, opening the pore. The hormone abscisic acid, produced under drought stress, triggers ion efflux and closure. Light, humidity, temperature, and internal CO2 concentration also influence stomatal aperture.

Distribution and evolution

Stomatal density varies with species and environment; many plants concentrate stomata on the underside of leaves. Stomata first appeared in early land plants over 400 million years ago and were a key innovation in terrestrial life. Their density in fossil leaves is used as a proxy for past atmospheric CO2 levels.

The word "stoma" is also used in zoology for mouth-like openings, and in medicine for a surgically created opening in the body (see the medical article on stomas).
