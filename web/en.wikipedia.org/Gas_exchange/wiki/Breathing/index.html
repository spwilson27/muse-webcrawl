Breathing

Breathing is the process of moving air into and out of the lungs to facilitate gas exchange with the internal environment, mostly by bringing oxygen in and flushing out carbon dioxide. In humans and other mammals it is performed principally by the diaphragm and the intercostal muscles of the rib cage.

Mechanism

During inhalation the diaphragm contracts and flattens and the external intercostals lift the ribs, increasing thoracic volume and lowering intrapulmonary pressure so air flows in. Quiet exhalation is largely passive, driven by elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall. Forced breathing, as during exercise or singing, recruits accessory muscles.

A resting adult breathes about 12–20 times per minute, moving roughly 500 mL of air per breath (the tidal volume). Total lung capacity is around 6 litres in an average adult male.

Control

Breathing is generated by respiratory centres in the medulla and pons of the brainstem and is normally automatic, though it can be voluntarily overridden within limits. Central and peripheral chemoreceptors monitor carbon dioxide, pH, and oxygen in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid; rising CO2 is the dominant stimulus to breathe more deeply and rapidly.

Gas exchange

In the alveoli, oxygen diffuses into pulmonary capillary blood while carbon dioxide diffuses out, a process driven by partial-pressure gradients. Breathing is thus the ventilatory component of respiration, distinct from cellular respiration in the tissues.

Variations and disorders

Abnormal patterns include tachypnoea (rapid breathing), apnoea (cessation), Cheyne–Stokes respiration, and hyperventilation. Disorders of breathing control appear in sleep apnoea, Ondine's curse, and opioid overdose.
