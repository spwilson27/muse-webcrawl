Prestige (sociolinguistics)

In sociolinguistics, prestige is the level of regard normally accorded to a specific language variety or feature within a speech community. High-prestige (overtly prestigious) varieties are those associated with education, wealth, and institutional authority—typically the standard language taught in schools and used in broadcasting—while low-prestige varieties may nonetheless carry covert prestige, signalling local identity, solidarity, and toughness within their communities.

Overt and covert prestige

The concept was developed in the work of William Labov, whose studies of New York City department stores and of Martha's Vineyard showed speakers shifting their pronunciation toward prestigious norms in careful speech. Peter Trudgill's research in Norwich found that working-class men in particular over-reported their use of nonstandard forms, evidence that vernacular speech held covert prestige as a marker of masculinity and group loyalty.

Prestige helps explain the direction of language change: innovations from high-status groups tend to spread as "change from above", while vernacular changes spread as "change from below". Speakers' awareness of prestige hierarchies also underlies phenomena such as hypercorrection, style-shifting, and dialect levelling.
