Home theater

A home theater (also written home theatre) is an audio/video equipment setup in a private home, usually a dedicated room, designed to recreate the experience of watching a film in a commercial movie theater. A typical system combines a large-screen display or video projector, a multichannel surround-sound system driven by an AV receiver, and one or more source components such as a Blu-ray player, media streamer, or game console. The term home theater in a box describes a packaged system sold with all speakers and electronics included.

History

Home movie viewing began with film projectors and, later, videocassette recorders, but the modern home theater emerged in the 1980s and 1990s as stereo television broadcasts, VHS hi-fi audio, and LaserDisc brought multichannel sound into the home. Dolby Surround and Dolby Pro Logic matrixed a center and surround channel from a stereo signal, and enthusiasts assembled component systems around early AV receivers. The arrival of DVD in 1997, with discrete 5.1-channel Dolby Digital and DTS soundtracks, made true surround sound a mass-market feature. High-definition discs followed: Blu-ray and HD DVD added lossless audio in the form of Dolby TrueHD and DTS-HD Master Audio, and object-based formats Dolby Atmos and DTS:X later added height channels. Large flat-panel televisions and affordable 4K projectors have since made cinema-scale images commonplace, while streaming services deliver multichannel and immersive audio without physical media.

Components

Display

The picture may come from a large television—commonly an OLED, LCD, or mini-LED set of 55 inches or more—or from a front projector and projection screen, which can produce images of 100 inches and larger. Dedicated rooms favor projection with acoustically transparent screens that allow the center speaker to sit behind the image, as in commercial cinemas.

AV receiver and amplification

The AV receiver is the hub of the system: it switches and processes video, decodes surround formats from the lossy (Dolby Digital, DTS) to the lossless (Dolby TrueHD, DTS-HD Master Audio) and object-based (Atmos, DTS:X), and amplifies the signal to the speakers. Higher-end systems use a separate surround processor and power amplifiers. Automatic room-correction systems measure the room with a microphone and apply equalization and delay to compensate for speaker placement and room acoustics.

Speakers and subwoofers

A basic system has five speakers and a subwoofer (5.1): left, center, and right across the front, two surround speakers at the sides or rear, and a powered subwoofer handling low-frequency effects and bass below the main speakers' range. The center channel carries most dialogue and is considered the most important single speaker for film sound. Larger systems add rear surrounds (7.1) and ceiling or height speakers for immersive formats, written in configurations such as 5.1.2 or 7.1.4, where the final number counts height channels.

Sources

Sources include Ultra HD Blu-ray and Blu-ray players, streaming devices and smart-TV apps, game consoles, cable and satellite boxes, and home theater PCs. Disc sources remain prized by enthusiasts for their uncompressed or losslessly compressed audio and higher video bitrates compared with streaming.

Speaker configurations

  • 2.0 / 2.1 — stereo pair, with or without a subwoofer.
  • 3.1 — front left, center, right, and subwoofer.
  • 5.1 — the classic surround layout, standard since DVD.
  • 7.1 — adds two rear surround channels behind the listener.
  • 5.1.2 / 5.1.4 / 7.1.4 — adds two or four height channels for Dolby Atmos and DTS:X.

Dedicated rooms and design

Enthusiasts often build dedicated theater rooms with controlled lighting, acoustic treatment (absorption and diffusion panels), tiered seating, and sound isolation. The THX certification program, created by Lucasfilm, sets performance standards for equipment and room design intended to reproduce the director's intended soundtrack at reference level. Design guidelines typically address viewing distance and angle, speaker placement relative to the main listening position, subwoofer placement to manage room modes, and screen gain and black levels in darkened rooms.

The growth of home theater has been linked to changes in film distribution: premium video-on-demand and day-and-date streaming releases allow new films to be watched at home on release, and the quality gap between a well-equipped home system and a commercial cinema has narrowed considerably.
