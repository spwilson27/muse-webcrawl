DTS-HD Master Audio

DTS-HD Master Audio is a multi-channel, lossless audio codec developed by DTS, Inc. (now part of Xperi). It is used principally on Blu-ray Disc titles, where it serves as one of the two principal lossless surround formats alongside its chief competitor, Dolby TrueHD. DTS-HD Master Audio delivers audio performance that is bit-for-bit identical to the original studio master recording, while still retaining a conventional DTS core stream so that titles remain playable on older equipment.

History

DTS-HD Master Audio was introduced in the mid-2000s as the lossless flagship of the DTS-HD family, at the launch of the high-definition optical disc formats, Blu-ray Disc and HD DVD. Both formats mandated support for conventional DTS decoding, and Blu-ray players were required to handle the DTS core stream even where full Master Audio decoding was optional. As Blu-ray won the format war, DTS-HD Master Audio became the dominant lossless soundtrack format on commercial discs, helped by the fact that a single encode carried both the lossless extension and a legacy core. With the arrival of Ultra HD Blu-ray, Master Audio tracks continued to be widely used, frequently as the carrier for DTS:X object-based immersive audio.

Technical details

The codec uses a lossless compression algorithm that typically reduces the size of a raw PCM master by a factor of roughly two, with the exact ratio varying by content. On Blu-ray, DTS-HD Master Audio streams may run at variable bitrates up to a ceiling of 24.5 Mbit/s; on HD DVD the ceiling was 18.0 Mbit/s. Sampling rates of 48 kHz and 96 kHz are supported for multi-channel material, and 192 kHz for stereo. Audio may be encoded at up to 24 bits per sample.

Although the DTS-HD specification itself permits a larger number of channels and flexible speaker remapping, the Blu-ray implementation is limited to 7.1 discrete channels: front left and right, center, low-frequency effects (LFE), side surrounds, and rear surrounds. A distinctive feature of the format is its ability to store a secondary, differently arranged mix—such as a 5.1 or stereo downmix—within the extension data, allowing the player to select the configuration that matches the listener's home theater setup.

Core + extension structure

Every DTS-HD Master Audio stream is built in two layers:

  • Core — a standard DTS Digital Surround stream (up to 5.1 channels at 48 kHz, at bitrates up to 1,509 kbit/s) that any DTS decoder can play.
  • Extension — additional residual data which, when combined with the decoded core, reconstructs the original master bit-for-bit. The extension can also carry extra channels (such as the two additional channels that expand 5.1 to 7.1) and higher sampling rates.

This architecture guaranteed backward compatibility across the installed base of AV receivers: listeners without a Master Audio decoder still heard full-quality core DTS, while equipped systems reproduced the lossless master.
