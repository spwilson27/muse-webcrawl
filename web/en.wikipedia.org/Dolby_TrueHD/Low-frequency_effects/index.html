The low-frequency effects (LFE) channel is a band-limited audio track that is used for reproducing deep and intense low-frequency sounds in the 3–120 Hz frequency range. It is the ".1" in notations such as 5.1 and 7.1 surround sound, and it is reproduced by a subwoofer rather than by the main loudspeakers in a home theater system.

When a soundtrack is encoded in Dolby TrueHD, a lossless audio codec developed by Dolby Laboratories for use on Blu-ray Disc, the LFE channel is carried as a discrete channel within the bitstream. Because TrueHD is lossless, the low-frequency content delivered to the subwoofer is bit-for-bit identical to the studio master, preserving the full impact of explosions, musical bass, and other effects as the mixer intended.

History

The LFE channel emerged from the needs of 1970s theatrical sound. Systems such as Sensurround used dedicated low-frequency loudspeakers to reproduce rumble for films like Earthquake (1974). When Dolby Stereo 70 mm formats appeared, engineers found that the format's usable tracks could be devoted in part to bass effects. Dolby Digital, introduced in 1991, formally included a discrete LFE channel limited to content below about 120 Hz, recorded 10 dB lower in level than the main channels to provide additional headroom on playback.

Relationship to bass management

The LFE channel is distinct from the low-frequency content redirected by a receiver's bass management system. Bass management takes bass below a crossover frequency (commonly 80 Hz) from the main channels and sends it to the subwoofer; the LFE channel is a separate, authored track that is added to that redirected bass in the subwoofer output.
