Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011

The Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 (c. 14) was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that introduced fixed-term parliaments to the United Kingdom, with elections scheduled every five years. It was enacted during the Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition government following the 2010 general election.

Provisions

The Act provided that general elections would be held on the first Thursday in May every five years. An early election could be triggered only in two ways: if two-thirds of MPs (including vacant seats) voted for one in the House of Commons, or if the Commons passed a motion of no confidence in the government and no new government won a vote of confidence within fourteen days. The Act removed the royal prerogative to dissolve Parliament.

Use and controversy

Early elections were held under the Act in 2017, when Theresa May secured the required two-thirds majority, and in 2019, when the two-thirds route failed repeatedly and Parliament instead passed separate legislation, the Early Parliamentary General Election Act 2019, setting an election date by simple majority. Critics argued the 2019 episode showed the Act did not bind a determined government; supporters argued it had restrained executive power.

Repeal

The Act was repealed by the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, which revived the prerogative powers relating to the dissolution of Parliament and included an ouster clause intended to prevent judicial review of dissolution decisions.
