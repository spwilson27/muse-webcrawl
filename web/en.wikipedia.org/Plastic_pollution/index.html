Plastic pollution is the accumulation of plastic objects and particles — bottles, bags, fishing gear, and microscopic fragments — in Earth's environment, adversely affecting wildlife, habitats, and potentially human health. Because conventional plastics do not biodegrade but only break into ever-smaller pieces, nearly all plastic ever produced (over 8 billion tonnes since the 1950s) still exists somewhere as waste. Only about 9% of plastic waste has been recycled; most is landfilled, incinerated, or leaks into nature.

Sources and distribution

The largest sources are single-use packaging, which accounts for roughly 40% of plastic demand, along with discarded fishing equipment, agricultural films, and synthetic clothing fibres shed in washing. Rivers carry land-based waste to the sea, where currents concentrate debris in subtropical gyres, most famously the Great Pacific Garbage Patch. Weathering fragments macroplastics into microplastics (under 5 mm) and nanoplastics, now detected from Arctic ice and deep-sea sediment to drinking water, seafood, and human blood and placental tissue.

Effects

Marine animals ingest plastic or become entangled in it: seabirds, turtles, seals, and whales are among at least 700 species documented to interact with debris, often fatally. Microplastics enter food webs, sorb pollutants, and may act as vectors for chemicals and microbes. On land, plastic mulches and waste alter soils, and open burning of waste releases toxic emissions. The full consequences for human health — from endocrine-disrupting additives such as phthalates and bisphenols — remain under active investigation.

Responses

Responses range from local bans on bags, straws, and microbeads, deposit-return schemes, and extended producer responsibility laws, to industry commitments and clean-up technology. In 2022, UN member states agreed to negotiate a legally binding global treaty on plastic pollution across its full life cycle. Critics note that recycling alone cannot absorb production growing toward a projected tripling by 2060, and argue for caps on virgin plastic production and redesign toward reuse systems.
