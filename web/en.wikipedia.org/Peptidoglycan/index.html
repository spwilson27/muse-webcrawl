Peptidoglycan

CompositionAlternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid cross-linked by short peptides
LocationBacterial cell wall (outside the cytoplasmic membrane)
Also known asMurein
TargetsPenicillin and other β-lactam antibiotics; lysozyme

Peptidoglycan, also known as murein, is a polymer of sugars and amino acids that forms a mesh-like layer outside the plasma membrane of most bacteria, constituting the cell wall. It gives the cell its shape and protects it against osmotic pressure. The structure is a distinctive feature of bacteria and the target of many antibiotics.

Structure

The backbone of peptidoglycan consists of alternating residues of N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) joined by β-(1,4) glycosidic bonds. Attached to each NAM is a short peptide chain of alternating L- and D-amino acids — typically L-alanine, D-glutamic acid, a diamino acid such as meso-diaminopimelic acid or L-lysine, and two D-alanines. Cross-links between these peptide chains on neighbouring strands knit the sheet into a strong, elastic, three-dimensional network.

Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria

In Gram-positive bacteria the peptidoglycan layer is thick (tens of layers) and forms the outermost cell wall, interwoven with teichoic acids. In Gram-negative bacteria the layer is only a few nanometres thick and lies between the inner membrane and an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide. This structural difference explains the Gram stain reaction and influences antibiotic susceptibility.

Biosynthesis and antibiotics

Peptidoglycan is built from a lipid-linked precursor (lipid II) flipped across the membrane and polymerised by penicillin-binding proteins, which perform the final transpeptidation cross-linking step. β-lactam antibiotics such as penicillin mimic the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus and irreversibly inhibit these enzymes, weakening the wall and causing lysis. The enzyme lysozyme, found in tears and saliva, attacks the glycan backbone itself.
