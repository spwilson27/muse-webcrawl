James Wilson Marshall (8 October 1810 – 10 August 1885) was an American carpenter and sawmill operator best known for discovering gold at Sutter's Mill in Coloma, California, on 24 January 1848 — the event that triggered the California Gold Rush.

Early life

Marshall was born in New Jersey and drifted west, living in Indiana, Illinois, Missouri, and Oregon before reaching California in 1845. He settled at Sutter's Fort, served in the Bear Flag Revolt period with Frémont's battalion, and afterwards entered partnership with John Sutter to build a water-powered sawmill on the American River at Coloma.

The discovery

On the morning of 24 January 1848, while inspecting the sawmill's tailrace, Marshall picked up a pea-sized nugget of gold. Tests — including boiling in lye soap and hammering — confirmed it was gold. The discovery was meant to be kept quiet, but word spread; by spring, as Sam Brannan publicised the find in San Francisco, workers deserted the mill and Marshall's enterprise was ruined by the rush it had started. Nine days after the find, California was ceded to the United States by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo — too late for the news to influence the negotiations.

Later life

Marshall never profited from his discovery. His mill failed, claims he staked were overrun, and he was driven from his own land by miners. He worked as a blacksmith, tried viticulture, lectured briefly, and lived largely in poverty at Kelsey, California; the state granted him a small pension in 1872, which was later allowed to lapse. He died in 1885 and was buried on a hill overlooking Coloma, where the Marshall Gold Discovery State Historic Park and a monument mark the site, his statue pointing toward the spot of the discovery.
