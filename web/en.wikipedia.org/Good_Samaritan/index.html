Good Samaritan

The parable of the Good Samaritan is a parable told by Jesus in the Gospel of Luke (10:25–37). In it, a traveller is beaten, robbed and left half dead on the road from Jerusalem to Jericho. A priest and a Levite pass him by, but a Samaritan — a member of a people despised by Jesus's audience — stops to help him. In modern usage, a "Good Samaritan" is anyone who renders aid to a stranger in need.

The parable

Prompted by a lawyer's question, "Who is my neighbour?", Jesus tells how the Samaritan "had compassion" on the wounded man, bandaged his wounds with oil and wine, set him on his own animal, took him to an inn and paid the innkeeper for his care. Jesus concludes: "Go and do likewise." The story redefines neighbourliness in terms of mercy shown across ethnic and religious enmity rather than communal boundaries.

Interpretation

Commentators have read the parable at both literal and allegorical levels. Early church writers such as Augustine interpreted it allegorically, with the Samaritan representing Christ and the inn the Church. Modern readings emphasise its challenge to prejudice and its ethic of practical compassion; the shock of a Samaritan as hero to a Jewish audience is central to its force.

Cultural and legal legacy

The parable has inspired hospitals, charities and humanitarian organisations bearing the Samaritan name. In law, "Good Samaritan laws" in many jurisdictions protect people who voluntarily assist others in emergencies from civil liability, and the term is used in some statutes for protections covering food donors and rescuers.
