Angioedema

Angioedema is the rapid swelling of the deeper layers of the skin and mucous membranes, typically affecting the face, lips, tongue, throat, and sometimes the intestines and genitals. It resembles urticaria (hives) but occurs in deeper tissues and usually does not itch.

Causes

Angioedema may be histamine-mediated — part of an allergic reaction or a side effect of drugs — or bradykinin-mediated. ACE inhibitors, widely used for hypertension, are a common drug cause. Hereditary angioedema results from deficiency or dysfunction of C1 esterase inhibitor, an inherited condition described in the 1880s by Heinrich Quincke and William Osler; attacks can be triggered by stress, trauma, or dental work.

Symptoms and treatment

Swelling develops over minutes to hours and lasts one to several days. Involvement of the tongue or larynx can obstruct the airway and is a medical emergency. Allergic angioedema is treated with antihistamines, corticosteroids, and epinephrine where anaphylaxis is suspected; bradykinin-mediated attacks respond poorly to those drugs and are treated with C1 inhibitor concentrate, icatibant, or ecallantide.
