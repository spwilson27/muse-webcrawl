Morphine

Morphine
Class: opioid analgesic
Source: opium poppy (Papaver somniferum)
Isolated: 1804, by Friedrich Sertürner
Status: WHO essential medicine; controlled substance

Morphine is a strong opiate analgesic found naturally in opium, the dried latex of the opium poppy. It acts directly on the central nervous system to decrease the feeling of pain, and it remains the standard against which other opioids are measured. Morphine is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines, but it also carries a high risk of dependence and overdose, and is strictly controlled internationally.

History

Morphine was first isolated from opium in 1804 by the German pharmacist's assistant Friedrich Sertürner, who named it after Morpheus, the Greek god of dreams. It was the first alkaloid isolated from a plant. Commercial production by Merck began in 1827, and the invention of the hypodermic syringe in the 1850s made it widely used — with widespread addiction following, notably among soldiers in the American Civil War.

Pharmacology and uses

Morphine binds mainly to the μ-opioid receptor, producing analgesia, euphoria, sedation, miosis and respiratory depression. It is used for severe acute and chronic pain, including cancer and end-of-life care, myocardial infarction pain, and acute pulmonary oedema. Codeine is partly converted to morphine in the body, while heroin (diamorphine) is a morphine derivative that crosses the blood–brain barrier faster.

Risks

Adverse effects include nausea, constipation, tolerance, dependence and potentially fatal respiratory depression; overdose is reversed with naloxone.
