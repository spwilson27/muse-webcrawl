Deglobalization

Deglobalization (American spelling: deglobalisation in British English) is the process of diminishing interdependence and integration between countries and economies around the world. It is characterised by declines, or slowdowns in the growth, of international trade, foreign direct investment, cross-border financial flows, migration, and the length and complexity of global supply chains. The term gained prominence after the Great Recession of 2007–2009, when measures such as the ratio of world trade to world GDP stopped rising after decades of increase — a phenomenon also described as "slowbalisation".

Measurement and debate

Whether the world is genuinely deglobalizing is debated. Some indicators, such as trade in goods relative to output, plateaued after 2008, while trade in services and data flows continued to grow rapidly. Analysts distinguish between a genuine reversal of integration and a reconfiguration of it — for example through "friend-shoring", in which supply chains are redirected toward politically allied countries rather than shortened overall. Others argue that deglobalization is primarily political, visible in rising tariffs, industrial subsidies, export controls, and sanctions, rather than in aggregate flow data.

Causes

Proposed causes include the financial crisis of 2008 and the political backlash against its distributional effects; rising inequality and job losses in manufacturing regions of developed countries; the COVID-19 pandemic, which exposed the fragility of long supply chains; geopolitical rivalry, particularly between the United States and China; and wars and sanctions, notably following Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Political movements favouring protectionism, economic nationalism, and greater national self-sufficiency have also been linked to the trend.

Historical precedent

An earlier era of deglobalization is often identified in the period between the First and Second World Wars, when the highly integrated world economy of the late 19th century fragmented under the impact of war, the Great Depression, competitive devaluations, and protectionist measures such as the United States' Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act of 1930.
