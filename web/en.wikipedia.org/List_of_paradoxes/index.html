List of paradoxes

A paradox is a statement or argument that, despite apparently valid reasoning from apparently true premises, leads to a conclusion that seems contradictory, absurd or unacceptable. This list of paradoxes collects the best-known paradoxes from logic, mathematics, physics, philosophy, economics and everyday life. Some are true paradoxes; others are merely counter-intuitive results, or puzzles that expose errors in sloppy reasoning.

Logic and self-reference

  • Liar paradox — "This sentence is false." If true, it is false; if false, it is true.
  • Russell's paradox — The set of all sets that do not contain themselves both must and must not contain itself; it overturned naive set theory.
  • Barber paradox — A barber shaves all and only those men who do not shave themselves: who shaves the barber?
  • Crocodile dilemma — A crocodile promises to return a child if the parent correctly guesses whether it will do so.
  • Curry's paradox — "If this sentence is true, then Germany borders China" appears to prove any arbitrary claim.
  • Berry paradox — "The smallest positive integer not definable in under sixty letters."
  • Richard's paradox — Diagonalisation over all English definitions of real numbers defines a real number not among them.
  • Grelling–Nelson paradox — Is the word "heterological" (not describing itself) heterological?
  • Yablo's paradox — An infinite sequence of sentences, each saying all the following ones are false, with no self-reference.

Mathematics and statistics

  • Zeno's paradoxes — Achilles and the tortoise; the dichotomy paradox; the arrow paradox: motion appears impossible if space and time are infinitely divisible.
  • Sorites paradox (heap paradox) — Removing one grain never destroys a heap, yet enough removals leave no heap.
  • Banach–Tarski paradox — A ball can be split into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two balls identical to the original (using the axiom of choice).
  • Birthday paradox — In a group of just 23 people, the chance that two share a birthday exceeds 50%.
  • Monty Hall problem — Switching doors doubles the chance of winning the car.
  • Simpson's paradox — A trend present in separate groups can reverse when the groups are combined.
  • Gabriel's horn — A solid with finite volume but infinite surface area.
  • Hilbert's paradox of the Grand Hotel — A fully occupied hotel with infinitely many rooms can still accommodate infinitely many new guests.
  • Ross–Littlewood paradox — Adding and removing balls infinitely often leaves the urn, apparently, empty.
  • St. Petersburg paradox — A lottery with infinite expected value that few would pay much to enter.
  • Two envelopes problem — Switching envelopes always seems advantageous.
  • Braess's paradox — Adding a road to a network can make all traffic slower.
  • Benford's law paradoxes / inspection paradox — Your bus always seems to come after a longer-than-average wait.
  • Coastline paradox — The measured length of a coastline grows without bound as the ruler shrinks.

Physics

  • Twin paradox — A travelling twin returns younger than the stay-at-home twin (special relativity).
  • Grandfather paradox — Travelling back in time and preventing one's own existence.
  • Fermi paradox — If extraterrestrial civilisations are likely, "where is everybody?"
  • Schrödinger's cat — A cat in a box is apparently both alive and dead until observed.
  • EPR paradox — Quantum entanglement seems to imply "spooky action at a distance".
  • Maxwell's demon — A being that appears to violate the second law of thermodynamics by sorting molecules.
  • Olbers' paradox — If the universe were infinite and eternal, the night sky would be as bright as the Sun.
  • Heat death paradox — Why has the universe not already reached thermodynamic equilibrium?
  • Wheeler's delayed-choice / quantum eraser paradoxes — Later choices seem to affect earlier events.
  • Black hole information paradox — Does information falling into a black hole get destroyed, violating quantum mechanics?
  • Ladder paradox (pole and barn) — A relativistically contracted ladder both does and does not fit in a barn.
  • Hydrostatic paradox — The pressure at the bottom of a vessel depends only on depth, not on the vessel's shape.

Philosophy

  • Ship of Theseus — If every plank is replaced, is it the same ship?
  • Paradox of hedonism — Pleasure is best achieved by not aiming directly at it.
  • Omnipotence paradox — Can an omnipotent being create a stone it cannot lift?
  • Problem of evil (Epicurean paradox) — The coexistence of an omnipotent, omnibenevolent God and evil.
  • Paradox of free will — Foreknowledge and free choice appear incompatible.
  • Theseus's other puzzle: the paradox of tolerance — Unlimited tolerance can destroy tolerance itself (Karl Popper).
  • Meno's paradox — Inquiry seems impossible: you cannot search for what you do not know, nor recognise it if found.
  • Paradox of the ravens (Hempel's paradox) — Observing a green apple seems to confirm that all ravens are black.
  • Unexpected hanging paradox — A prisoner deduces he cannot be hanged by surprise, yet is.
  • Buridan's ass — An ass exactly between two haystacks starves for lack of a reason to choose.

Economics and decision theory

  • Paradox of thrift — If everyone saves more, aggregate demand and incomes may fall.
  • Paradox of value (diamond–water paradox) — Water is essential but cheap; diamonds are frivolous but expensive.
  • Giffen paradox — A staple good whose demand rises as its price rises.
  • Leontief paradox — The capital-rich United States exported labour-intensive goods.
  • Allais paradox and Ellsberg paradox — Choices that violate expected-utility theory.
  • Condorcet paradox (voting paradox) — Majority preferences can cycle: A beats B, B beats C, C beats A.
  • Arrow's impossibility theorem — No voting system satisfies all reasonable fairness criteria.
  • Prisoner's dilemma — Individually rational choices lead to a collectively worse outcome.
  • Newcomb's paradox — One-box or two-box before a near-perfect predictor?
  • Parrondo's paradox — Two losing games can combine into a winning one.
  • Abilene paradox — A group decides on a course of action none of its members actually wants.
  • Tragedy of the commons — Shared resources are over-exploited by rational individual use.
