Prediction

A prediction (Latin prae-, "before", and dicere, "to say") is a statement about a future event or about data that is not yet observed. Predictions are often, but not always, based upon experience or knowledge of a model of the system concerned. There is no universal agreement about the exact difference between "prediction" and "estimation"; different authors and disciplines ascribe different connotations.

Science

In science, a prediction is a rigorous, often quantitative, statement forecasting what will be observed under specified conditions, derived from a hypothesis or theory. A theory that makes successful novel predictions is corroborated; failed predictions prompt revision or rejection, a theme in Karl Popper's philosophy of falsifiability. Famous examples include the prediction of Neptune from anomalies in the orbit of Uranus, and the bending of light near the Sun predicted by general relativity and observed in 1919.

Statistics and forecasting

Forecasting applies statistical models to time series in weather prediction, economics, epidemiology and business planning. Forecast accuracy can be measured with scoring rules and prediction intervals, and methods range from regression and exponential smoothing to ensemble numerical weather prediction and machine learning. Expert judgement, prediction markets and superforecasting research study how human predictions can be improved and aggregated.
