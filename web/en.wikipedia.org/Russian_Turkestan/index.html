Russian Turkestan

Russian Turkestan (Russian: Русский Туркестан) was the western part of Turkestan within the Russian Empire's Central Asian territories, administered as the Turkestan Governor-Generalship (later Krai) from 1867 to 1917, with its capital at Tashkent. It encompassed much of present-day Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan, southern Kazakhstan, and Turkmenistan's east, while the protectorates of Bukhara and Khiva retained nominal autonomy under their emir and khan.

Conquest

Russian forces advanced into Central Asia from the 1860s, driven by commercial ambitions, the desire to secure cotton supplies during the American Civil War, and rivalry with British India in the "Great Game". Tashkent fell in 1865 under General Mikhail Chernyayev; Samarkand was taken in 1868; Khiva became a protectorate in 1873 and Kokand was annexed in 1876 after an uprising. The Turkmen lands were subdued by General Mikhail Skobelev, culminating in the storming of Geok Tepe in 1881 and the annexation of Merv in 1884.

Administration and society

The first Governor-General, Konstantin von Kaufman (1867–1882), combined military rule with a policy of largely ignoring Islam and internal affairs. Cotton monoculture expanded dramatically, tied to irrigation works and, from the 1880s, the Trans-Caspian Railway linking Samarkand and Tashkent to the Caspian Sea. Russian and Slavic settlement grew in the steppe provinces. Resistance culminated in the Central Asian revolt of 1916, triggered by a decree conscripting native men for labour battalions during the First World War; its suppression killed large numbers of Kyrgyz and Kazakhs. After the Russian Revolution, the region was contested in the Basmachi revolt and eventually divided in the 1920s into the Soviet republics of Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan, and Kazakhstan.
