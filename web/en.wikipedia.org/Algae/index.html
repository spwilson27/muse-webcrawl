Algae (singular alga) is an informal term for a large and diverse group of photosynthetic organisms that are not necessarily closely related, and is thus polyphyletic. Ranging from microscopic single cells to giant kelps tens of metres long, algae live chiefly in water and lack the true roots, stems and leaves of land plants. Together they produce a large share of the world's oxygen and form the base of most aquatic food webs.

Diversity and classification

The algae span several major lineages of eukaryotes, plus the cyanobacteria — photosynthetic bacteria sometimes called "blue-green algae". The principal groups include the green algae (close relatives of land plants), red algae, brown algae (including kelps and other large seaweeds), diatoms with their ornate silica shells, dinoflagellates, golden algae and euglenoids. Estimates of the number of species range from about 30,000 to more than a million, reflecting how many microscopic forms remain undescribed. Algal diversity arose through endosymbiosis, in which ancestral eukaryotes engulfed photosynthetic cyanobacteria, giving rise to chloroplasts.

Forms and habitats

Algae may be unicellular and free-floating (phytoplankton), filamentous, colonial, or large multicellular seaweeds with holdfasts, stipes and blades. They dominate the sunlit waters of oceans, lakes and rivers, and also occur in soil, on bark and rocks, in snow and ice, and in symbiosis — as the photosynthetic partners in lichens and inside corals and other animals.

Ecology

Phytoplankton photosynthesis fixes an estimated half of global carbon dioxide fixation and supplies a comparable share of atmospheric oxygen. Algae feed zooplankton, shellfish and fish, while seaweed beds shelter marine life. Under excess nutrients, algae can proliferate into harmful algal blooms that deplete oxygen or release toxins. Historically, the remains of diatoms formed diatomaceous earth, and ancient algal deposits contributed to petroleum formation.

Human uses

People harvest algae as food — nori, kombu, wakame and dulse among them — and extract agar, alginates and carrageenan for use as thickeners and gelling agents. Algae are farmed for fertiliser, animal feed, nutritional supplements such as spirulina, pigments and cosmetics, and are researched intensively as sources of biofuel and as tools for wastewater treatment and carbon capture.
