Australopithecus

Temporal range: Pliocene – Pleistocene, c. 4.2–1.9 million years ago

Known species: A. afarensis, A. africanus, A. anamensis, A. sediba, and others

First described: Raymond Dart, 1925 (Taung Child)

Australopithecus (from Latin australis, "southern", and Greek pithekos, "ape") is a genus of early hominins that existed in Africa during the Pliocene and Early Pleistocene epochs. Its species were bipedal but retained adaptations for climbing, had brain sizes only modestly larger than chimpanzees, and are widely regarded as close to the ancestry of the genus Homo.

Discovery and species

The genus was named by Raymond Dart in 1925 for the Taung Child, a juvenile skull of Australopithecus africanus from South Africa. Later discoveries included A. afarensis, represented by the famous partial skeleton "Lucy" (found in Ethiopia in 1974) and the Laetoli footprints in Tanzania, which demonstrate habitual upright walking by about 3.6 million years ago. Other proposed species include A. anamensis, A. garhi, A. bahrelghazali, A. deyiremeda and A. sediba, the last proposed as a transitional form toward Homo.

Anatomy and lifestyle

Australopithecines were roughly 1.2–1.5 metres tall with marked sexual dimorphism. Their teeth and jaws suggest a diet of fruit, leaves and, in some species, hard or abrasive foods; robust forms are often placed in the separate genus Paranthropus. Stone tool use is documented from around 3.3 million years ago at Lomekwi, Kenya, possibly predating Homo.
