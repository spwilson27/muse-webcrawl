Neurovascular coupling

Neurovascular coupling (NVC) refers to the relationship between local neural activity and subsequent changes in cerebral blood flow. When a region of the brain becomes active, blood flow to that region increases — a response known as functional hyperaemia — delivering oxygen and glucose to active neurons and removing metabolic waste. This mechanism, first described by Roy and Sherrington in 1890, forms the physiological basis of functional neuroimaging techniques such as BOLD fMRI and functional near-infrared spectroscopy.

Mechanism

Coupling is mediated by the neurovascular unit, comprising neurons, astrocytes, endothelial cells, pericytes and vascular smooth muscle. Synaptic release of glutamate triggers signalling cascades: in neurons, calcium activates neuronal nitric oxide synthase, producing the vasodilator nitric oxide; in astrocytes, calcium elevations lead to release of prostaglandins, epoxyeicosatrienoic acids and potassium ions that relax nearby arterioles and capillaries. Pericytes on capillaries may initiate dilation that propagates upstream to penetrating arterioles. The resulting blood-flow increase typically overshoots local oxygen consumption, decreasing the deoxyhaemoglobin fraction — the effect detected by BOLD contrast.

Clinical significance

Impaired neurovascular coupling is implicated in stroke, Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, small-vessel disease, hypertension and migraine. Breakdown of coupling and of the blood–brain barrier is increasingly seen as an early event in neurodegeneration. Preserving neurovascular function is therefore a target of research into vascular contributions to cognitive impairment.
