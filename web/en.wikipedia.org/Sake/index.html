Sake

Sake (Japanese: 酒, pronounced [sake]), also spelled saké and referred to in Japanese as nihonshu (日本酒, "Japanese liquor"), is an alcoholic beverage made by fermenting polished rice. Though often called "rice wine," its production resembles brewing beer: starch in the rice must first be converted to sugars, a task performed by the mould Aspergillus oryzae (kōji), after which yeast ferments the sugars to alcohol. Sake typically contains about 14–16% alcohol by volume and ranges in flavour from dry and crisp to rich and sweet. It is Japan's national beverage, with roots reaching back more than a thousand years, and in 2024 UNESCO added traditional sake-making with kōji to its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage.

Production

Sake brewing begins with special sake rice, milled ("polished") to remove a portion of the outer grain — the polishing ratio is a primary marker of grade, with premium ginjō and daiginjō sakes milled to 60% or 50% of the original grain or less. After washing, soaking, and steaming, part of the rice is inoculated with kōji mould to produce the sugars; a yeast starter (shubo) is prepared, and the main mash (moromi) is built up in three additions over four days. Fermentation proceeds for roughly a month, after which the sake is pressed, filtered, pasteurised, and aged — usually around six months — before blending and bottling. The master brewer (tōji) directs the process, traditionally in winter.

Grades and styles

Japanese liquor law defines premium categories by polishing ratio and whether a small amount of distilled alcohol is added (honjōzō styles) or not (junmai, "pure rice", styles). Common designations include junmai, honjōzō, ginjō, and daiginjō, alongside specialty types such as cloudy nigori, unpasteurised namazake, sparkling sake, and aged koshu. Flavour descriptors range from karakuchi (dry) to amakuchi (sweet), measured by the Sake Meter Value.

History and culture

Rice fermentation in Japan is documented from the early medieval period, when sake was brewed at the imperial court and in temples and shrines; it remains central to Shinto ritual, weddings, and New Year celebrations. Major brewing regions include Nada in Kobe, Fushimi in Kyoto, and Niigata and Hiroshima prefectures, each known for distinctive water and style. In recent decades consumption in Japan has declined while exports and international interest have grown, with sake breweries now operating in North America, Europe, and elsewhere.
