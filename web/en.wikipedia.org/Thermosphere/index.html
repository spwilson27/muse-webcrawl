Thermosphere

Altitude~80–90 km to 500–1,000 km
TemperatureUp to ~2,500 °C (highly variable)

The thermosphere is the layer of Earth's atmosphere directly above the mesosphere and below the exosphere. Within this layer of the atmosphere, ultraviolet radiation causes photoionization and photodissociation of molecules, creating ions; the thermosphere thus constitutes the larger part of the ionosphere. Taking its name from the Greek thermos (θερμός) meaning heat, the thermosphere begins at about 80 km (50 mi) above sea level. At these high altitudes, the residual atmospheric gases sort into strata according to molecular mass.

Temperatures in the thermosphere increase with altitude and can rise to around 2,500 °C (4,530 °F) or higher during periods of high solar activity. Despite these high measured temperatures, the gas is so tenuous that an ordinary thermometer would register well below freezing, because temperature here reflects the average kinetic energy of very few molecules and heat transfer by conduction is negligible.

Dynamics

The thermosphere is strongly influenced by the solar cycle: extreme ultraviolet and X-ray radiation heat and expand the layer, increasing atmospheric drag on satellites in low Earth orbit. The International Space Station orbits within the thermosphere, at an altitude of roughly 330–420 km, and requires periodic reboosts to counteract drag. The layer also hosts auroras, produced when charged particles excite atmospheric gases, and much of it overlaps the ionosphere, whose ionised regions reflect radio waves and enable long-distance shortwave communication.

Boundaries

The lower boundary, the mesopause, lies near 80–90 km, close to the Kármán line (100 km) conventionally taken as the boundary of space. The upper boundary, the thermopause, varies between about 500 and 1,000 km depending on solar activity, above which the exosphere extends outward and gradually merges with interplanetary space.
