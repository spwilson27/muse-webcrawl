Hall–Héroult process

The Hall–Héroult process is the major industrial process for smelting aluminium. It involves dissolving aluminium oxide (alumina) in molten cryolite and electrolysing the solution to obtain pure aluminium metal. The process was independently invented in 1886 by American chemist Charles Martin Hall and French engineer Paul Héroult, both aged 23, and it transformed aluminium from a precious metal into a cheap, ubiquitous material.

Description

Alumina (Al₂O₃) is dissolved in a bath of molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) with calcium fluoride and aluminium fluoride additives, which lowers the melting point from alumina's 2,072 °C to about 940–980 °C. Electric current passes between consumable carbon anodes and a carbon-lined cathode: aluminium ions are reduced to liquid metal that pools at the cell bottom and is siphoned off, while oxygen reacts with the anodes to form carbon dioxide. Modern cells operate at 300,000–600,000 amperes.

Energy and environmental issues

The process is extremely energy intensive, consuming roughly 13–15 megawatt-hours of electricity per tonne of aluminium, so smelters are typically sited near cheap hydroelectric or geothermal power. Anode consumption generates large quantities of carbon dioxide, and under fault conditions ("anode effects") the process can emit perfluorocarbons, potent greenhouse gases. Recycling aluminium, by contrast, uses only about five percent of the energy of primary production.
