Measles vaccine

Measles vaccine protects against becoming infected with measles. Nearly all those who do not develop immunity after a single dose develop it after a second dose. When the rate of vaccination within a population is greater than 92%, outbreaks of measles typically no longer occur; however, they may occur again if the rate of vaccination decreases. The vaccine's effectiveness lasts many years. It is unclear if it becomes less effective over time. The vaccine may also protect against measles if given within a couple of days of exposure.

The measles vaccine is commonly given as part of the combined MMR vaccine.

Use

The vaccine is generally safe, including for those with HIV infections. Most children do not experience any side effects; those that occur are usually mild, such as fever, rash, or pain at the site of injection. The measles vaccine is available both by itself and in combinations such as the MMR vaccine (against measles, mumps, and rubella) and the MMRV vaccine (which adds varicella/chickenpox).

The World Health Organization recommends two doses for all children: the first at 12 months of age in most countries (9 months where measles is common) and the second at 15–18 months or at 4–6 years, depending on the national schedule.

History

John Enders and Thomas C. Peebles isolated the measles virus in 1954. Enders and his team developed the Edmonston-B strain vaccine, licensed in the United States in 1963. Maurice Hilleman further attenuated the strain to produce the Moraten strain used in most vaccines today. Before widespread vaccination, measles infected nearly every child; vaccination reduced measles deaths worldwide by roughly 80% between 2000 and 2017.

Effectiveness

One dose of the MMR vaccine is about 93% effective at preventing measles; two doses are about 97% effective. Measles vaccination averted an estimated 57 million deaths between 2000 and 2021, according to the World Health Organization.
