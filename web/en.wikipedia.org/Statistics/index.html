Statistics

The normal distribution is a central object in statistical theory.

Statistics is the discipline that concerns the collection, organization, analysis, interpretation, and presentation of data. In applying statistics to a scientific, industrial, or social problem, it is conventional to begin with a statistical population or a process to be studied. Statistics deals with every aspect of data, including the planning of its collection in terms of surveys and experiments.

Descriptive and inferential statistics

Descriptive statistics summarise a sample using measures of central tendency such as the mean, median, and mode, and measures of dispersion such as the standard deviation and interquartile range. Inferential statistics draw conclusions about a population from a sample, using hypothesis tests, confidence intervals, and regression analysis, all grounded in probability theory.

History

The discipline traces roots to demography and probability in the 17th century — the work of John Graunt on mortality tables, and of Pascal and Fermat on games of chance. In the 19th century, Quetelet, Galton, and Pearson developed correlation and regression; in the 20th, R. A. Fisher systematised experimental design, maximum likelihood, and the analysis of variance, while Neyman and Pearson formalised hypothesis testing. Bayesian statistics, descended from Bayes' theorem, grew rapidly with modern computing.

Methods

Key methods include sampling theory, experimental design, linear and generalised linear models, time-series analysis, survival analysis, non-parametric methods, and multivariate analysis. Statistical practice today is inseparable from computing, with languages such as R and Python dominating data analysis, and with machine learning and data science overlapping heavily with classical statistics.

Criticism and misuse

Statistics can mislead when samples are biased, when many hypotheses are tested without correction (p-hacking), or when correlation is presented as causation. The replication crisis in psychology and medicine has driven reforms favouring pre-registration, effect sizes, and confidence intervals over bare significance thresholds.
