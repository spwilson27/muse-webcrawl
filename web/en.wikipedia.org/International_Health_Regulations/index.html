International Health Regulations

The International Health Regulations (IHR) are an instrument of international law that provide a global legal framework for preventing, protecting against, controlling and responding to the international spread of disease. They are binding on 196 countries, including all member states of the World Health Organization (WHO). The current version, the IHR (2005), was adopted by the World Health Assembly on 23 May 2005 and entered into force on 15 June 2007; amendments adopted in 2024 followed the COVID-19 pandemic.

Provisions

Under the IHR (2005), states must develop core capacities for surveillance and response and must notify the WHO of events that may constitute a public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC), assessed using the decision instrument in Annex 2 of the Regulations. Certain diseases, including smallpox, poliomyelitis due to wild-type poliovirus, human influenza caused by a new subtype, and severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), are always notifiable. The WHO Director-General may declare a PHEIC after consulting an Emergency Committee; declarations have been made for H1N1 influenza (2009), poliomyelitis (2014), Ebola in West Africa (2014) and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (2019), Zika virus (2016), COVID-19 (2020) and mpox (2022 and 2024).

The Regulations also govern health measures applied to travellers, ships and aircraft, including the ship sanitation certificates and the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis (the "yellow card") used for yellow fever vaccination.

History

The IHR descend from the international sanitary conferences of the nineteenth century, beginning in Paris in 1851, which sought to coordinate quarantine measures against cholera and plague. The WHO adopted the International Sanitary Regulations in 1951; these were renamed the International Health Regulations in 1969 and originally covered cholera, plague, yellow fever and smallpox. The narrow disease list and poor compliance led to the comprehensive 2005 revision, prompted in part by the 2003 SARS outbreak.
