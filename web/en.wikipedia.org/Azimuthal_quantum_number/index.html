Azimuthal quantum number


Atomic orbitals for ℓ = 0 (s), 1 (p), 2 (d) and 3 (f).

The azimuthal quantum number is a quantum number for an atomic orbital that determines its orbital angular momentum and describes the shape of the orbital. It is usually denoted by the letter ℓ (a script l) and is also known as the angular momentum quantum number, the orbital quantum number or the second quantum number. Together with the principal quantum number n, the magnetic quantum number mℓ and the spin quantum number ms, it forms the set of quantum numbers that uniquely describe the quantum state of an electron in an atom.

Allowed values

For a given principal quantum number n, the azimuthal quantum number can take the integer values ℓ = 0, 1, 2, …, n − 1. Each value of ℓ defines an electron subshell with a characteristic shape. By long-standing convention, subshells are labelled with letters: ℓ = 0 is an s subshell, ℓ = 1 a p subshell, ℓ = 2 a d subshell and ℓ = 3 an f subshell, after which the naming continues alphabetically (g, h, …) though such subshells are not occupied in the ground states of known elements. The letters s, p, d and f derive from the words once used to describe spectral lines: sharp, principal, diffuse and fundamental.

A subshell with azimuthal quantum number ℓ contains 2ℓ + 1 orbitals, distinguished by the magnetic quantum number, and can therefore hold up to 2(2ℓ + 1) electrons: 2 in an s subshell, 6 in p, 10 in d and 14 in f.

Angular momentum

The magnitude of the electron's orbital angular momentum is determined by ℓ through the relation |L| = √[ℓ(ℓ + 1)] ħ, where ħ is the reduced Planck constant. The azimuthal quantum number thus measures orbital angular momentum in units of ħ; an electron in an s orbital (ℓ = 0) has no orbital angular momentum, while electrons in p, d and f orbitals have progressively more.

History

The azimuthal quantum number arose in Arnold Sommerfeld's relativistic extension of the Bohr model (the Bohr–Sommerfeld model), in which electrons moved in elliptical orbits and ℓ (then usually called k) described the orbit's eccentricity and angular momentum. In modern quantum mechanics, derived from the Schrödinger equation for the hydrogen atom, ℓ emerges naturally from the angular part of the wavefunction, expressed in spherical harmonics, and it determines both the shape of the orbital and the number of angular nodes, which equals ℓ.

Chemical significance

Because the shape and energy of an atom's outer orbitals govern its chemical behaviour, the azimuthal quantum number underlies the structure of the periodic table: elements are grouped into s-block, p-block, d-block (transition metals) and f-block (lanthanides and actinides) according to the subshell being filled. The splitting of subshell energies by ℓ is also responsible for the order in which orbitals fill, described by the Aufbau principle and rules such as the n + ℓ (Madelung) rule.
