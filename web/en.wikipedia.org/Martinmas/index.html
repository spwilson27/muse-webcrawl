Martinmas, also known as St. Martin's Day, is the feast day of Saint Martin of Tours, celebrated on 11 November each year. In the Middle Ages it was one of the most important festivals of the year, marking the end of the agrarian season, the slaughtering of livestock for winter, the tasting of the new wine, and a traditional date for settling accounts, paying rents, and hiring servants.

History

Martin of Tours (c. 316–397), a former Roman soldier who became a monk and bishop, was buried on 11 November 397. His feast spread widely through western Europe. Martinmas fell at the boundary between autumn and winter and functioned as a kind of harvest thanksgiving and New Year in folk tradition; the period of mild weather sometimes occurring around the feast is proverbially called "St. Martin's summer".

Customs

In Germany and the Netherlands, children take part in lantern processions (Martinszug) on the evening of the feast, singing songs about St. Martin and receiving sweets, often led by a figure dressed as the saint on horseback. Roast goose ("Martinsgans") is the traditional meal, recalling the legend that Martin hid among geese to avoid becoming bishop. In wine regions, Martinmas is when the new vintage is first drunk. In Scotland, Martinmas was one of the four traditional quarter days for legal and academic terms, and it coincides with Armistice Day commemorations in many Commonwealth countries held on the same date.
