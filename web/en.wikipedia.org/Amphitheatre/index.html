Amphitheatre

An amphitheatre (also spelled amphitheater) is an open-air venue for entertainment and spectacles, consisting of tiered seating arranged around a central performance space. The term derives from the Greek amphitheatron — "theatre on both sides" — distinguishing the fully enclosed, typically oval or circular Roman building from the semicircular Greek theatre. Roman amphitheatres hosted gladiatorial contests, wild-beast hunts (venationes), public executions, and mock sea battles (naumachiae). The largest and most famous is the Colosseum in Rome, dedicated in 80 AD, which could hold some 50,000–80,000 spectators.

Structure

A Roman amphitheatre consists of an elliptical arena — the name means "sand," spread to absorb blood — ringed by a high podium wall and rising tiers of seats (the cavea), divided by rank: senators and the emperor nearest the action, the general populace at the top. Beneath the arena floor of larger venues lay a hypogeum of cages, lifts, and corridors for animals, gladiators, and scenery. Retractable awnings (velarium), rigged by sailors, shaded the audience. Radial staircases and numbered archways (vomitoria) allowed tens of thousands to enter and disperse quickly — a system echoed in modern stadium design.

History

Gladiatorial games began as Campanian funeral rites; the earliest surviving stone amphitheatre, at Pompeii (c. 70 BC), preceded Rome's first permanent example, that of Statilius Taurus (29 BC). More than 230 amphitheatres are known across the Roman Empire, from the Colosseum and the Arena of Verona to El Jem in Tunisia and Arles and Nîmes in Gaul — the latter two used today for concerts and bullfights. Games dwindled with Christianization and imperial bans in the 5th century. In the modern era "amphitheatre" also denotes natural bowl-shaped landforms and semicircular lecture halls, and outdoor music venues (such as Red Rocks Amphitheatre in Colorado) revived the name.
