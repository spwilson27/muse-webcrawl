Lost Generation

The Lost Generation was the social generational cohort that came of age during World War I. The term is also used more specifically for the expatriate writers and artists who lived in Paris during the 1920s. Gertrude Stein is credited with coining the phrase, which Ernest Hemingway popularised in the epigraph to his 1926 novel The Sun Also Rises: "You are all a lost generation."

According to Hemingway, Stein had heard the phrase une génération perdue from a French garage owner who had used it of young men who had served in the war and were difficult to repair — like a car that could not be fixed. The label captured a sense of disillusionment, aimlessness and spiritual loss among young people whose youth had been consumed by the industrialised slaughter of the Great War.

The writers

The Paris circle included Hemingway, F. Scott Fitzgerald, Gertrude Stein, T. S. Eliot (in London), Ezra Pound, James Joyce (from a slightly older generation), John Dos Passos, E. E. Cummings, Sylvia Beach of the Shakespeare and Company bookshop, and the publisher and patron circles around them. Their work — experimental in form, spare in style, and often preoccupied with war, alcohol, expatriation and broken ideals — defined much of literary modernism.

The demographic sense

In the broader demographic usage, the Lost Generation comprises people born roughly between 1883 and 1900, who reached adulthood during or just after the First World War. The cohort experienced the influenza pandemic of 1918, Prohibition in the United States, the Roaring Twenties and then the Great Depression. Later generations included the Greatest Generation, the Silent Generation and the Baby Boomers.
