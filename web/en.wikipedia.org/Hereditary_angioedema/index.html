Hereditary angioedema

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare genetic disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of severe swelling (angioedema) affecting the face, extremities, gastrointestinal tract, and airways. The condition is most commonly caused by a deficiency or dysfunction of C1 esterase inhibitor (C1-INH), a protein that regulates the complement and contact systems. Without adequate C1-INH control, excessive bradykinin is produced, increasing vascular permeability and causing fluid to leak into surrounding tissues.

Hereditary angioedema
Specialty: Immunology, hematology
Inheritance: Autosomal dominant
Gene: SERPING1 (types I and II)

Signs and symptoms

Attacks typically develop over several hours and resolve within two to five days without treatment. Swelling of the bowel wall produces severe abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, while laryngeal edema can obstruct the airway and is potentially fatal. Unlike allergic angioedema, HAE swelling is not accompanied by hives and does not respond to antihistamines, corticosteroids, or epinephrine.

Types

Type I HAE involves low levels of C1-INH; type II involves normal levels of a dysfunctional protein. A third form, HAE with normal C1-INH, is associated with mutations in genes such as F12 (factor XII), PLG (plasminogen), and ANGPT1.

Treatment

On-demand treatments include plasma-derived or recombinant C1-INH concentrate, the bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist icatibant, and the kallikrein inhibitor ecallantide. Long-term prophylaxis options include lanadelumab, berotralstat, and subcutaneous C1-INH. See also angioedema and complement system.
