Local access and transport area

A local access and transport area (LATA) is a term used in U.S. telecommunications to define a geographical area, generally centred on a city or other identifiable community of interest, in which a local telephone company may offer services. The LATA system was created as part of the 1984 breakup of AT&T, under the Modification of Final Judgment.

History

After divestiture, the United States was divided into 161 LATAs, later growing to nearly 200. The Regional Bell Operating Companies were permitted to provide telephone service within a LATA (intraLATA traffic), while long-distance (interLATA) calls had to be carried by interexchange carriers such as AT&T, MCI, or Sprint unless competition rules allowed otherwise.

Structure

Each LATA is identified by a three-digit code. LATAs do not necessarily correspond to area codes or state boundaries; some LATAs cross state lines, and large metropolitan areas may be served by several area codes within a single LATA.

Modern relevance

Subsequent legislation, notably the Telecommunications Act of 1996, gradually relaxed the restrictions associated with LATAs, but the term remains in use in carrier regulation, tariffs, and network planning.
