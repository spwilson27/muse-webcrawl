Paul Cézanne (19 January 1839 – 22 October 1906) was a French Post-Impressionist painter whose work introduced new modes of representation and influenced avant-garde artistic movements of the early 20th century. He is often regarded as the bridge between Impressionism and Cubism; both Matisse and Picasso are said to have called him "the father of us all".

Life and work

Born in Aix-en-Provence, Cézanne studied law at his father's insistence before devoting himself to painting, moving between Paris and Provence. A childhood friend of Émile Zola, he exhibited with the Impressionists in 1874 and 1877 but mostly worked in isolation in the south of France, methodically exploring still life, portraits, bathers, and the Mont Sainte-Victoire, which he painted more than 80 times. His mature style builds form from modulated patches of colour, treating nature "by the cylinder, the sphere, the cone".

Legacy

Recognition came late: Ambroise Vollard gave him a solo exhibition in 1895, and the retrospective at the Salon d'Automne of 1907, a year after his death from pneumonia, profoundly influenced younger artists. His series paintings — The Card Players and the Large Bathers — are among the most celebrated works in modern art.
