Amateur radio call signs

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Amateur radio call signs are unique identifiers for the 3 million amateur radio operators around the world. Call signs are regulated internationally by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) as well as nationally by individual governments, and each call sign is assigned to a specific operator or station, serving as a legal identification during transmissions.

Structure

An amateur radio call sign consists of a prefix, which identifies the country (and sometimes a subdivision within it), and a suffix, which distinguishes the individual licensee. Under the ITU Radio Regulations, call signs in the international series are formed from an internationally allocated prefix followed by a numeral and a suffix of one to four characters. For example, the United States uses prefixes beginning with A, K, N, and W; an American call sign such as W1AW is the well-known call of the ARRL headquarters station.

Allocation

National administrations allocate call signs within their assigned blocks. In many countries, newly licensed operators are assigned call signs in sequence, while "vanity" programs allow licensees to request specific available call signs. Supplementary identification signs, such as portable or mobile indicators (historically appended as a stroke, e.g. /P or /M), may be added under national rules.

Special event and club call signs

Short or distinctive call signs are often issued temporarily for contests, expeditions, and special events. Club stations receive their own call signs held by a trustee. DXpeditions to rare locations use call signs allocated by the host administration and are highly sought after by operators collecting confirmations (QSL cards) from different "entities" on the DXCC list.
