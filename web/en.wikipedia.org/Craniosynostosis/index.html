Craniosynostosis

Craniosynostosis is a condition in which one or more of the fibrous sutures in a young infant's skull prematurely fuses by turning into bone (ossification), thereby changing the growth pattern of the skull. Because the skull cannot expand perpendicular to the fused suture, it compensates by growing parallel to the closed suture, producing a characteristic abnormal head shape and, in some cases, raised intracranial pressure. Craniosynostosis occurs in about 1 in 2,000 to 2,500 live births.

Types and presentation

The head shape depends on which suture fuses:

  • Sagittal synostosis (the most common) — a long, narrow head (scaphocephaly);
  • Coronal synostosis — unilateral fusion causes frontal plagiocephaly with forehead and brow asymmetry; bilateral fusion causes brachycephaly;
  • Metopic synostosis — a triangular forehead (trigonocephaly);
  • Lambdoid synostosis (rare) — flattening at the back of the head on one side.

Most cases are isolated (non-syndromic). Syndromic craniosynostosis, involving multiple sutures, occurs in conditions such as Crouzon syndrome, Apert syndrome, Pfeiffer syndrome, and Muenke syndrome, often caused by mutations in fibroblast growth factor receptor genes (FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3) or TWIST1.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on physical examination of head shape and palpation of the sutures, confirmed by skull X-ray or CT imaging with three-dimensional reconstruction. Treatment is surgical, ideally in the first year of life, and options include endoscopic strip craniectomy followed by helmet therapy in young infants, and open cranial vault remodelling for older infants or complex cases. Surgery aims to normalise head shape, allow normal brain growth, and prevent raised intracranial pressure; children are typically followed by multidisciplinary craniofacial teams.
