The Siege of Constantinople in April 1204 was the culmination of the Fourth Crusade. The Crusaders and their Venetian allies, diverted from their original goal of Egypt and the Holy Land, assaulted and captured the capital of the Byzantine Empire, subjecting it to a notorious three-day sack.

Background

An earlier attack in July 1203 had placed the pretender Alexios IV on the throne alongside his father Isaac II. When Alexios proved unable to pay the Crusaders the sums he had promised, and was himself overthrown and murdered by Alexios V Doukas in February 1204, the Crusade's leaders resolved to conquer the city for themselves, agreeing in March to partition the empire.

The assault

A first assault on 9 April failed, but on 12 April a favourable north wind drove Venetian ships close to the sea walls along the Golden Horn; soldiers crossed from ship-mounted "flying bridges" to the towers, opened a gate, and the city fell. Emperor Alexios V fled during the night.

The sack

For three days the Crusaders looted Constantinople, destroying or carrying off countless works of art and relics — including the bronze Horses of St Mark, taken to Venice — desecrating churches, and killing many inhabitants. Contemporary chroniclers such as Niketas Choniates lamented the destruction as worse than the deeds of the empire's enemies.

Aftermath

The empire was partitioned under the Partitio Romaniae: Baldwin of Flanders became emperor of the new Latin Empire, Venice gained extensive maritime possessions, and Byzantine successor states formed at Nicaea, Epirus, and Trebizond. Constantinople was recovered by Michael VIII Palaiologos in 1261, but the sack permanently embittered relations between Greek and Latin Christians.
