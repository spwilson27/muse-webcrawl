Sonata form

Sonata form (also sonata-allegro form or first movement form) is a musical structure generally consisting of three main sections: an exposition, a development, and a recapitulation. It has been used widely since the middle of the 18th century, especially in the first movements of sonatas, symphonies, concertos, and string quartets.

Structure

In the exposition, the main thematic material is presented, typically with a primary theme in the tonic key and a secondary theme in a contrasting key — the dominant in major-key movements, or the relative major in minor-key movements. A closing section confirms the new key. The exposition is conventionally repeated.

The development then reworks the thematic material through fragmentation, sequence, and modulation to remote keys, creating instability that is resolved by the return of the tonic. The recapitulation restates the exposition's material, but with the secondary theme now in the tonic, so that the movement's tonal conflict is resolved. A coda may follow.

History

Sonata form developed during the Classical period from earlier binary forms. Composers such as Joseph Haydn, Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, and Ludwig van Beethoven brought it to maturity. Beethoven expanded the development section and the coda, and Romantic composers continued to adapt the form, sometimes fusing it with other structures. Music theorists such as Heinrich Schenker, and more recently James Hepokoski and Warren Darcy, have offered influential accounts of the form's norms and deformations.
