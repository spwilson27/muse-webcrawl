Rider–Waite tarot deck

The Rider–Waite tarot deck (also known as the Waite–Smith, Rider–Waite–Smith, or Rider Tarot deck) is a widely popular deck for tarot card reading, first published by the Rider Company in 1909. The cards were drawn by artist Pamela Colman Smith under the direction of the mystic and occultist Arthur Edward Waite, drawing on the esoteric teachings of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. Waite's deck was the first mass-produced tarot to illustrate the pip cards of the Minor Arcana with full figurative scenes — a departure from earlier decks, in which only the trumps and court cards bore pictures.

The deck, together with Waite's guide The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (1910/1911), became the most influential tarot in the English-speaking world and the model for countless later decks and for the iconography of modern cartomancy.

Creation

Arthur Edward Waite (1857–1942), a scholar of Western esotericism and member of the Golden Dawn, commissioned the deck in 1909, engaging Pamela Colman Smith (1878–1951), a fellow Golden Dawn member and a successful artist and theatre designer, to illustrate it. Smith completed the 78 paintings in about six months (April–October 1909), apparently inspired in part by the 15th-century Italian Sola Busca tarot — the earliest known deck with illustrated pip cards, photographs of which she may have seen at the British Museum. The deck appeared in December 1909 from William Rider & Son of London, in a first edition known to collectors by its "crackle" card backs and a variant with roses-and-lilies backs.

Design and symbolism

Waite reorganized the traditional trumps according to Golden Dawn attributions: he swapped the numbers of Strength and Justice (making Strength VIII and Justice XI) to fit astrological correspondences, and renamed or reworked several cards (the Pope became the Hierophant, the Papess the High Priestess). The imagery embeds Kabbalistic, astrological, and alchemical symbolism amid outwardly medieval scenes. Smith's art — bold outlines, flat color, and stage-like compositions — gives the deck its distinctive accessibility, and her scenic Minor Arcana translate the suit emblems into narrative tableaux (for example, the Ten of Swords showing a figure lying face down pierced by ten swords beneath a black sky).

Major Arcana

The 22 trumps run from 0 The Fool to XXI The World, including The Magician, The High Priestess, The Empress, The Emperor, The Hierophant, The Lovers, The Chariot, Strength, The Hermit, Wheel of Fortune, Justice, The Hanged Man, Death, Temperance, The Devil, The Tower, The Star, The Moon, The Sun, Judgement, and The World. Several images — The Fool stepping toward a cliff edge, The Hanged Man suspended serenely by one foot — became canonical representations used far beyond tarot practice.

Minor Arcana

The 56 Minor Arcana comprise four suits — Wands (clubs/batons), Cups, Swords, and Pentacles (coins) — each with ten pip cards and four court cards: Page, Knight, Queen, and King. Waite associated the suits with the elements fire, water, air, and earth respectively, and Smith's scenes supplied the meanings that later "little white books" and most modern tarot instruction still teach.

Publication history and editions

U.S. Games Systems acquired publishing rights and has issued the deck in many editions since 1971, including the standard Rider–Waite, the Smith–Waite Centennial edition (2009, issued in the Pamela Colman Smith Commemorative Set), and recolorings. Because of the dates of publication and the authors' deaths, the deck's copyright status varies by jurisdiction; it is public domain in many countries, and in 2021 the images entered the public domain in the United Kingdom as well (70 years after Smith's death). Modern scholarship and publishers increasingly credit Smith as co-creator, using the name "Rider–Waite–Smith."
