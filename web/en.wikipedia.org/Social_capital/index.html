Social capital

FieldSociology, political science, economics
Key figuresPierre Bourdieu, James Coleman, Robert Putnam
FormsBonding, bridging and linking capital

Social capital refers to the networks of relationships among people who live and work in a particular society, enabling that society to function effectively. It encompasses the trust, norms of reciprocity and shared understandings that arise from social connections, and it is treated as a resource analogous to physical and human capital that can facilitate collective action and individual opportunity.

Major theorists

Pierre Bourdieu defined social capital as the aggregate of actual or potential resources linked to possessing a durable network of institutionalised relationships, emphasising its role in reproducing class advantage. James Coleman treated it as the aspects of social structure that facilitate action, including obligations, expectations and information channels. Robert Putnam popularised the concept in Bowling Alone (2000), documenting declining civic engagement in the United States and distinguishing bonding capital (within groups) from bridging capital (across groups).

Effects and criticism

High social capital is associated with lower crime, better health, more effective government and economic development; organisations such as the World Bank have promoted it in development policy. Critics note definitional vagueness, difficulties of measurement, and a "dark side": tight networks can also exclude outsiders, enforce conformity, or sustain organised crime and corruption.
