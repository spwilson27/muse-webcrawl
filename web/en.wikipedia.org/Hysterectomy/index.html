Hysterectomy
The surgical removal of the uterus; may also involve removal of the cervix, ovaries, fallopian tubes and surrounding structures.

A hysterectomy is the surgical removal of the uterus and thus the termination of pregnancy and the capability thereof. It may also involve removal of the cervix, ovaries (oophorectomy), fallopian tubes (salpingectomy) and other surrounding structures. Hysterectomy is one of the most commonly performed major operations on women worldwide.

Types

In a total hysterectomy the uterus and cervix are removed; in a subtotal (partial) hysterectomy the cervix is retained; a radical hysterectomy, performed for some cancers, also removes surrounding tissues and the upper vagina. Surgery may be abdominal, vaginal, laparoscopic or robot-assisted, with minimally invasive approaches offering faster recovery. Removal of both ovaries before natural menopause induces surgical menopause, with consequences for hormone production normally regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis.

Indications

Common indications include uterine fibroids causing pain or heavy bleeding, endometriosis, adenomyosis, uterine prolapse, abnormal uterine bleeding unresponsive to other treatment, and gynaecological cancers of the uterus, cervix or ovaries. Emergency hysterectomy is occasionally required for uncontrolled postpartum haemorrhage — a cause of catastrophic hypovolemia.

History and rates

The first successful planned abdominal hysterectomies date to the 19th century; mortality was initially very high but fell with anaesthesia and antisepsis. Rates vary widely between countries and have declined in many places as uterus-sparing alternatives — hormonal therapies, endometrial ablation and uterine artery embolisation — have become available.
