The Green Mountains are a mountain range in the US state of Vermont, running north–south for about 400 kilometres from the Massachusetts border to the Quebec border. They form part of the northern Appalachian Mountains and gave Vermont its very name — from the French Verd Mont, "green mountain".

Geography

The range is generally 30 to 60 kilometres wide. Its highest summit is Mount Mansfield (1,339 m), followed by Killington Peak (1,291 m) and Mount Ellen. The principal chain forms the spine of Vermont, with the Long Trail — the oldest long-distance hiking trail in the United States, built between 1910 and 1930 — running its entire length; the Appalachian Trail coincides with it in the south.

Geologically, the Green Mountains are ancient metamorphic rocks thrust up during the Taconic and Acadian orogenies more than 400 million years ago, then eroded and finally sculpted by Pleistocene glaciers into narrow ridges and rounded summits.

Ecology and economy

Dense northern hardwood and spruce–fir forest clothe the range — nearly all of Vermont was logged in the nineteenth century, and today's forest is second growth little over a century old. Much of the range lies within the Green Mountain National Forest, established in 1932. Ski resorts such as Stowe, Sugarbush, Killington and Stratton make the range a major centre of winter tourism in the Northeast, while fall foliage attracts visitors each autumn.

History

The Green Mountains gave their name to the Green Mountain Boys, the militia led by Ethan Allen during the land disputes of the 1770s and the American Revolution, and to the independent Republic of Vermont (1777–1791). Samuel de Champlain is credited with naming them in 1647, reportedly exclaiming at the verdant hills across the lake that bears his name.
