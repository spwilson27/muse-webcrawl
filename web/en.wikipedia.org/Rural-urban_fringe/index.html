Rural–urban fringe

The rural–urban fringe, also known as the peri-urban area, the outskirts, or the urban hinterland, is the land use interface between town and countryside, or — in simpler terms — the area surrounding a city where urban and rural land uses intermix. It is the transition zone where urban and rural uses meet, often characterised by fragmented development and competing demands on land.

Characteristics

Land uses typical of the fringe include out-of-town retail parks, distribution warehouses, garden centres, golf courses, sewage treatment works, airports, park-and-ride sites, smallholdings and commuter housing estates. Agriculture continues but is influenced by proximity to the urban market — through farm shops, horse grazing ("horsiculture") and diversification — while land values reflect speculation about future development.

Planning issues

The fringe is a focus of planning policy because uncontrolled sprawl can erode countryside and increase car dependence. In the United Kingdom, green belts were designated around major cities from the mid-20th century specifically to contain the fringe and prevent urban sprawl. Similar tools elsewhere include urban growth boundaries and agricultural zoning.

Related concepts

The fringe forms part of the classic concentric and sector models of urban structure and shades into the wider commuter hinterland or metropolitan area. Geographers distinguish the inner fringe, where urban uses dominate, from the outer fringe, where farmland predominates but urban influences are strong.
