Catalysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Catalysis
EffectIncreases reaction rate by lowering activation energy
TypesHomogeneous, heterogeneous, biocatalysis
Term coinedJöns Jacob Berzelius, 1835

Catalysis is the increase in the rate of a chemical reaction by a catalyst — a substance that participates in the reaction but is regenerated at the end, so it is not consumed overall. Catalysts work by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. Because a catalyst speeds up both the forward and reverse reactions equally, it does not change the overall thermodynamics or equilibrium of a reaction, only how quickly equilibrium is reached.

Types

Homogeneous catalysis

The catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants, typically dissolved in the same solution. Examples include acid–base catalysis and many transition-metal complex catalysed reactions used in fine chemical synthesis.

Heterogeneous catalysis

The catalyst is in a different phase, usually a solid surface on which gases or liquids react. Industrially dominant examples include the iron catalyst of the Haber process for ammonia synthesis, platinum-group metals in catalytic converters, and zeolites in fluid catalytic cracking of petroleum.

Biocatalysis

Enzymes are protein catalysts that accelerate biochemical reactions with extraordinary specificity and rate enhancements. Nearly every metabolic reaction in living organisms is enzyme-catalysed.

Significance

It is estimated that catalysts are involved in the production of the great majority of industrially important chemicals by volume. Catalysis underlies technologies from fertiliser production and petroleum refining to pharmaceutical synthesis and emissions control, and is a central theme of green chemistry because it can reduce energy use and waste.
