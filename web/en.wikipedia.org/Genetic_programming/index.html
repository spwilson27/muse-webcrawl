Genetic programming

In artificial intelligence, genetic programming (GP) is a technique of evolving programs, starting from a population of unfit (usually random) programs, fit for a particular task by applying operations analogous to natural genetic processes to the population of programs. It is essentially a heuristic search technique often described as a type of evolutionary algorithm.

Method

A population of candidate programs, usually represented as parse trees built from a set of functions and terminals, is evaluated against a fitness measure defined by test cases. The fittest individuals are selected to produce offspring through subtree crossover — exchanging branches between two parent trees — and mutation, which randomly alters a subtree. Over many generations, the population tends to evolve programs that solve the task better. Because search spaces are vast, evolved programs can grow without improving, a phenomenon known as bloat, which is countered by size limits and parsimony pressure.

History

The idea of evolving computer programs traces to work by Alan Turing and, in the 1980s, to systems by Stephen F. Smith and Nichael Cramer. Genetic programming was popularised by John Koza's 1992 book Genetic Programming, which demonstrated evolved solutions to symbolic regression, control, and classification problems and later reported numerous "human-competitive" results, including evolved electronic circuit designs that infringed or duplicated patented inventions.

Variants and applications

Variants include linear GP, which evolves sequences of machine-code instructions; Cartesian GP, which uses graph representations; and grammar-guided GP. Applications include symbolic regression, automated machine learning, antenna and circuit design, financial modelling, and the evolution of game-playing strategies.
