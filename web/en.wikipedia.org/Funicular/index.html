Funicular

A funicular is a type of cable railway system that connects points along a railway track laid on a steep slope. Two counterbalanced cars are permanently connected by a cable that runs through a pulley at the upper station, so that as one car ascends, the other descends, largely balancing its weight. The name derives from the Latin funiculus, the diminutive of funis, meaning "rope".

Operation

The two cars are designed so their floors remain level or are stepped to match the incline. The cable is driven by a motor or, historically, by a water-balance system in which tanks on the upper car are filled so its greater weight pulls the lower car up. Because the cars counterbalance each other, only the difference in load, plus friction, must be overcome by the drive, making funiculars energy-efficient for steep gradients.

Track layouts

Several layouts exist. Two-rail systems give each car its own track; three-rail systems share the middle rail; and four-rail systems interleave two independent tracks. Many funiculars use a single track with a passing loop in the middle, where the cars have offset double-flanged wheels so each always takes the same side of the loop — a design perfected on systems such as the Gelmerbahn in Switzerland, among the steepest funiculars in the world.

History and notable examples

Early funiculars served mines and canals; the Reisszug in Salzburg, dating to the early 16th century, is among the oldest known. Famous examples include the Angels Flight in Los Angeles, the Duquesne Incline in Pittsburgh, the Peak Tram in Hong Kong, the Gelmerbahn and Stanserhorn funiculars in Switzerland, and the Flying Dutchman at the Cape of Good Hope. Related technologies include the cable car, the cliff railway, and the inclined elevator, which differs in using a single car and a counterweight rather than two passenger cars.
