Rendering equation

Rendering equation
FieldComputer graphics
Introduced1986, by James Kajiya; independently David Immel et al.
DescribesEquilibrium distribution of light in a scene

In computer graphics, the rendering equation is an integral equation describing the balance of light energy in a scene: the light leaving a point in a given direction equals the light emitted from that point plus the light reflected from all incoming directions. Introduced by James Kajiya in 1986 (and derived independently by David Immel and colleagues from heat-transfer theory), it provides the theoretical foundation for physically based rendering and global illumination algorithms.

The equation

Lo(x, ωo) = Le(x, ωo) + ∫Ω fr(x, ωi, ωo) Li(x, ωi) (ωi · n) dωi

Here Lo is the outgoing radiance from point x in direction ωo; Le is the emitted radiance; the integral sums over the hemisphere of incoming directions ωi; fr is the bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF), describing how the surface reflects light; Li is the incoming radiance; and the cosine term accounts for foreshortening. Because the incoming light at one point is the outgoing light from another, the equation is recursive and self-referential.

Solving the equation

The rendering equation cannot be solved analytically for general scenes, so rendering algorithms approximate it numerically. Path tracing uses Monte Carlo integration, following random light paths through the scene. The radiosity method discretises surfaces into patches and solves for diffuse-only transport. Photon mapping, bidirectional path tracing and Metropolis light transport are other strategies, each with different strengths for caustics, glossy reflections and complex indirect light. Kajiya's original paper also introduced path tracing as a solution method.

Limitations

The classical rendering equation models geometric optics: it assumes light travels in straight lines, interacts instantaneously, and is not polarised or subject to wave effects such as diffraction and interference. It also ignores fluorescence and phosphorescence (wavelength shifting over time) and subsurface scattering must be added with a more general formulation, the radiative transfer equation, of which the rendering equation is a special case.
