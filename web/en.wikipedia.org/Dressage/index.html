Dressage (from French dresser, "to train") is a form of horse riding performed in exhibition and competition, as well as an art sometimes pursued solely for the sake of mastery. As an equestrian sport defined by the International Equestrian Federation, dressage is described as "the highest expression of horse training", in which horse and rider perform a series of predetermined movements, known as "figures" or "movements", from memory in a standard arena.

History

The art descends from the cavalry training of classical antiquity — Xenophon wrote On Horsemanship in the 4th century BC — and was refined in the Renaissance riding academies of Italy and France. The Spanish Riding School in Vienna, founded in the 16th century, preserves the classical tradition with its Lipizzaner stallions and the "airs above the ground" such as the levade, courbette and capriole. Modern competitive dressage grew out of military tests and entered the Olympic Games in 1912.

Movements and levels

Horses progress through levels of increasing difficulty, from walk–trot–canter basics to the Grand Prix, which demands collected and extended gaits, pirouettes, flying changes in sequence (one-tempis), and the artificial gaits of piaffe (a cadenced trot in place) and passage (an elevated, suspended trot). The German training scale — rhythm, suppleness, contact, impulsion, straightness, collection — structures schooling worldwide. In Grand Prix Freestyle, riders choreograph these movements to music.

Competition

Judges score each movement from 0 to 10, expressing results as percentages; elite Grand Prix scores exceed 80%. Germany has historically dominated team competition, while riders such as Charlotte Dujardin of Great Britain, on Valegro, set record scores above 93% in freestyle. Dressage also forms one phase of the sport of eventing, and para-equestrian dressage is a Paralympic discipline.

Controversies

The sport periodically debates training methods, notably hyperflexion of the neck ("rollkur"), which the FEI has restricted, and questions of judging subjectivity and horse welfare.
