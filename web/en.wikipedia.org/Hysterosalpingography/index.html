Hysterosalpingography

Normal hysterosalpingogram with free peritoneal spillage of contrast.

Hysterosalpingography (HSG), also known as uterosalpingography, is a radiologic procedure to investigate the shape of the uterine cavity and the shape and patency of the fallopian tubes. In this procedure, a radio-opaque contrast agent is injected through a cannula in the cervical canal and X-ray fluoroscopy images are taken as it fills the uterus and tubes. It is a mainstay of the investigation of infertility.

Procedure

The examination is usually performed after menstruation and before ovulation to avoid irradiating an early pregnancy. The cervix is cleaned and cannulated, contrast (water-based or oil-based) is injected under fluoroscopic observation, and spot films record the uterine outline, tubal filling, and free spillage of contrast into the peritoneal cavity, which demonstrates tubal patency.

Findings

HSG can reveal congenital uterine anomalies, submucosal fibroids, endometrial polyps, intrauterine adhesions (Asherman's syndrome), salpingitis isthmica nodosa, hydrosalpinx, and tubal occlusion. Blocked tubes, a major cause of infertility, are localised to the cornual, mid-tubal, or fimbrial end.

Therapeutic effect and risks

Flushing the tubes during HSG—particularly with oil-based contrast such as Lipiodol—appears to increase subsequent pregnancy rates, as shown in the H2Oil trial (2017). Risks include pelvic pain, contrast allergy, infection (especially with hydrosalpinx), and a small radiation dose. HSG has partly been supplemented by hysterosalpingo-contrast sonography (HyCoSy), which avoids radiation, and by laparoscopy with dye testing, the reference standard for tubal assessment.
