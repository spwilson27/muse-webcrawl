Nazism

Nazism (German: Nationalsozialismus), formally National Socialism, is the far-right totalitarian ideology of the Nazi Party (NSDAP) in Germany, led by Adolf Hitler. It combined extreme nationalism, racism (especially antisemitism), anti-communism, and the Führer principle of absolute obedience to the leader.

Ideology

Nazism preached the racial superiority of a so-called "Aryan" master race, the need for Lebensraum (living space) in Eastern Europe, the elimination of Jews and other groups it deemed enemies or inferior, eugenics and "racial hygiene", and the subordination of the individual to the Volksgemeinschaft (people's community). It rejected democracy, liberalism, and Marxism alike, though it appropriated mass-mobilisation techniques and nationalist appeals.

Rise and rule

The party rose amid defeat in the First World War, hyperinflation, and the Great Depression. Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933 and quickly established a dictatorship. Nazi racial policy culminated in the Holocaust — the genocide of six million Jews — alongside the murder of Roma, people with disabilities, Poles and Soviet prisoners of war, and others. Expansionist war from 1939 killed tens of millions before Germany's defeat in 1945.

Aftermath

The NSDAP was banned and its leaders tried at Nuremberg. Neo-Nazism persists in fringe movements, and Nazi symbols and denial of the Holocaust are criminalised in Germany and several other countries.
