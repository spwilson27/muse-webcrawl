Felix Warburg

Felix Moritz Warburg (January 14, 1871 – October 20, 1937) was a German-born American banker and philanthropist. A member of the Warburg banking family of Hamburg, he became a senior partner in the New York investment bank Kuhn, Loeb & Co. and was one of the leading Jewish philanthropists of his era.

Early life and career

Warburg was born in Hamburg, German Empire, the fourth son of Moritz and Charlotte Warburg, whose family bank, M. M. Warburg & Co., had been founded in 1798. In 1894 he emigrated to the United States and married Frieda Schiff, daughter of the financier Jacob Schiff. Through the marriage he joined Kuhn, Loeb & Co., his father-in-law's firm, becoming one of its senior partners and a prominent figure in American finance. His brothers included the banker Paul Warburg, an architect of the Federal Reserve System, and the art historian Aby Warburg.

Philanthropy

Warburg devoted much of his fortune and energy to charitable causes, particularly on behalf of Jewish communities. In 1914 he was a principal founder of the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee (the "Joint"), which distributed relief to Jews suffering in war-torn Europe and Palestine, and he served as its first chairman. He supported the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, the Jewish Theological Seminary, and numerous educational and refugee organizations. Though not an ideological Zionist, he backed the development of a Jewish homeland in Palestine and served as a leader of the enlarged Jewish Agency.

He was also a patron of music and education in New York, a founder of the Federation for the Support of Jewish Philanthropic Societies of New York City, and a supporter of orchestras and settlement houses.

Death and legacy

Warburg died suddenly in New York in October 1937. His Fifth Avenue mansion later became the home of the Jewish Museum. His son Edward Warburg became a noted art patron and co-founder of the Museum of Modern Art's collections circle, and his family remained influential in American finance and philanthropy for generations.
