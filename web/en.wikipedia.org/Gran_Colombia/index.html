Gran Colombia ("Great Colombia") is the historiographical name given to the Republic of Colombia that existed from 1819 to 1831. Proclaimed after Simón Bolívar's victory at the Battle of Boyacá, the republic united the former Spanish territories of New Granada, Venezuela, Quito (Ecuador) and Panama into a single state, realising Bolívar's dream of a united Spanish South America.

History

The republic was formally established by the Congress of Angostura in 1819 and organised under the Constitution of Cúcuta (1821), with Bogotá as capital. Bolívar served as president while campaigns continued to secure independence, including the victories at Carabobo (1821) and Pichincha (1822). Guayaquil was annexed in 1822.

Dissolution

Regional rivalries, disputes between centralists and federalists, and the ambitions of regional leaders such as José Antonio Páez in Venezuela undermined the union. The constitutional convention at Ocaña failed in 1828; Bolívar briefly ruled as dictator. In 1830 Venezuela and Ecuador seceded, Bolívar resigned and died near Santa Marta, and in 1831 the republic was formally dissolved into the Republic of New Granada, Venezuela and Ecuador.

Legacy

Gran Colombia endures as a symbol of Latin American unity. The modern flags of Colombia, Ecuador and Venezuela all derive from its tricolour.
