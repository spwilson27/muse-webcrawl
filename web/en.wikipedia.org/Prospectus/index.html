Prospectus (finance)

In finance, a prospectus is a disclosure document that describes a financial security for potential buyers. It commonly provides investors with material information about mutual funds, stocks, bonds, and other investments offered for sale to the public. In the United States, a prospectus for a public offering must be filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) as part of a registration statement, and issuers and underwriters face legal liability for material misstatements or omissions in it.

Contents

A typical prospectus includes a summary of the offering, the issuer's business and management, audited financial statements, the terms of the securities, the intended use of proceeds, risk factors, and details of the underwriting syndicate. During an initial public offering, issuers circulate a preliminary prospectus — colloquially the "red herring," for the red warning printed on its cover — before the final prospectus containing the offering price is issued. Mutual funds provide a statutory prospectus and a shorter summary prospectus covering objectives, fees, risks, and performance.

Regulation

Prospectus requirements arose from the securities reforms of the 1930s, including the US Securities Act of 1933, which enshrined a philosophy of full disclosure rather than government approval of an investment's merits. In the European Union, the Prospectus Regulation standardizes when a prospectus is required for offers to the public or admission to trading on regulated markets. Exemptions commonly apply to private placements sold only to accredited or institutional investors.
