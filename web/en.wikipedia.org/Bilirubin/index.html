Bilirubin

Bilirubin is a yellow compound that occurs in the normal catabolic pathway that breaks down heme in vertebrates. It is produced when old or damaged red blood cells are broken down, and is excreted in bile and urine. Elevated levels of bilirubin in the blood cause the yellowing of the skin and eyes known as jaundice.

Formation and metabolism

Bilirubin is formed by the breakdown of heme, first into the green pigment biliverdin by the enzyme heme oxygenase, and then into bilirubin by biliverdin reductase. This "unconjugated" (indirect) bilirubin is insoluble in water and travels in the blood bound to albumin to the liver, where it is conjugated with glucuronic acid, making it water-soluble so that it can be excreted in bile. In the intestine, bacteria convert it into urobilinogen and stercobilin, the latter giving faeces their brown colour.

Clinical significance

Blood bilirubin levels are a standard part of liver function tests. High levels (hyperbilirubinemia) may indicate haemolysis, liver disease such as hepatitis or cirrhosis, or blockage of the bile ducts. Newborns commonly develop mild jaundice because their livers cannot yet process bilirubin efficiently; severe cases are treated with phototherapy, which converts bilirubin into water-soluble isomers. Very high levels in infants can cause kernicterus, a form of brain damage. A hereditary mild elevation is seen in Gilbert's syndrome.
