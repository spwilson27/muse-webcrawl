Joseph Marie Jacquard

Joseph Marie Charles dit Jacquard (7 July 1752 – 7 August 1834) was a French weaver and merchant who invented the Jacquard loom (1804), a programmable loom controlled by punched cards. It was the first machine to use punched cards to control a sequence of operations and is considered an important landmark in the prehistory of computing.

Life and invention

Born in Lyon, the center of the French silk industry, Jacquard inherited his parents' workshop. Weaving figured silk required a drawboy to lift warp threads in intricate patterns; building on earlier work by Basile Bouchon, Jean-Baptiste Falcon, and Jacques de Vaucanson, Jacquard devised a head that read a chain of punched cards, each card controlling one pass of the shuttle and automatically selecting the threads to lift. Demonstrated in 1801 and patented in 1804, his loom let a single weaver produce complex brocade without assistants.

Legacy

Lyon's weavers initially resisted the loom, fearing unemployment, but by 1812 some 11,000 were in use in France, transforming textile manufacture. The punched-card principle directly influenced Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine and Herman Hollerith's tabulating machines; "jacquard" survives as the name of fabrics woven with intricate patterns. A famous woven silk portrait of Jacquard himself, produced on his loom from 24,000 punched cards, impressed Babbage, who owned a copy.
