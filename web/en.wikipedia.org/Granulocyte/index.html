Granulocyte

Granulocytes are cells of the innate immune system characterized by the presence of specific granules in their cytoplasm. Such granules distinguish them from the other white blood cells (leukocytes), the agranulocytes. Granulocytes are also called polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMN, PML, or PMNL) because of the varying shapes of the nucleus, which is usually lobed into three segments. This distinguishes them from the mononuclear agranulocytes.

The term polymorphonuclear leukocyte often refers specifically to neutrophil granulocytes, the most abundant of the granulocytes; the other types are eosinophils, basophils, and mast cells. Granulocytes are produced via granulopoiesis in the bone marrow.

Types

Neutrophils

Neutrophils are normally found in the bloodstream and are the most abundant type of phagocyte, constituting 60% to 65% of the total circulating white blood cells, and consisting of two subpopulations: neutrophil-killers and neutrophil-cagers. They are short-lived (5–135 hours) and highly mobile. Neutrophils are the first cells to arrive at a site of infection, recruited by chemokines such as interleukin-8 (IL-8).

Eosinophils

Eosinophils make up about 1–4% of white blood cells. Their granules stain intensely with the acidic dye eosin, giving them a brick-red appearance. They are important in defense against parasitic infections and are heavily involved in allergic reactions and asthma.

Basophils

Basophils are the rarest granulocytes, accounting for less than 1% of circulating leukocytes. Their granules contain histamine and heparin, and they play a central role in inflammatory and allergic responses.

Mast cells

Mast cells are tissue-resident granulocytes derived from the same lineage. They reside in connective and mucosal tissues and mediate allergic responses through the release of histamine and other mediators upon activation, often via IgE antibodies.

Development

Granulocytes develop from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow through a process called granulopoiesis. The committed progenitor passes through stages — myeloblast, promyelocyte, myelocyte, metamyelocyte and band cell — before maturing. The hormone granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) is the principal regulator of neutrophil production.

Granule contents

Examples of toxic materials produced or released by degranulation are:

  • Antimicrobial peptides (defensins)
  • Hydrolytic enzymes such as lysozyme and elastase
  • Oxidative burst products (hydrogen peroxide, hypochlorous acid)
  • Acid hydrolases
  • Bactericidal permeability-increasing protein (BPI)

Clinical significance

Low granulocyte counts (granulocytopenia, or agranulocytosis when severe) increase susceptibility to bacterial and fungal infections and may result from bone marrow suppression, chemotherapy, or autoimmune destruction. Excess production occurs in myeloproliferative disorders such as chronic myeloid leukemia. Granulocyte transfusions are occasionally used to treat patients with severe neutropenia and life-threatening infection.
