Homo heidelbergensis

Homo heidelbergensis
Fossil range: Middle Pleistocene, c. 700,000–200,000 years ago
Type specimen: Mauer 1 mandible
Height: approx. 157–185 cm
Brain size: approx. 1,100–1,400 cm³

Homo heidelbergensis is an extinct species or subspecies of archaic human that lived in the Middle Pleistocene, roughly 700,000 to 200,000 years ago. It is widely regarded as the common ancestor (or close to it) of Neanderthals in Europe, Denisovans in Asia and modern humans in Africa. The species was named in 1908 after the Mauer mandible, a jawbone found in a sand quarry near Heidelberg, Germany, in 1907.

Discovery and taxonomy

The Mauer jaw — massive, chinless, but with surprisingly modern teeth — was the first evidence of an ancient human in Europe. Later finds assigned to the species include the Arago skull from France, the Petralona skull from Greece, the Steinheim skull from Germany, the Swanscombe remains from England, the Broken Hill (Kabwe) skull from Zambia and the vast assemblage from the Sima de los Huesos ("Pit of Bones") at Atapuerca, Spain, containing remains of at least 28 individuals dated to about 430,000 years ago. Some researchers split the African material into Homo rhodesiensis (and, in a 2021 proposal, Homo bodoensis), while others treat H. heidelbergensis as a single widespread species; the boundaries remain debated.

Anatomy

H. heidelbergensis was tall and robustly built — males averaged about 175 cm and 62 kg, with wide hips and thick limb bones adapted to cold or seasonal climates. The skull combines a large brain (1,100–1,400 cm³, overlapping the modern range) with archaic features: massive brow ridges, a low, long braincase, a projecting face and no chin. Leg proportions suggest efficient long-distance walking. A remarkably complete pelvis from Sima de los Huesos, nicknamed "Elvis", indicates a broad body plan.

Behaviour and technology

The species made Acheulean handaxes and, later, Levallois prepared-core tools. The Schöningen spears from Germany — wooden throwing spears about 300,000–400,000 years old, found with butchered horse bones — show sophisticated big-game hunting. Evidence of controlled fire becomes common at sites from around 400,000 years ago (such as Qesem Cave in Israel), though earlier claims are contentious. At Boxgrove in England (c. 500,000 years ago), H. heidelbergensis butchered horses and rhinos. Structures of wood and the deliberate burial suggested by the Sima de los Huesos accumulation hint at complex social behaviour, and studies of ear anatomy suggest hearing tuned to speech-like frequencies.

Evolutionary position

Ancient DNA from the Sima de los Huesos fossils shows early Neanderthal affinities, placing those populations on the Neanderthal line by 430,000 years ago. Genetic and fossil evidence together indicate that the populations ancestral to Neanderthals, Denisovans and modern humans diverged roughly 600,000–800,000 years ago, and H. heidelbergensis (in the broad sense) is the usual name for the stem from which all three descend.
