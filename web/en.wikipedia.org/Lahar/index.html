Lahar

Lahar
TypeVolcanic mudflow / debris flow
Origin of termJavanese

A lahar (from Javanese) is a violent type of mudflow or debris flow composed of a slurry of pyroclastic material, rocky debris and water, flowing down from a volcano, typically along a river valley. Lahars are among the deadliest volcanic hazards.

Formation

Lahars form when water — from a crater lake, melting snow and ice, or heavy rain — mixes with loose volcanic ash and debris on a volcano's flanks. Eruptions can trigger them directly, but they also occur years later when rain remobilises ash deposits. The slurry has the consistency of wet concrete, can flow at tens of kilometres per hour, and can grow in volume as it erodes its channel.

Notable disasters

The 1985 eruption of Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia melted its summit ice cap; lahars buried the town of Armero and killed around 23,000 people, one of the worst volcanic disasters in history. Lahars from Mount Pinatubo (1991) continued to devastate surrounding provinces for years after the eruption. Tangiwai, New Zealand (1953), was destroyed when a lahar from Mount Ruapehu washed out a rail bridge, causing a train disaster that killed 151.

Mitigation

Monitoring systems — acoustic flow monitors, tripwires and rain gauges — now guard valleys below volcanoes such as Mount Rainier, whose ancient Osceola Mudflow shows that lahars can reach far into populated lowlands.
