Pupillometry

Pupillometry is the measurement of pupil size and reactivity. The pupil of the eye dilates (mydriasis) and constricts (miosis) in response to light, cognitive effort, emotional arousal, drugs, and neurological conditions, and these changes can be measured precisely with specialised instruments called pupillometers.

Methods

Modern pupillometry typically uses infrared cameras that track the pupil without interfering with vision, often as part of eye-tracking systems. In clinical neurology, handheld automated pupillometers quantify the pupillary light reflex, reporting variables such as baseline diameter, constriction latency, constriction velocity, and percentage change.

Applications

In cognitive psychology and psycholinguistics, task-evoked pupillary responses index mental effort and attention: the pupil dilates as tasks grow harder, an effect explored in the work of Daniel Kahneman and colleagues. In medicine, pupillometry is used to assess brain injury, monitor anaesthesia and pain, and detect autonomic dysfunction. Researchers also study pupillary responses to emotional stimuli, marketing materials, and signs of fatigue or drug influence.
