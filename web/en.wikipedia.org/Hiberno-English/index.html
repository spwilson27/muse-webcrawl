Hiberno-English

Hiberno-English, also called Irish English, is the set of varieties of the English language spoken in Ireland. Shaped by centuries of contact with the Irish language (and, in Ulster, by Scots), it preserves distinctive features of pronunciation, vocabulary, and grammar not found in other standard varieties.

Features

Characteristic traits include rhoticity (pronouncing /r/ after vowels), the "after" perfect ("I'm after eating" — I have just eaten), borrowed directly from Irish syntax; the use of "ye" as a plural second-person pronoun; epenthetic vowels in words like "fillum" for film; and Irish-derived vocabulary such as craic, eejit, and gasún. Many varieties distinguish the vowels of horse and hoarse or pronounce th as a dental stop.

Varieties

Major groupings include the prestige Dublin and supraregional southern varieties, mid-Ulster English, and Ulster Scots-influenced speech in the north. Hiberno-English usage is richly represented in the works of writers from Synge to Roddy Doyle. See also English language and languages of Ireland.
