Philippine Revolution

The Philippine Revolution (1896–1898), also known as the Tagalog War by the Spanish, was a conflict between the people and military forces of the Philippines and Spanish colonial authorities. The revolution began in August 1896, when Spanish authorities discovered the Katipunan, a secret anti-colonial society founded by Andrés Bonifacio. It culminated in the proclamation of Philippine independence on June 12, 1898, and the establishment of the First Philippine Republic.

Background

More than three centuries of Spanish rule, the Propaganda Movement of reformist ilustrados such as José Rizal, and the execution of the Gomburza priests in 1872 fueled rising Filipino nationalism. In 1892 Bonifacio founded the Katipunan (KKK), a secret society aiming at independence through armed revolt.

Course of the revolution

The revolution erupted with the Cry of Pugad Lawin in August 1896. Fighting spread through the Tagalog provinces. A power struggle within the movement led to the Tejeros Convention and the execution of Bonifacio in 1897, after which Emilio Aguinaldo assumed leadership. The Pact of Biak-na-Bato (December 1897) brought a temporary truce and Aguinaldo's exile to Hong Kong. The revolution resumed in 1898 alongside the Spanish–American War; United States naval forces destroyed the Spanish fleet at the Battle of Manila Bay, and Aguinaldo proclaimed independence at Kawit, Cavite, on June 12, 1898.

Aftermath

The Treaty of Paris (1898) transferred the Philippines from Spain to the United States, leading to the Philippine–American War and delaying full independence until 1946.
