An N95 respirator is a disposable particulate-filtering facepiece respirator certified by the U.S. National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) to filter at least 95% of airborne particles, including the most penetrating particle size of about 0.3 micrometres. The letter "N" denotes that the filter is not resistant to oil-based aerosols.

Use

N95s are widely used in healthcare — to protect against airborne infectious agents such as tuberculosis and respiratory viruses — and in industry against dusts from construction, mining and agriculture. Proper protection requires fit testing and a tight seal to the face; facial hair can compromise the seal. During the COVID-19 pandemic, N95s (and equivalents such as KN95 and FFP2) became central to infection control, and shortages led to extended-use and decontamination protocols.

Design and history

The filtering medium is typically melt-blown polypropylene bearing an electrostatic charge that captures particles by interception, impaction, diffusion and electrostatic attraction. The modern N95 standard dates to NIOSH's 1995 revision of respirator certification (42 CFR Part 84), which introduced the N/R/P series at 95, 99 and 99.97% efficiency levels.
