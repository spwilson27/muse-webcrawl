Italian neorealism

Italian neorealism (Italian: Neorealismo) was a film movement that emerged in Italy in the mid-1940s, characterised by stories of the poor and working class, shot on location with mostly non-professional actors, in the social and economic conditions of post-war Italy. It is one of the most influential movements in the history of cinema, and a defining chapter of Italian cinema.

Origins and characteristics

Neorealism arose from opposition to the glossy studio productions of the Fascist era and from the material reality of a devastated country: with Cinecittà damaged and used as a refugee camp, directors took their cameras into the streets. Screenwriter Cesare Zavattini became the movement's theorist, calling for films that followed ordinary people through everyday life with minimal plot contrivance. Hallmarks include location shooting, natural light, long takes, improvised dialogue, dialect, unresolved endings, and an emphasis on social commentary — poverty, unemployment, the black market, and the wounds left by fascism and occupation.

Key films and directors

Luchino Visconti's Ossessione (1943) is usually treated as the precursor. The movement's landmark works include Roberto Rossellini's war trilogy — Rome, Open City (1945), Paisan (1946) and Germany, Year Zero (1948) — and Vittorio De Sica's films scripted by Zavattini: Shoeshine (1946), Bicycle Thieves (1948), Miracle in Milan (1951) and Umberto D. (1952). Visconti's La Terra Trema (1948), shot with Sicilian fishermen speaking dialect, and Giuseppe De Santis's Bitter Rice (1949) are other canonical examples.

Influence and decline

Championed by critics such as André Bazin, neorealism profoundly influenced world cinema — from Satyajit Ray in India to the French New Wave and beyond — and reshaped the careers of its own makers, as Fellini, Antonioni and others evolved beyond it. By the early 1950s commercial pressures, government displeasure at films "washing dirty linen in public", and Italy's economic recovery diluted the movement into the milder "pink neorealism", and its principals moved on to new styles.
