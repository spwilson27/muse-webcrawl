Canudos

The settlement of Canudos in the Bahian sertão (placeholder)

Canudos was a settlement founded in 1893 in the arid backlands (sertão) of Bahia, in northeastern Brazil, by the itinerant preacher Antônio Vicente Mendes Maciel, known as Antônio Conselheiro ("Antônio the Counselor"). The community attracted thousands of impoverished peasants, former slaves, and indigenous people drawn by Conselheiro's millenarian preaching and promises of a more just social order. It was destroyed by the Brazilian army in 1897 during the War of Canudos.

Foundation and growth

After years of wandering the backlands preaching, repairing churches and cemeteries, Conselheiro settled with his followers at a farm called Canudos on the Vaza-Barris River. The community, which he named Belo Monte, grew rapidly — estimates of its population range from about 15,000 to 30,000 inhabitants — becoming one of the largest settlements in the Bahian interior. Land, work, and food were shared, and the community largely refused to recognise the authority and taxes of the recently proclaimed Brazilian republic, which Conselheiro denounced as the work of the Antichrist.

War of Canudos

Tensions with landowners, the Church, and republican authorities escalated into open conflict in 1896. The first three military expeditions sent against Canudos were defeated by the settlers, whose knowledge of the terrain and fanatical resistance humiliated the young republic. A fourth expedition in 1897, numbering some 8,000–10,000 troops with modern artillery under General Carlos Machado Bittencourt's overall direction, finally crushed the settlement after a long siege. Canudos was razed, most of its inhabitants killed, and Antônio Conselheiro died — probably of dysentery — shortly before the final assault on 5 October 1897. Total deaths are estimated at 15,000–25,000 settlers and around 5,000 soldiers.

Legacy

The war was famously chronicled by Euclides da Cunha in Os Sertões (1902; translated as Rebellion in the Backlands), a classic of Brazilian literature that portrayed the conflict as a clash between coastal "civilisation" and the neglected backlands. The site of the old settlement was submerged in the 1960s by the Cocorobó reservoir; a new town of Canudos was built nearby. The episode remains a powerful symbol in Brazilian culture of state violence against the poor and of messianic resistance.
