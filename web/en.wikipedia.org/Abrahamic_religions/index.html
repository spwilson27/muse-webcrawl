Abrahamic religions

The Abrahamic religions are a group of monotheistic religions that regard the patriarch Abraham as a central figure and trace their spiritual origins to the covenant between Abraham and God. The three largest are Judaism, Christianity and Islam; smaller traditions often included are the Baháʼí Faith, the Druze faith, Rastafari and Samaritanism. Together, adherents of Abrahamic religions make up more than half of the world's population.

Common features

All Abrahamic religions worship one God, regarded as the creator of the universe, and share elements of scripture and narrative, including the figures of Adam, Noah, Abraham and Moses. Abraham is portrayed in Genesis as the recipient of God's covenant and the ancestor of the Israelites through Isaac and of the Arabs through Ishmael. Jerusalem is holy to Jews, Christians and Muslims alike.

Differences

The traditions diverge on central doctrines: Judaism awaits the Messiah and follows the Torah and rabbinic law; Christianity holds that Jesus is the Messiah and Son of God and centres on his resurrection; Islam regards Muhammad as the final prophet and the Quran as God's final revelation, honouring Jesus as a prophet but rejecting his divinity. The term "Abrahamic" gained wide currency in interfaith discourse in the late twentieth century.
