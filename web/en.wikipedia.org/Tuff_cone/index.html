Tuff cone

A tuff cone is a type of volcanic cone formed by phreatomagmatic eruptions, in which rising magma interacts explosively with external water (from a lake, the sea, or groundwater). The resulting eruptions fragment the magma into fine ash that, when wet, slumps and consolidates into tuff. Tuff cones typically have broad, gently sloping flanks and a wide crater relative to their height, in contrast to the steep slopes of scoria cones.

Formation and examples

Because the ash is often deposited wet and at high angles of repose that then slump, tuff cones show beds dipping both inward and outward from the crater rim. Diamond Head in Honolulu, Hawaii, is a well-known tuff cone formed by eruptions through shallow seawater, as is Koko Head. Tuff rings are closely related, lower-relief variants built of similar deposits.
