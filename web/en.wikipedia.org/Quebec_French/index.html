Quebec French

Quebec French (French: français québécois) is the predominant variety of the French language in Canada. It is the variety spoken by the great majority of francophone Canadians, particularly in the province of Quebec, and is characterised by distinctive pronunciation, vocabulary, idioms and grammatical features shaped by the varieties brought from France in the 17th and 18th centuries and by subsequent contact with English and Indigenous languages.

History

French settlement in New France drew settlers largely from northwestern France — Normandy, Perche, Brittany, Poitou and the Paris region — whose diverse dialects levelled into a relatively uniform colonial French, in some respects more conservative than the evolving language of Paris. After the British conquest of 1760, Quebec French developed in relative isolation, preserving features such as the diphthongisation of long vowels and older pronunciations lost in European French, while absorbing English loanwords through trade, industry and administration.

Features

  • Pronunciation: affrication of /t/ and /d/ before high front vowels (tu pronounced [tsy]); laxing of high vowels in closed syllables; distinctive nasal vowels.
  • Vocabulary: archaisms such as char (car) and magasiner (to shop), borrowings from Indigenous languages such as achigan, and calques from English. The informal register is known as joual when strongly marked.
  • Grammar: the interrogative particle -tu, and distinctive uses of pronouns and prepositions.

Status

French is the sole official language of Quebec under the Charter of the French Language (Bill 101, 1977). The Office québécois de la langue française regulates and promotes standard Quebec French, coining terms such as courriel (email) that have spread to the wider Francophonie. Standard Quebec French is mutually intelligible with European French, though informal registers can present difficulties for outsiders.
