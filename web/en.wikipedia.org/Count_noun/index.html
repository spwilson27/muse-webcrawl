Count noun

Count noun
FieldLinguistics, grammar
DefinitionA noun denoting a countable entity, with distinct singular and plural forms
Exampleschair/chairs, apple/apples, idea/ideas
OppositeMass noun (uncountable noun)
Typical determinersa, one, two, many, few, each, every

In linguistics, a count noun (also countable noun) is a noun that can be modified by a numeral and occurs in both singular and plural forms, denoting discrete, individually countable entities: one chair, two chairs. Count nouns contrast with mass nouns (uncountables) such as water or furniture, which denote undifferentiated stuff and cannot normally be pluralised or directly counted.

Count nouns, unlike mass nouns, can be enumerated directly: three apples.

Grammatical behaviour

English count nouns take the indefinite article a/an in the singular, accept numerals and quantifiers such as many and few, and form regular or irregular plurals (dog/dogs, child/children). Mass nouns instead take much and little, lack plurals, and must be counted via measure or container words — two glasses of water, three pieces of furniture. Many nouns shift between the classes depending on sense: cake is mass in "we ate cake" but count in "she baked three cakes", and paper differs in "some paper" versus "a paper" (a newspaper or article).

Across languages

Languages vary greatly in how they mark countability. Classifier languages such as Chinese, Japanese, and Thai require a numeral classifier between a number and almost any noun. Some languages mark plurality only on count nouns or not at all, relying on context. In formal semantics, the count/mass distinction is analysed in terms of whether a noun's denotation is quantised (made of distinct atoms) versus cumulative — any two portions of water together are still water, whereas two chairs are not a chair.
