The Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) is a non-profit corporation established by the United States Congress in Title I of the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 to oversee the audits of public companies and SEC-registered brokers and dealers, in order to protect investors and the public interest. It was created in the wake of the accounting scandals at Enron and WorldCom, which destroyed the audit firm Arthur Andersen and exposed the weaknesses of the profession's previous self-regulation through peer review.

Functions

The PCAOB registers public accounting firms that audit issuers; sets auditing, quality-control, ethics, and independence standards; inspects registered firms (annually for firms auditing more than 100 issuers, and at least triennially for others); and investigates and disciplines firms and auditors for violations. Its five-member board is appointed by the Securities and Exchange Commission, which also approves the PCAOB's rules and budget; no more than two members may be accountants. The Board is funded by an accounting support fee levied on public companies.

History and controversies

Founding chairman William McDonough, former president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, led the Board from 2003. Notable enforcement episodes include the 2017–2018 scandal in which former PCAOB staff leaked confidential inspection plans to KPMG personnel, leading to criminal convictions and a record $50 million SEC penalty against KPMG in 2019. A long-running dispute over inspection access in China and Hong Kong was addressed by the Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act of 2020, and in 2022 the PCAOB secured protocol access to inspect China-based firms. In Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB (2010) the Supreme Court upheld the Board's constitutionality while curing a separation-of-powers defect by making board members removable at will by the SEC.
