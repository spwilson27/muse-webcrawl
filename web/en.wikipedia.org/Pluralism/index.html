Pluralism

Religious and cultural pluralism in a modern city

Pluralism, in its most general sense, denotes a condition or framework in which diversity — of beliefs, values, cultures, interests or entities — is recognised, accommodated or positively valued, as opposed to monism, which seeks a single unifying principle or authority. The term is used across philosophy, political science, religion, law and sociology with distinct but related meanings.

Political pluralism

In political science, pluralism is the theory that power in a democratic society is dispersed among many groups — interest groups, unions, businesses, parties, churches and voluntary associations — which compete and bargain so that no single group dominates. Classical pluralists such as Robert Dahl argued that this competition, combined with elections, keeps government responsive. Critics, including elitists and Marxists, countered that wealth and organisation give some groups persistently greater influence, a position Dahl himself later qualified. Corporatist and consociational arrangements in countries such as the Netherlands historically institutionalised pluralism by organising society into recognised "pillars" with their own schools, media and parties.

Philosophical pluralism

Metaphysical pluralism holds that reality consists of many kinds of substances or principles rather than one (monism) or two (dualism). In ethics, value pluralism, associated with Isaiah Berlin, holds that human values — liberty, equality, justice — are genuinely many, objective, but sometimes incompatible and incommensurable, so moral conflict cannot always be resolved by a single scale. Epistemological pluralism accepts multiple legitimate methods or frameworks of knowledge.

Religious and cultural pluralism

Religious pluralism can mean the coexistence of different religions within a society, or the theological position — defended by John Hick — that the great world religions are equally valid responses to the same ultimate reality. Cultural pluralism describes a society in which minority groups maintain their distinct identities within a shared political framework, an ideal sometimes contrasted with assimilation and expressed by the metaphor of the "salad bowl" rather than the "melting pot". Legal pluralism recognises that more than one legal system — state, customary, religious — may operate within the same society.
