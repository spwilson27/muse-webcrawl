Walter Anthony Rodney (23 March 1942 – 13 June 1980) was a Guyanese historian, political activist, and academic. He is best known for his 1972 book How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, a landmark work that argued Africa's poverty was the direct result of European colonialism and the Atlantic slave trade, and which profoundly influenced scholarship on development and postcolonial thought.

Early life and education

Rodney was born in Georgetown, British Guiana (now Guyana), into a working-class family. He won a scholarship to Queen's College in Georgetown and later to the University of the West Indies (UWI) in Jamaica, graduating in 1963 with first-class honours in history. He earned his PhD from the School of Oriental and African Studies (SOAS) in London in 1966, at the age of 24, with a thesis on the slave trade on the Upper Guinea Coast, later published as A History of the Upper Guinea Coast, 1545–1800.

Career and activism

Rodney taught at the University of Dar es Salaam in Tanzania and at UWI Mona in Jamaica. His dismissal from Jamaica by the government of Hugh Shearer in October 1968 — barring him from re-entering the country after he attended a Black writers' conference in Montreal — sparked the "Rodney Riots" in Kingston, protests that became a landmark in Caribbean political history. His lectures to ordinary people outside the university, which he called "groundings", were published posthumously as The Groundings with My Brothers (1969).

In Tanzania he was part of a circle of radical scholars developing critiques of underdevelopment. How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (1972), published while he was there, argued that Africa was deliberately exploited and underdeveloped by European colonial regimes, which retarded the continent's economic, social, and political growth.

Return to Guyana and assassination

Rodney returned to Guyana in 1974, where he was denied a promised academic post at the University of Guyana by the government of Forbes Burnham. He became a leading figure in the Working People's Alliance (WPA), a political movement opposing the Burnham regime, and his activism during the "revolt" of 1979 saw several WPA members charged with arson after a fire at government offices.

On 13 June 1980, Rodney was killed in Georgetown when a bomb concealed in a walkie-talkie exploded in his car. His brother Donald, who was with him, survived. A sergeant of the Guyana Defence Force, Gregory Smith, was implicated in the killing and fled to French Guiana. A 2014–2016 commission of inquiry concluded that Rodney's death was an assassination carried out by agents of the Guyanese state with the knowledge of Prime Minister Burnham. Rodney was 38 years old.

Legacy

Rodney's work remains central to dependency theory, African studies, and pan-African thought. The Walter Rodney Foundation, the Rodney Chair at the University of the West Indies, and numerous lectures and institutions preserve his legacy; in 2023 Guyana posthumously awarded him the Order of Excellence, the nation's highest honour.
