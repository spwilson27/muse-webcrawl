Angelman syndrome

Angelman syndrome
SpecialtyMedical genetics, neurology, paediatrics
SymptomsSevere intellectual disability, absent/minimal speech, ataxic gait, frequent smiling and laughter, seizures, sleep disturbance
OnsetNoticed by 6–12 months of age
CauseLoss of function of the maternal UBE3A gene on chromosome 15q11–13
FrequencyAbout 1 in 12,000–20,000 births

Angelman syndrome is a genetic disorder that mainly affects the nervous system. It is characterised by severe intellectual disability, developmental delay, severely impaired or absent speech, problems with movement and balance (ataxia) — often with a characteristic hand-flapping, wide-based gait — and a distinctive happy demeanour with frequent smiling, laughter and excitability. It was first described in 1965 by the English paediatrician Harry Angelman, who called the children "puppet children" because of their jerky gait and happy affect.

Cause

Angelman syndrome results from loss of expression of the maternally inherited copy of the UBE3A gene in chromosome region 15q11–q13. In most cases (about 70%) this is due to a deletion of the maternal region; other mechanisms include paternal uniparental disomy (two copies from the father), imprinting defects, and mutations in UBE3A itself. The same chromosomal region, when deleted from the paternal copy, causes the contrasting Prader–Willi syndrome — the classic example of genomic imprinting in humans.

Signs and symptoms

Developmental delay becomes apparent by 6–12 months. Speech is severely affected, with most individuals using few or no words, while receptive language and non-verbal communication are relatively better. Seizures, often difficult to control, begin in early childhood; EEG abnormalities are characteristic. Other features include microcephaly developing by age two, sleep disturbance, fascination with water, protruding tongue, and a love of music. Adults generally remain dependent on carers but may continue to learn new skills.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is clinical and confirmed by genetic testing (methylation analysis of 15q11–13, followed by testing to determine the molecular class). There is no cure; management is supportive and multidisciplinary — anti-seizure medication, physiotherapy, speech and communication therapy (including augmentative communication), behaviour and sleep management. Life expectancy appears to be near normal. Research into reactivating the silent paternal copy of UBE3A is an active area of investigation.
