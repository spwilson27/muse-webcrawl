White House Chief of Staff

AppointerThe President of the United States
Formation1946 (as Assistant to the President); 1961 (current title)
First holderJohn R. Steelman
Reports toPresident of the United States

The White House chief of staff is the head of the Executive Office of the President of the United States and a cabinet position, in practice, though not formally of cabinet rank. The chief of staff manages the White House staff, controls access to the president, and is typically among the president's closest advisers.

Role

Duties vary by administration but commonly include selecting and supervising key staff, structuring the flow of information and people to the Oval Office, protecting the president's time, and negotiating with Congress and executive departments on the president's agenda. Because the role requires no Senate confirmation, the chief of staff is one of the most powerful unelected officials in the federal government.

History

The position evolved from the president's private secretary. President Harry Truman formalised the role as "Assistant to the President" in 1946, appointing John R. Steelman. Dwight D. Eisenhower, drawing on military practice, established the chief-of-staff model with Sherman Adams. Every president since has had a chief of staff, though Kennedy and Johnson initially preferred collegial staff systems.

Notable holders

Long-serving chiefs of staff include Andrew Card under George W. Bush and Denis McDonough under Barack Obama. James Baker, chief of staff to Ronald Reagan and George H. W. Bush, is often cited as a model of the office's effective use.
