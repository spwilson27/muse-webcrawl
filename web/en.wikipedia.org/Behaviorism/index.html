Behaviorism

Behaviorism is a systematic approach to understanding the behavior of humans and other animals. It assumes that behavior is either a reflex evoked by the pairing of certain antecedent environmental stimuli, or a consequence of that individual's history, including especially reinforcement and punishment contingencies, together with the individual's current motivational state and controlling stimuli.

History

Behaviorism began with John B. Watson's 1913 manifesto, "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It", which rejected introspection and argued psychology should study only observable behavior. Ivan Pavlov's experiments on conditioned reflexes in dogs and Edward Thorndike's law of effect provided its experimental foundations. B. F. Skinner later developed radical behaviorism and the analysis of operant conditioning, studying how consequences shape behavior in the "Skinner box"; his 1957 book Verbal Behavior attempted to extend the account to language.

Conditioning

In classical conditioning, a neutral stimulus paired with a biologically potent stimulus comes to evoke a conditioned response. In operant conditioning, behavior is shaped by its consequences: positive and negative reinforcement increase behavior, while punishment decreases it. Schedules of reinforcement — fixed or variable, ratio or interval — produce characteristic patterns of responding.

Criticism and legacy

Noam Chomsky's 1959 review of Verbal Behavior is often credited with undermining behaviorism's dominance and helping launch the cognitive revolution. Tolman's demonstrations of latent learning and cognitive maps in rats also challenged strict stimulus–response theory. Nevertheless, behaviorist methods survive in applied behavior analysis, behavior therapy, and behavioral economics, and its experimental rigor permanently reshaped psychology.
