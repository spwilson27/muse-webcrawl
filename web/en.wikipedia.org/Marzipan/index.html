Marzipan

Marzipan
Type: Confection
Main ingredients: Almond meal, sugar, sometimes almond oil or extract
Variations: Frutta martorana, Lübecker Marzipan, Königsberger Marzipan

Marzipan is a confection consisting primarily of sugar or honey and almond meal (ground almonds), sometimes augmented by almond oil or extract. Pliable and smooth, it is often made into sweets: common uses are marzipan-filled chocolate, small imitations of fruits and vegetables, rolled into thin sheets to cover cakes (notably Christmas cakes and wedding cakes), and shaped into small figures of animals or people for seasonal celebrations.

History

The origins of marzipan are disputed, with claims from Persia, the Middle East, Spain (through al-Andalus) and Italy. It spread across Europe in the Middle Ages and was long sold by apothecaries, since sugar was classed as a drug. The Hanseatic city of Lübeck became a famous centre of production — Lübecker Marzipan is protected by EU law and must contain at least 70% almonds. Toledo in Spain and Königsberg in East Prussia were other noted centres; Königsberger marzipan is characteristically flame-grilled on top.

Production and uses

Marzipan is produced by grinding blanched almonds with sugar and binding the mixture, sometimes with egg white, glucose syrup or rose water for flavour. Its low moisture content gives it a long shelf life. Because it holds fine detail, confectioners model it into fruits (Sicilian frutta martorana), pigs in Germany (a symbol of good luck), and decorations for seasonal cakes. Almond paste, a related American product, contains a lower proportion of almonds and coarser texture. Persipan, a cheaper substitute made from apricot or peach kernels, is used in some German baking.
