Gruiformes

The Gruiformes are an order of birds containing a considerable number of living and extinct bird families, with a widespread geographical diversity. As traditionally defined, the group included cranes, rails, coots, bustards, trumpeters, the kagu, the sunbittern, and several other families. Molecular studies have since narrowed the core Gruiformes and moved some former members elsewhere.

Classification

In modern classifications based on DNA evidence, the order Gruiformes contains six families: the cranes (Gruidae), the limpkin (Aramidae), the trumpeters (Psophiidae), the rails, crakes, gallinules and coots (Rallidae), the finfoots (Heliornithidae), and the flufftails (Sarothruridae). Groups formerly placed here but now assigned to other orders include the bustards (Otidiformes), mesites (Mesitornithiformes), and the kagu and sunbittern (Eurypygiformes). The seriemas, once included, are now placed in Cariamiformes, close to falcons and parrots.

Characteristics

Gruiform birds are highly diverse in size and habit, from small secretive marsh-dwelling crakes to cranes standing 1.5 m tall. Many are ground-dwelling birds of wetlands and grasslands with long legs and necks, omnivorous diets, and precocial chicks. Rails in particular have repeatedly evolved flightlessness on oceanic islands, making many island species vulnerable to introduced predators; numerous gruiform species are threatened or extinct.

Evolution

The gruiform lineage is ancient, with stem relatives known from the Palaeogene. The giant extinct flightless birds of the group include the Gastornithidae (sometimes placed in their own order) and, among more distant relatives once classified here, the terror birds (Phorusrhacidae) of South America.
