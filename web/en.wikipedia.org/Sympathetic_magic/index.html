Sympathetic magic

Sympathetic magic, also called imitative magic, is a type of magic based on imitation or correspondence. The concept was developed most influentially by the Scottish anthropologist James George Frazer in The Golden Bough (first published 1890), where he classified magic into two fundamental principles.

The two laws

Frazer proposed that sympathetic magic rests on two "laws of thought". The law of similarity holds that "like produces like": an effect may be produced by imitating it, so a magician can harm an enemy by injuring an image of them. Frazer called magic based on this principle "homeopathic" or imitative magic. The law of contact or contagion holds that things once in contact continue to act on each other at a distance; thus hair, nail clippings, or clothing of a person may be used to influence them. Frazer called this "contagious magic". The famous "voodoo doll" or poppet combines both principles.

Examples

Examples documented by anthropologists include effigies pierced or melted to harm their targets, rituals imitating rainfall to summon rain, cave paintings hypothesised to ensure successful hunts, and the treatment of jaundice with yellow objects under the doctrine of signatures. Practices resembling sympathetic magic appear in folk traditions worldwide, from European witchcraft to healing customs in Asia and Africa.

Criticism and later thought

Later anthropologists criticised Frazer's armchair method and his view of magic as failed science. Marcel Mauss and Henri Hubert offered a sociological account of magic, while cognitive scientists have linked sympathetic-magic intuitions to evolved contamination-avoidance psychology: psychologists such as Paul Rozin demonstrated that educated modern subjects still show implicit beliefs in contagion and similarity, for example refusing to eat fudge shaped like faeces or to drink juice touched by a sterilised cockroach.
