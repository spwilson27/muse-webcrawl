Language isolate

A language isolate is a language that has no demonstrable genealogical (or "genetic") relationship with any other known language. In other words, it forms a language family of exactly one member. The best-known examples include Basque in Europe, Ainu in Japan, Burushaski in northern Pakistan, and Sumerian of ancient Mesopotamia. Korean is frequently cited as an isolate, though some classifications group it with the small Koreanic family including Jeju.

Isolates versus unclassified languages

An isolate is distinct from an unclassified language: an unclassified language may lack the documentation needed to determine its relationships, whereas an isolate has been studied well enough that linguists are confident no relatives can be demonstrated with present evidence. Isolates may once have had relatives that died out without adequate records, a common fate in regions where languages were displaced by expanding families such as Indo-European or Bantu.

Notable isolates

Among living languages, frequently cited isolates include Basque, Burushaski, Ainu (nearly extinct), Ksunda (Kusunda) of Nepal, Nihali of India, Zuni and Haida (the latter two sometimes linked to proposed families that remain unproven) in North America, and numerous languages of New Guinea and Amazonia, such as Ticuna and Pirahã. Extinct isolates include Sumerian, Elamite, Etruscan, and Hattic.

Attempts at classification

Many proposals have attempted to relate isolates to larger groupings — Basque to ancient Iberian or to the languages of the Caucasus, Sumerian to a wide range of candidates, Ainu to Altaic and others — but none has won general acceptance. The comparative method requires systematic correspondences, and for most isolates the evidence is simply insufficient or the proposed similarities are judged coincidental.
