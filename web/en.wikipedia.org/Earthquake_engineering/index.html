Earthquake engineering

Shake-table testing of a seismic design

Earthquake engineering is an interdisciplinary branch of engineering that designs and analyses structures — buildings, bridges, dams and lifelines — with respect to their behaviour under the loads and ground motions of earthquakes. Its central goal is not to make structures earthquake-proof, but to ensure performance: preventing collapse and loss of life in strong shaking, limiting damage in moderate events, and keeping critical facilities operational.

Principles

Modern seismic design relies on capacity design and ductility: structures are detailed so that controlled, energy-dissipating yielding occurs in chosen members ("fuses") while brittle failure is suppressed. Engineers characterize hazard using response spectra and probabilistic seismic hazard analysis, then verify designs by nonlinear analysis and shake-table testing. Techniques include base isolation, which decouples the building from ground motion on bearings; energy dissipation devices such as viscous and friction dampers; and mass dampers for tall buildings.

Codes and practice

Seismic codes — such as ASCE 7 and Eurocode 8, and national codes in Japan, Chile and New Zealand — prescribe design ground motions and detailing rules by seismic zone and building importance. The discipline grew from lessons of disasters from San Francisco (1906) and Messina (1908) through Kobe (1995), where soft-storey and non-ductile concrete failures drove major code reforms. Retrofitting of existing vulnerable buildings, including unreinforced masonry and soft-storey housing, remains a major challenge worldwide. Performance-based earthquake engineering formalizes design targets as probabilities of repair cost, downtime and casualties.
