Allomorph

In linguistics, an allomorph is one of two or more variant forms (morphs) of a single morpheme — the smallest meaningful unit of a language — where the choice between variants is conditioned by the linguistic environment. The concept is parallel to the allophone in phonology.

One morpheme, several surface forms: the English plural.

Examples in English

The English plural morpheme has three common allomorphs: /s/ as in cats, /z/ as in dogs, and /ɪz/ as in horses, selected according to the final sound of the noun stem. The past-tense morpheme likewise appears as /t/ (walked), /d/ (played), and /ɪd/ (waited). Some allomorphs are not phonologically predictable: the plural of ox is oxen, retaining an archaic suffix, and the negative prefix in- appears as im- before labials (impossible) and il-/ir- by assimilation (illegal, irregular).

Types of conditioning

Allomorphy may be phonologically conditioned, as in the plural examples, where neighbouring sounds determine the variant; morphologically conditioned, where a specific grammatical context requires a particular form; or lexically conditioned, where the variant is an idiosyncratic property of individual words, as with oxen. A special case is the zero allomorph, where a morpheme has no overt realization, as in the plural of sheep.
