Dower

Dower is a provision accorded by law but traditionally by a husband or his family, to a wife for her support after her husband's death. The term is normally used in the common law English system. Legally, dower is the life interest (or "life estate") a widow acquires in a portion—customarily one-third—of the inheritable real property (lands) owned by her deceased husband during the marriage. It is distinct from dowry, which is property a bride brings to or is given at marriage, and from a jointure, a settlement made before marriage.

History

Dower was a feature of feudal land law in England and France. Under the Magna Carta of 1215 a widow was entitled to her dower "without difficulty" and to remain in her husband's house for forty days after his death (the widow's quarantine). At common law a widow's rights attached to lands of which the husband had been seised during the marriage, even lands he had sold, which made dower a serious encumbrance on titles; in practice it was often barred by marriage settlements, jointures, or, in the United States, recorded releases.

Modern law

In England, dower in its common-law form was effectively abolished by the Administration of Estates Act 1925, which replaced it with statutory rights of a surviving spouse. In the United States, dower was retained in many states into the 20th century, sometimes extended to widowers as "curtesy." Most states have abolished or replaced it with an elective share of the deceased spouse's estate, though a few jurisdictions—including Kentucky, Ohio and Arkansas—retain forms of dower. Similar institutions existed in civil-law countries, such as the douaire in France and the widow's portion in Scotland known as terce.
