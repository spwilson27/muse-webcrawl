Mimesis

Mimesis (Ancient Greek: μίμησις, mīmēsis) is a term used in literary criticism and philosophy that carries a wide range of meanings, including imitation, representation, mimicry, "the act of resembling", the act of expression, and the presentation of the self. In ancient Greece it was central to theories of art: for Plato, art was a potentially deceptive imitation of appearances; for Aristotle, imitation was natural to human beings and a legitimate, pleasurable way of learning about the world.

[Image: Greek theatre masks representing dramatic imitation of life]

Classical usage

In the Republic, Plato argued that art imitates physical things, which themselves imitate the Forms, making art "thrice removed" from truth, though he allowed more complex uses of the concept elsewhere. In the Poetics, Aristotle made mimesis the foundation of poetry and drama: tragedy is an imitation (mimesis) of an action, and through plot — the arrangement of incidents — it can reveal universals rather than mere copies of particulars.

Later developments

The concept passed through Roman and Renaissance literary theory, often rendered as imitatio. In the 20th century, Erich Auerbach's Mimesis: The Representation of Reality in Western Literature (1946) traced shifting styles of realism from Homer and the Bible to Virginia Woolf. René Girard developed a theory of "mimetic desire", arguing that human desire is imitative and triangular, leading to rivalry and violence. Michael Taussig and Homi Bhabha later used mimesis in anthropological and postcolonial contexts.
