The Schrödinger equation is a linear partial differential equation that governs the wave function of a non-relativistic quantum-mechanical system. Formulated by the Austrian physicist Erwin Schrödinger in late 1925 and published in 1926, it is the central equation of wave mechanics and one of the most successful equations in physics, predicting atomic spectra and chemical bonding with great precision.

Formulation

The time-dependent equation states:

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ

where ψ is the wavefunction, ℏ is the reduced Planck constant, and Ĥ is the Hamiltonian operator representing the total energy of the system. For a single particle in a potential V(r), it becomes:

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = [ −ℏ²/2m ∇² + V(r) ] ψ

Separation of variables yields the time-independent equation Ĥψ = Eψ, an eigenvalue problem whose solutions give the allowed energy levels (for example, the hydrogen atom's spectrum) and stationary states. Max Born's interpretation treats |ψ|² as a probability density.

History and significance

Building on de Broglie's hypothesis of matter waves, Schrödinger published his equation in a series of papers in 1926, soon afterwards showing its equivalence to Heisenberg's matrix mechanics. Schrödinger himself disliked the probabilistic interpretation that became standard (the Copenhagen interpretation), and his "Schrödinger's cat" thought experiment of 1935 dramatised the measurement problem the equation leaves open. Relativistic generalisations include the Klein–Gordon and Dirac equations; the Schrödinger equation nonetheless remains the workhorse of quantum chemistry, condensed-matter physics and quantum technology.
