In chemistry, an alcohol is an organic compound that carries at least one hydroxyl functional group (–OH) bound to a saturated carbon atom. The term originally referred to ethanol, the alcohol in alcoholic drinks, but now denotes the whole class. Alcohols are classified as primary, secondary or tertiary according to the number of other carbon atoms attached to the carbon bearing the –OH group.

Properties

The hydroxyl group makes alcohols polar and able to form hydrogen bonds, giving them higher boiling points and greater water solubility than hydrocarbons of similar size. Short-chain alcohols such as methanol, ethanol and propanol are miscible with water; solubility falls as the hydrocarbon chain lengthens. Alcohols are weakly acidic and react as nucleophiles and as bases.

Reactions

Characteristic reactions include oxidation (primary alcohols to aldehydes and carboxylic acids, secondary alcohols to ketones), dehydration to alkenes or ethers, esterification with carboxylic acids, and conversion to alkyl halides. Methanol is produced industrially from synthesis gas; ethanol is made both by fermentation of sugars and by hydration of ethylene.

Uses

Ethanol is used in beverages, as a solvent, antiseptic and fuel; methanol is an industrial feedstock and fuel; isopropyl alcohol is a common disinfectant; and polyols such as glycerol and sorbitol are used in foods, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals. Long-chain fatty alcohols serve in detergents and surfactants. Some alcohols, notably methanol and ethylene glycol, are highly toxic; methanol poisoning can cause blindness and death.

Nomenclature

In IUPAC nomenclature, alcohols take the suffix -ol (e.g., ethanol, propan-2-ol), with common names formed from the alkyl group plus "alcohol" (e.g., ethyl alcohol). Diols, triols and polyols carry two, three or many hydroxyl groups respectively.
