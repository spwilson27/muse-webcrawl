Consequentialism

Consequentialism is a class of normative ethical theories holding that the consequences of one's conduct are the ultimate basis for judgement of the rightness or wrongness of that conduct. A consequentialist judges an act by its outcome: an action is morally right if it produces good consequences, and rival theories such as deontology and virtue ethics differ precisely in not making consequences the sole criterion.

Utilitarianism and varieties

The best-known form is utilitarianism, developed by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, which holds that one should maximise overall well-being. Consequentialism may be act-based, judging each action individually, or rule-based, judging actions by rules whose general adoption has the best consequences. Critics object that unrestricted consequentialism can license intuitively wrongful acts — such as punishing an innocent person to calm a mob — whenever the sums come out favourably; defenders reply with more sophisticated formulations or accept the implications. Thinkers such as Peter Singer have developed consequentialist arguments in applied ethics, including the effective altruism movement.
