Subject-matter jurisdiction

Subject-matter jurisdiction
FieldCivil procedure
Related conceptsPersonal jurisdiction, venue, standing

Subject-matter jurisdiction is the authority of a court to hear cases of a particular type or cases relating to a specific subject matter. For instance, a bankruptcy court only has jurisdiction over bankruptcy cases. It is distinct from personal jurisdiction, which concerns the court's power over the parties to a lawsuit.

In the United States, subject-matter jurisdiction is a constitutional and statutory limit on a court's power that generally cannot be waived by the parties; a judgment rendered by a court lacking subject-matter jurisdiction is void and may be challenged at any time, even on appeal or collaterally.

Federal courts

United States federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction. Their subject-matter jurisdiction is defined principally by two heads of jurisdiction in Article III of the Constitution and implementing statutes:

  • Federal question jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1331): cases arising under the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States.
  • Diversity jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1332): cases between citizens of different states (or citizens and foreign nationals) where the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000.

Federal courts also exercise supplemental jurisdiction over related state-law claims under 28 U.S.C. § 1367.

State courts

State courts, by contrast, are generally courts of general jurisdiction, presumed competent to hear any matter not exclusively reserved to the federal courts. However, many states maintain specialised courts of limited jurisdiction, such as probate courts, small-claims courts, and family courts.
