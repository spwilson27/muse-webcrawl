Paneth cells are specialised epithelial cells found at the base of the crypts of Lieberkühn in the small intestine. They are easily recognised under the microscope by their large, brightly eosinophilic (pink-staining) secretory granules. First described in detail by the Austrian physiologist Joseph Paneth in 1888, they are key effectors of innate immunity in the gut and help maintain the neighbouring intestinal stem cells.

Function

When exposed to bacterial products, Paneth cells discharge their granules into the crypt lumen, releasing antimicrobial substances — principally α-defensins (called cryptdins in mice), lysozyme and secretory phospholipase A2. These molecules kill or inhibit bacteria, shaping the composition of the intestinal microbiota and protecting the stem-cell zone from infection. Paneth cells also supply growth and maintenance signals (such as Wnt3, EGF and Notch ligands) to the Lgr5-positive stem cells nestled between them.

Clinical significance

Paneth cell dysfunction is implicated in Crohn's disease: variants in genes involved in autophagy and bacterial sensing (such as ATG16L1 and NOD2) produce abnormal Paneth cell granules and are associated with ileal Crohn's. Reduced defensin production is also found in ileal Crohn's patients. Paneth cells are normally absent from the colon and stomach, so their appearance there (Paneth cell metaplasia) signals chronic mucosal injury and inflammation.
