Bond order

Field: Chemistry

Definition: Number of chemical bonds between two atoms

Related concepts: Valence, molecular orbital theory

In chemistry, bond order is a formal measure of the number of chemical bonds between a pair of atoms. A single bond has bond order 1, a double bond order 2, and a triple bond order 3. Higher bond order generally indicates a stronger, shorter bond. In molecular orbital theory, bond order is defined as half the difference between the number of electrons in bonding orbitals and the number in antibonding orbitals.

Examples

In the nitrogen molecule N2, the two atoms are joined by a triple bond, giving a bond order of 3. In oxygen, O2, the bond order is 2, and in hydrogen, H2, it is 1. Fractional bond orders arise where resonance structures contribute: in benzene, each carbon–carbon bond has a bond order of 1.5, intermediate between single and double. The nitric oxide molecule, NO, has a bond order of 2.5 because one electron occupies an antibonding orbital.

Unusual bond orders

Bond orders of four and above are known in transition-metal chemistry. Quadruple bonds occur in compounds such as the octachlorodirhenate anion. In 2005, a stable compound containing a quintuple bond between two chromium atoms was reported, and computational studies suggest sextuple bonding character in gaseous W2 and Mo2.

Relation to bond properties

For bonds between the same pair of elements, increasing bond order correlates with shorter bond length and greater bond dissociation energy. Thus carbon–carbon bond lengths decrease from ethane (single) through ethene (double) to ethyne (triple). Bond order is therefore a useful qualitative predictor of molecular stability and reactivity, though in delocalised systems the distribution of electrons may make a simple integer assignment misleading.
