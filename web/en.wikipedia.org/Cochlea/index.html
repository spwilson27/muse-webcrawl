Cochlea

The cochlea is the spiral-shaped, fluid-filled cavity in the inner ear that converts sound vibrations into nerve impulses. Its name derives from the Greek kokhlías, "snail", referring to its coiled form — in humans it makes about two and a half turns and measures roughly 9 mm across at its base. Together with the vestibular system, which serves balance, the cochlea forms the labyrinth of the ear.

Structure

The cochlea is divided lengthwise into three compartments: the scala vestibuli and scala tympani, filled with perilymph, and between them the scala media (cochlear duct), filled with potassium-rich endolymph. The scala media is bounded below by the basilar membrane, upon which sits the organ of Corti, containing some 15,000 sensory hair cells arranged in one row of inner hair cells and three rows of outer hair cells. Sound enters when the stapes of the middle ear rocks against the oval window, launching pressure waves through the perilymph; the round window membrane bulges to accommodate the displacement.

Function

The basilar membrane varies in stiffness and width along its length, so a travelling wave peaks at a characteristic place for each frequency: high frequencies at the stiff base, low frequencies at the flexible apex. This tonotopic organisation, described by Georg von Békésy (Nobel Prize, 1961), gives the cochlea its function as a frequency analyser. Inner hair cells transduce the motion into signals in the auditory nerve, while outer hair cells act as a "cochlear amplifier", actively contracting (via the motor protein prestin) to sharpen tuning and boost sensitivity by up to 40–60 dB. Damage to hair cells, which do not regenerate in mammals, is the main cause of sensorineural hearing loss.

Clinical aspects

Disorders involving the cochlea include noise-induced and age-related hearing loss (presbycusis), Ménière's disease, and ototoxic damage from certain drugs. The cochlea is also the site of action of cochlear implants, which bypass damaged hair cells by electrically stimulating the auditory nerve directly with an electrode array threaded into the scala tympani.
