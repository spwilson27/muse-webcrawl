Industrial Workers of the World

The Industrial Workers of the World (IWW), nicknamed the Wobblies, is an international labour union founded in Chicago in 1905. It advocates industrial unionism — organizing all workers in an industry into "One Big Union" regardless of trade, skill, race, or sex — and ultimately the abolition of the wage system and workers' control of industry. Its preamble declares that "the working class and the employing class have nothing in common."

History

Founders included Big Bill Haywood, Eugene V. Debs, Mary Harris "Mother" Jones, Daniel De Leon, and Lucy Parsons. Rejecting the craft-based American Federation of Labor, the IWW organized workers others ignored: migrant farmhands, loggers, miners, and immigrant textile operatives. Its famous victories included the Lawrence textile strike of 1912 (the "Bread and Roses" strike) and the Paterson silk strike of 1913; its free-speech fights challenged local bans on street speaking across the American West.

Decline and revival

During the First World War the IWW opposed militarism and was crushed by federal raids, mass trials, and state criminal-syndicalism laws; vigilante violence killed organizers such as Frank Little and Wesley Everest, and the 1917 Bisbee deportation saw 1,200 striking miners expelled into the desert. A 1924 split and competition from CIO industrial unions reduced the IWW to a small remnant. Membership revived modestly from the 1990s onward, with organizing among retail, coffee-shop, and fast-food workers and the publication of its newspaper, the Industrial Worker. The IWW's songs — by Joe Hill, Ralph Chaplin ("Solidarity Forever"), and others, collected in the Little Red Songbook — remain classics of labour culture.
