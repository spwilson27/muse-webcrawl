Isis was a major goddess in ancient Egyptian religion whose worship spread throughout the Greco-Roman world. Isis was first mentioned in the Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BC) as one of the main characters of the Osiris myth, in which she resurrects her slain brother and husband, the divine king Osiris, and produces and protects his heir, Horus. She was believed to help the dead enter the afterlife as she had helped Osiris, and she was considered the divine mother of the pharaoh, who was likened to Horus.

Her maternal aid was invoked in healing spells to benefit ordinary people. Originally, she played a limited role in royal rituals and temple rites, although she was more prominent in funerary practices and magical texts. She was usually portrayed in art as a human woman wearing a throne-shaped hieroglyph on her head. During the New Kingdom (c. 1550–1070 BC), as she took on traits that originally belonged to Hathor, the preeminent goddess of the earlier period, Isis came to be portrayed wearing Hathor's headdress: a sun disk between cow horns.

In the Osiris myth

Isis is part of the Ennead of Heliopolis, a family of nine deities descended from the creator god. Her parents are the earth god Geb and the sky goddess Nut. In the central myth, her brother-husband Osiris is murdered by their brother Set, who dismembers the body. Isis, with her sister Nephthys, searches for and reassembles the pieces, temporarily reviving Osiris so that she can conceive Horus. She then hides the infant Horus in the Nile Delta marshes and protects him until he grows up to challenge Set for the throne — a struggle that made Isis the archetype of the devoted wife and mother.

Spread of worship

In the first millennium BC, Isis's cult grew beyond Egypt. Her great temple on the island of Philae drew pilgrims from across the Mediterranean world, and under the Ptolemies and Romans her worship — often in the form of mystery rites promising personal salvation and protection — spread to Greece, Italy, and as far as Britain and the Danube. She absorbed the attributes of many local goddesses and was invoked as a universal deity, Isis Panthea. Her temple at Philae remained active into the 6th century AD, among the last functioning pagan temples in the Roman world, before being closed under Justinian around 536–537 AD.
