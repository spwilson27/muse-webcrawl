Salman Rushdie

Sir Ahmed Salman Rushdie (born 19 June 1947) is an Indian-born British-American novelist. His work, combining magical realism with historical fiction, often deals with the connections and migrations between Eastern and Western civilizations. His second novel, Midnight's Children (1981), won the Booker Prize and is considered one of the finest novels of the twentieth century. His fourth novel, The Satanic Verses (1988), was at the centre of a major controversy: in 1989 Iran's Ayatollah Khomeini issued a fatwa calling for his assassination, forcing Rushdie into hiding for nearly a decade.

Early life and career

Rushdie was born in Bombay into a Kashmiri Muslim family and educated at Rugby School and King's College, Cambridge, where he read history. He worked as an advertising copywriter in London before publishing his first novel, Grimus (1975), a science-fiction work that met with little notice.

Midnight's Children

Midnight's Children (1981) tells the story of Saleem Sinai, born at the exact moment of Indian independence on 15 August 1947, whose life mirrors the history of modern India. Mixing magical realism, allegory, and a teeming narrative voice, it won the Booker Prize in 1981 and was twice named the best of all Booker winners — the "Booker of Bookers" in 1993 and the Golden Booker in 2008. Shame (1983) applied a similar approach to Pakistan.

The Satanic Verses controversy

The Satanic Verses (1988), whose dream sequences concerning a prophet-like figure offended many Muslims, was banned in several countries amid protests and book burnings. On 14 February 1989 Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini issued a fatwa sentencing Rushdie to death. Rushdie went into hiding under British police protection; his Japanese translator Hitoshi Igarashi was murdered in 1991, and his Italian translator and Norwegian publisher were also attacked. The affair became a defining international test of free expression. The fatwa was never formally revoked, though the Iranian government distanced itself from it in 1998, and Rushdie gradually returned to public life.

Later work

Rushdie's later books include the children's story Haroun and the Sea of Stories (1990), written for his son during his hiding; The Moor's Last Sigh (1995); The Ground Beneath Her Feet (1999); Shalimar the Clown (2005); Two Years Eight Months and Twenty-Eight Nights (2015); and Victory City (2023). His memoir Joseph Anton (2012) recounts the fatwa years. He was knighted in 2007 and lives in the United States.

2022 attack

On 12 August 2022, as Rushdie was about to lecture at the Chautauqua Institution in New York state, an assailant rushed the stage and stabbed him repeatedly. Rushdie survived but was blinded in one eye and left with other lasting injuries. He wrote about the attack and his recovery in the memoir Knife: Meditations After an Attempted Murder (2024).
