Coupling constant

In physics, a coupling constant or gauge coupling parameter (or more simply a coupling) is a number that determines the strength of the force exerted in an interaction. Usually, the Lagrangian (or the Hamiltonian) of a system describing an interaction can be separated into a kinetic part and an interaction part; the coupling constant determines the strength of the interaction part with respect to the kinetic part. When a coupling is dimensionless, its size determines whether perturbation theory can be used to compute the effects of the interaction accurately.

Fine-structure constant

The best-known coupling is the electromagnetic coupling, whose dimensionless measure is the fine-structure constant α ≈ 1/137.036, equal to the square of the elementary charge in natural units. Quantum electrodynamics is tractable because α is small: each additional vertex in a Feynman diagram multiplies the amplitude by a factor of order α, so successive terms rapidly shrink.

Running couplings

In quantum field theory, couplings are not truly constant: their effective values vary with the energy scale of the interaction, a phenomenon described by the renormalization group and the beta function. In quantum chromodynamics the strong coupling decreases at high energies (asymptotic freedom) and grows at low energies, explaining why quarks cannot be isolated (confinement). The gravitational coupling, by contrast, is extremely weak at ordinary energies but grows with energy.

Weak and strong couplings

When a coupling is of order one or larger, perturbative methods fail and techniques such as lattice gauge theory are required. The strong interaction's coupling at the Z boson mass is about 0.118, while the weak interaction couplings are comparable in size to the electromagnetic one, their apparent feebleness at low energies arising from the large masses of the W and Z bosons.
