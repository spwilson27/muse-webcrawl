The small intestine (or small bowel) is the part of the gastrointestinal tract between the stomach and the large intestine where most digestion and nearly all absorption of nutrients takes place. In adult humans it is about 6–7 metres long, coiled within the abdomen and suspended by the mesentery.

Structure

It has three regions. The duodenum (~25 cm) receives chyme from the stomach plus bile and pancreatic juice, and begins chemical digestion. The jejunum (middle ~40%) is the main site of nutrient absorption. The ileum (final ~60%) absorbs vitamin B12 and bile salts before emptying into the caecum at the ileocaecal valve. The inner surface is massively amplified by circular folds, finger-like villi and microvilli (the brush border), giving an absorptive area estimated at 30–200 m².

Function

Peristalsis mixes and propels chyme; enzymes from the pancreas and brush border complete the breakdown of proteins, fats and carbohydrates, and absorbed nutrients pass into the blood (via the hepatic portal vein) or, for most fats, into the lymph. The small intestine also houses much of the gut-associated immune system, including Peyer's patches in the ileum, and a vast microbiome.

Clinical significance

Disorders include coeliac disease (gluten-triggered villous atrophy), Crohn's disease, infections causing diarrhoea, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, obstruction, and malabsorption syndromes. Loss of large lengths (short bowel syndrome) can require parenteral nutrition.
