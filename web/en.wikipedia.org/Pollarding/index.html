Pollarding

Pollarding is a pruning system in which the upper branches of a tree are regularly cut back, promoting a dense head of foliage and branches. It has been practised in Europe since medieval times, historically to produce fodder, firewood and timber out of the reach of grazing animals, and is now used mainly to keep street and garden trees to a manageable size.

Practice

In pollarding, young trees have their leading shoots removed once they reach a chosen height, typically two to three metres, and the regrowth is cut back at intervals of one to several years to the same points, producing swollen "pollard heads" or knuckles from which clusters of straight shoots emerge. Species commonly pollarded include willows, limes, planes, poplars and oaks. Because the cutting keeps the tree in a juvenile state, pollarded trees often live much longer than unpruned specimens of the same species; many ancient pollards survive in places such as Burnham Beeches and Epping Forest in England.

History and uses

Pollarding was integral to wood-pasture and coppice-with-standards management, yielding poles, fuel and leaf fodder while allowing livestock to graze beneath. It differs from coppicing, in which cutting is done at ground level. Today the technique is widely used in urban arboriculture—famously on London planes and French street trees—to control size, maintain clearance and reduce the risk of limb failure, though critics argue it can be disfiguring if begun on mature trees or allowed to lapse.
