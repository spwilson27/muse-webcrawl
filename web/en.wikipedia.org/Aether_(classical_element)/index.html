Aether (classical element)

Aether (Greek: αἰθήρ, aithḗr), also known as the quintessence or fifth element, was the material that fills the region of the universe above the terrestrial sphere, in the cosmology of ancient Greek philosophy. Alongside the four classical elements of earth, water, air, and fire, aether formed the substance of the heavens.

Origin and mythology

In Greek mythology, Aether was a primordial deity, the personification of the bright upper air breathed by the gods, as opposed to the ordinary air (ἀήρ) breathed by mortals. In Hesiod's Theogony, Aether was the son of Erebus and Nyx (Darkness and Night) and the brother of Hemera (Day).

Aristotle's fifth element

Aristotle added aether as a fifth element in his On the Heavens. The four terrestrial elements were subject to generation and corruption and moved naturally in straight lines; the heavens, by contrast, appeared eternal and unchanging, and the stars moved in perfect circles. Aristotle therefore reasoned that the celestial region must be made of a different substance — aether — whose natural motion was circular and which was neither heavy nor light, ungenerated and imperishable.

Medieval and later use

Medieval scholastic philosophy adopted the quintessence as the substance of the celestial spheres. Alchemists later used "quintessence" for a concentrated extract or elixir. In the 19th century, physicists revived the word for the hypothetical luminiferous aether, the medium once thought to carry light waves; the Michelson–Morley experiment (1887) found no evidence for it, and Einstein's special relativity made it unnecessary.
