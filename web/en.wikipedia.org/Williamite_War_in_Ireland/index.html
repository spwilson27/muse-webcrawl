Date: March 1689 – 3 October 1691
Location: Ireland
Result: Williamite victory; Treaty of Limerick

Williamite War in Ireland

The Williamite War in Ireland (1689–1691), also known in Irish as Cogadh an Dá Rí ("War of the Two Kings"), was a conflict between supporters of the deposed Catholic king James II of England (the Jacobites) and those of William III (the Williamites), who had seized the English throne in the Glorious Revolution of 1688. It formed part of the wider Nine Years' War between France and the Grand Alliance.

Background

James II had appointed the Catholic Richard Talbot, Earl of Tyrconnell, as Lord Deputy of Ireland, and by 1689 most of Ireland was controlled by Jacobites. After James landed at Kinsale in March 1689 with French troops, the conflict became the main theatre in which his attempt to regain the throne was decided.

Course of the war

Key episodes included the Siege of Derry (1689), in which Protestant defenders held out until relieved; the Battle of Newtownbutler; and William's landing at Carrickfergus in June 1690. On 1 July 1690 (Old Style) William defeated James at the Battle of the Boyne, after which James fled to France. In 1691 the Jacobite army under the Marquis de St Ruth was destroyed at the Battle of Aughrim, the bloodiest battle in Irish history. The war ended with the siege of Limerick and the Treaty of Limerick in October 1691.

Aftermath

The treaty allowed Jacobite soldiers to leave for France (the "Flight of the Wild Geese") and promised Catholics certain protections, but its civil articles were largely repudiated by the Protestant-dominated Irish Parliament, which enacted the Penal Laws. The war cemented Protestant ascendancy in Ireland, and the Boyne victory is still commemorated annually by the Orange Order on 12 July.
