Modernization theory

Modernization theory is a body of social-science theory explaining how societies transition from "traditional" to "modern" forms through economic growth, urbanisation, education, and mass communication, and how such development transforms political and cultural life. Dominant in American academia and policy in the 1950s and 1960s, it framed development policy during the Cold War as an alternative path to that offered by communism.

Key ideas

Economist Walt Rostow's The Stages of Economic Growth (1960) proposed that all societies pass through five stages, from traditional society through "take-off" to an age of high mass consumption. Sociologists such as Talcott Parsons and Daniel Lerner argued that modernisation involved differentiation of institutions and the spread of values — achievement orientation, rationality, empathy — that supported industrial capitalism and eventually liberal democracy. Seymour Martin Lipset proposed that economic development fosters democracy, a claim later refined by scholars of democratisation.

Criticism

From the late 1960s, modernization theory was attacked as ethnocentric and teleological: it treated Western history as the universal template and blamed underdevelopment on the internal characteristics of poor countries. Dependency theory, associated with Raúl Prebisch, Andre Gunder Frank, and Latin American structuralists, argued instead that underdevelopment was produced by the integration of the periphery into a world capitalist system on unequal terms; Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems theory extended this critique. Postcolonial scholars added criticisms of the theory's cultural assumptions.

Later developments

Revived in modified forms — including work on multiple modernities and the persistent statistical association between wealth and democracy — modernization theory remains a reference point in development studies and comparative politics, even as few scholars defend its original unilinear form.
