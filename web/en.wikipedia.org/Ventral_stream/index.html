Ventral stream

The ventral stream is one of two main pathways of the mammalian visual system, the other being the dorsal stream. It runs from the primary visual cortex (V1) in the occipital lobe forward through areas V2 and V4 into the inferior temporal cortex, and is often called the "what pathway" because it is principally concerned with object recognition and form representation.

Function

Proposed by Leslie Ungerleider and Mortimer Mishkin in 1982 from lesion studies in monkeys, the two-streams hypothesis holds that the ventral stream identifies what an object is, while the dorsal stream locates where it is. Cells along the ventral pathway show progressively larger receptive fields and increasingly complex, selective responses — from oriented edges in V1 to faces, hands and objects in inferotemporal cortex, including the fusiform face area in humans. David Milner and Melvyn Goodale later recast the distinction as perception (vision-for-identification) versus action (vision-for-guidance).

Clinical significance

Damage to the ventral stream can produce visual agnosias: patients may see yet fail to recognise objects (apperceptive and associative agnosia), faces (prosopagnosia, associated with fusiform damage), or written words (pure alexia). Colour processing in area V4 relates to cerebral achromatopsia when damaged. Despite the division, the two streams interact extensively, and modern accounts treat them as an interconnected network rather than isolated channels.
