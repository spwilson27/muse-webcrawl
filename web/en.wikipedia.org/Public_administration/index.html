Public administration

Public administration, or public policy and administration, is the academic discipline and field of practice concerned with the implementation of public policy and the management of public agencies and programs. It studies how government decisions are carried out, how public organizations are structured, and how civil servants are recruited and managed.

History

Systematic administration dates to ancient bureaucracies in China (with its examination-based civil service) and Rome. The modern academic field is usually traced to Woodrow Wilson's 1887 essay "The Study of Administration," which argued for a science of administration distinct from politics. Max Weber's analysis of bureaucracy and Frank Goodnow's politics–administration dichotomy shaped early theory. In the United States, the Pendleton Act of 1883 replaced the spoils system with merit-based appointments.

Theories and approaches

Major approaches include the traditional Weberian model of rule-bound hierarchy; New Public Management (1980s–90s), which imported market mechanisms, performance targets, and customer language into government; and the New Public Service and governance frameworks emphasizing networks, collaboration, and citizen engagement. Herbert Simon's critique of classical "proverbs" of administration and his focus on decision-making earned him a central place in the field.

Practice

Public administrators work in government agencies at national, regional, and local levels, and increasingly in nonprofits and contracted organizations delivering public services. Core functions include budgeting (see public finance), human resources, policy analysis, program evaluation, and intergovernmental relations. The Master of Public Administration (MPA) is the standard professional degree.
