Spica

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Observation data
Constellation: Virgo
Right ascension: 13h 25m 11.6s
Declination: −11° 09′ 41″
Apparent magnitude: +1.04
Spectral type: B1 III–IV + B2 V
Distance: ~250 light-years

Spica is the brightest object in the constellation of Virgo and one of the 20 brightest stars in the night sky. It has the Bayer designation α Virginis, Latinised to Alpha Virginis and abbreviated Alpha Vir or α Vir. Analysis of its parallax shows that it is located 250 ± 10 light-years from the Sun. It is a spectroscopic binary and rotating ellipsoidal variable star; a system whose two stars are so close together they are egg-shaped rather than spherical, and can only be separated by their spectra.

Observation

Spica is a bright blue-white star of apparent magnitude about +0.97 to +1.04, varying slightly as the two components orbit. It lies close to the ecliptic and can be occulted by the Moon and, rarely, by planets. A traditional way to locate it is to follow the arc of the handle of the Big Dipper to Arcturus, then "spike" onward to Spica — the mnemonic "arc to Arcturus and spike to Spica." The star's name derives from the Latin spīca virginis, "the virgin's ear of grain," reflecting its depiction as wheat held by Virgo.

Properties

The primary is a blue giant/subgiant of spectral type B1 with a mass around 11 times that of the Sun; the secondary is a B2 main-sequence star of about 7 solar masses. They orbit each other every four days at a separation of only about 0.12 astronomical units, close enough that their mutual tides distort them into ellipsoids, producing the observed brightness variation. The primary is also a Beta Cephei variable, pulsating with a period of about 0.17 days. Spica is a powerful source of X-ray and ultraviolet radiation and illuminates a faint reflection nebula in its vicinity.

History

Spica is believed to be the star whose observations led Hipparchus to discover the precession of the equinoxes around 130 BC, by comparing its longitude with earlier records by Timocharis. Nicolaus Copernicus also observed Spica extensively while developing his heliocentric model. In many cultures the star marked seasonal agricultural events.
