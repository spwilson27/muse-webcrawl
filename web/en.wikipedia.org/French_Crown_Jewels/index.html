The French Crown Jewels (French: Joyaux de la Couronne de France) comprise the crowns, orb, sceptres, diadems, and jewellery that were symbols of royal power between 752 and 1825, together with the many gems and precious pieces added to the collection over the centuries. The collection, begun in earnest by Francis I, who decreed in 1530 that it belonged to the state and could not be alienated, became one of the richest in Europe.

History

The treasury was kept at the Garde-Meuble in Paris. During the French Revolution the jewels were put on public display, then famously stolen in September 1792 in a spectacular burglary; most were recovered, but the great blue diamond known as the French Blue disappeared (it later resurfaced, recut, as the Hope Diamond), and the Sancy diamond's whereabouts were also disrupted. Napoleon and the restored Bourbon and July Monarchy kings added substantially to the collection, and Empress Eugénie's jewels under the Second Empire were celebrated for their splendour.

The 1887 sale

Fearing a royalist restoration — and that the jewels might symbolise it — the Third Republic voted in 1887 to sell the bulk of the collection. The auction dispersed famous pieces around the world; prices were often disappointing because the stones had been broken up from their settings. A few items of exceptional historic or artistic importance were retained, including the Regent Diamond (140.64 carats, considered one of the finest diamonds in the world), the Sancy, the pink Hortensia diamond, the Mazarin cut gems, and the Crown of Louis XV, whose replica stones replaced the originals that were sold.

Today

What remains of the French Crown Jewels is displayed principally in the Galerie d'Apollon of the Louvre Museum in Paris, with scientific specimens held by the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle. Occasional pieces sold in 1887 have since been reacquired by the Louvre through purchase and donation.
