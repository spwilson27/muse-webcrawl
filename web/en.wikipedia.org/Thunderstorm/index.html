Thunderstorm

A thunderstorm, also known as an electrical storm or a thundershower, is a storm characterized by the presence of lightning and its acoustic effect on the Earth's atmosphere, known as thunder. Relatively weak thunderstorms are sometimes called thundershowers. Thunderstorms form in cumulonimbus clouds when warm, moist air rises rapidly in an unstable atmosphere; at any given moment, roughly 2,000 thunderstorms are occurring somewhere on Earth.

Life cycle

A thunderstorm cell passes through three stages: the developing (cumulus) stage, dominated by a rising updraft; the mature stage, when rain begins to fall and downdrafts coexist with updrafts and lightning is most frequent; and the dissipating stage, dominated by the downdraft as precipitation cuts off the updraft. Individual cells typically last 30–60 minutes.

Types

Thunderstorms are classified as single-cell, multicell clusters, squall lines (multicell lines), and supercells. Severe thunderstorms — those producing hail of 2.5 cm or more, winds of 93 km/h or more, or tornadoes — can arise from any type but are most often associated with supercells. Hazards include lightning, flash flooding, large hail, damaging straight-line winds, and tornadoes.

Lightning and thunder

Lightning is a giant electrical discharge caused by charge separation within the cloud, mainly through collisions between ice crystals and graupel. The discharge superheats the air, whose rapid expansion produces thunder. Counting the seconds between the flash and the thunder gives an estimate of distance, since sound travels about 343 m/s.
