Search for extraterrestrial intelligence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Radio telescopes are the primary instruments used in the search for extraterrestrial intelligence.

The search for extraterrestrial intelligence (SETI) is a collective term for scientific searches for intelligent extraterrestrial life, for example, monitoring electromagnetic radiation for signs of transmissions from civilizations on other planets. Scientific investigation began shortly after the advent of radio in the early 1900s, and focused international efforts have been ongoing since the 1980s.

The nature of the search has changed dramatically since the 1960s, growing from the belief that extraterrestrial intelligences were probably common in the Universe to a search in the face of an almost complete lack of evidence of alien life on other planets beyond Earth. Projects use methods including monitoring the sky with radio telescopes, searching for optical signals with telescopes, and analyzing data with distributed computing. The best-known SETI project is SETI@home, launched in 1999, which used the idle processing power of millions of personal computers.

History

Early speculations about life on other planets date back centuries. In 1959, physicists Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison published a paper in Nature arguing that radio telescopes had become sensitive enough to detect transmissions from civilizations around other stars, and suggesting the 21-centimetre hydrogen line as a natural frequency to search. In 1960, astronomer Frank Drake conducted Project Ozma at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory in Green Bank, West Virginia, the first modern SETI experiment, listening to the stars Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani.

Drake also developed the famous Drake equation in 1961, a probabilistic argument used to estimate the number of active, communicative extraterrestrial civilizations in the Milky Way galaxy. In 1977, the Big Ear radio telescope at Ohio State University detected a strong narrowband signal, known as the "Wow! signal", which bore the expected hallmarks of an extraterrestrial origin but was never detected again.

Methods

Radio SETI searches for narrowband signals, which are not produced by known natural processes, using large radio telescopes such as the Allen Telescope Array, the Green Bank Telescope, and the FAST telescope in China. Optical SETI looks for brief pulses of laser light. More recent approaches include searching for technosignatures such as industrial pollution in exoplanet atmospheres, waste heat from Dyson spheres, or anomalous transits.

Organizations and projects

The SETI Institute, founded in 1984, is a nonprofit research organization dedicated to the search. Breakthrough Listen, launched in 2015 with funding from Yuri Milner, is the most comprehensive SETI program to date, surveying the nearest stars and galaxies across a wide range of frequencies. NASA conducted a SETI program until Congress cancelled its funding in 1993, after which the work continued with private funding.
