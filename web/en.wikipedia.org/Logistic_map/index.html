Logistic map

The logistic map is a polynomial mapping of degree 2, often cited as an archetypal example of how complex, chaotic behaviour can arise from very simple nonlinear dynamical equations. It is written xn+1 = r xn(1 − xn), where x is a number between 0 and 1 representing a population ratio and r is a positive parameter representing a combined rate of reproduction and starvation.

Behaviour

For r between 1 and 3 the population settles to a stable fixed point; beyond 3 it oscillates between two values, then four, eight and so on in a period-doubling cascade. The cascade accumulates at about r ≈ 3.57, beyond which chaotic behaviour appears, interspersed with windows of periodicity such as the period-3 window near 3.83. The ratios of the bifurcation intervals converge to the Feigenbaum constant δ ≈ 4.6692, a universal constant for such systems.

History

The map derives from the logistic equation introduced by Pierre François Verhulst in the 19th century as a model of population growth with limited resources. It was popularised by biologist Robert May in a 1976 paper in Nature and by Mitchell Feigenbaum's work on universality, becoming a foundational example in chaos theory.
