Prescriptive linguistics (or prescriptivism) is the practice of establishing and promoting rules and norms for how a language should be used, as opposed to descriptive linguistics, which records how a language is actually used. Prescriptive rules concern grammar, pronunciation, spelling, vocabulary and style.

History

Prescriptive traditions are ancient: Pāṇini's grammar of Sanskrit set norms in the first millennium BC, and Arabic and Latin grammarians codified prestige standards. In English, the 18th century saw influential grammars such as Robert Lowth's A Short Introduction to English Grammar (1762), which introduced or popularised proscriptions still debated today, including rulings on split infinitives and sentence-final prepositions.

Functions and criticism

Prescription supports standardisation, which aids education, publishing and communication across dialect regions, and underlies style guides and usage manuals. Academic linguists, however, generally argue that prescriptive rules often rest on aesthetics, class prestige or false analogies with Latin rather than linguistic evidence, and that language change is natural; they distinguish describing standard usage from condemning nonstandard dialects as "incorrect".

Examples of contested rules

Frequently debated prescriptions include the prohibitions on split infinitives and stranded prepositions, the differentiation of "less" and "fewer", and the status of singular "they"—the last now widely accepted by major style guides.
