Walkie-talkie

A walkie-talkie, more formally a handheld transceiver (HT), is a portable two-way radio that can both transmit and receive voice (and sometimes data) over radio waves. Unlike a mobile phone, a walkie-talkie communicates directly with other radios on the same channel, usually in half-duplex mode with push-to-talk operation, and needs no network infrastructure or subscription.

History

The first device widely called a walkie-talkie was the SCR-300 backpack radio developed for the US Army by Motorola (then Galvin Manufacturing) in 1940, designed by a team led by Daniel E. Noble; it was used extensively in the Second World War. Canadian inventor Donald Hings had earlier developed a portable "packset" for bush pilots. The first truly handheld model, the SCR-536 "handie-talkie", followed in 1941. After the war, two-way radios spread to police, emergency services, construction, and industry, and shrank steadily with transistorisation.

Use and technology

Modern units range from licence-free consumer radios — PMR446 in Europe, FRS/GMRS in the United States — with a range of a few hundred metres to a few kilometres, to professional digital radios (DMR, TETRA, P25) used by public-safety agencies with encryption, GPS, and repeater networks. Typical features include multiple channels, CTCSS/DCS squelch codes, VOX hands-free operation, and rugged, waterproof housings. Range depends on power, antenna, terrain, and frequency band; UHF is favoured for use in and around buildings.

Cultural presence

Walkie-talkies remain standard equipment for event staff, film crews, warehouses, schools, and outdoor recreation, and they are a staple of amateur ("ham") radio, where dual-band handhelds are often an operator's first radio.
