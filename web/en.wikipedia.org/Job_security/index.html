Job security

[Image placeholder: employees at work]

Job security is the probability that an individual will keep their job; a job with a high level of job security is one from which a person is unlikely to be made unemployed. Basic economic theory holds that during periods of economic expansion businesses experience increased demand, profits, and cash reserves, which together increase confidence in hiring and retaining staff; during recessions the opposite tends to occur.

Factors affecting job security

Job security varies widely across countries, industries, and occupations. Government jobs and jobs in heavily unionised industries often carry stronger protections. Education and training levels also matter: more skilled workers generally have more secure employment and better prospects of finding new work if they lose a job. Economic conditions, technological change, and automation can all reduce security in particular sectors, while employment-protection legislation and collective bargaining can increase it.

Job insecurity

Job insecurity is the perceived threat of losing one's job or of deterioration in the quality of one's job. Research in occupational health psychology associates job insecurity with lower job satisfaction, reduced organisational commitment, poorer mental and physical health, and increased workplace accidents. Studies have found that the health effects of chronic job insecurity can rival those of actual job loss.

By country

In the United States, most private-sector employment is "at will", meaning employees can be dismissed for almost any reason not prohibited by law, though public-sector and unionised jobs typically offer more protection. Many European countries have considerably stronger statutory protections against dismissal, and systems such as Germany's Kurzarbeit short-time work scheme are designed to preserve jobs during downturns.
