Ancient Greek medicine

Ancient Greek medicine was a collection of theories and practices that developed from roughly the 5th century BC, moving healing away from purely religious explanation toward observation and reasoning about natural causes. Its most famous figure, Hippocrates of Kos, gave his name to the Hippocratic Corpus and the physician's oath.

Religious and rational healing

Healing sanctuaries of Asclepius, such as Epidaurus, combined ritual incubation with diet and exercise. Alongside temple medicine, Hippocratic writers insisted that diseases have natural causes: the treatise On the Sacred Disease argues that epilepsy is no more divine than any other illness, and works like Airs, Waters, Places linked health to environment and season.

Humoural theory

The dominant framework held that the body contained four humours—blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile—whose balance (eucrasia) meant health and whose imbalance (dyscrasia) meant disease. Treatment aimed at restoring balance through regimen, diet, drugs, bloodletting, and sometimes surgery. The theory, systematised by Galen in the 2nd century AD, governed European medicine for well over a millennium.

Later developments

Hellenistic Alexandria advanced anatomy through dissection by Herophilus and Erasistratus, distinguishing nerves, arteries, and veins. Rival schools—Dogmatists, Empiricists, Methodists—debated the role of theory and experience. Greek medicine passed through Roman, Syriac, and Arabic transmission to shape medieval and Renaissance practice.
