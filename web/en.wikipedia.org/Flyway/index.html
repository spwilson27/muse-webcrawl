A flyway is a flight path used by large numbers of birds while migrating between their breeding grounds and their overwintering sites. Because the same routes are used by many populations, flyways are a useful framework for the conservation and management of migratory birds and the wetlands and other habitats on which they depend.

Major flyways

At the continental scale, ornithologists commonly recognise four great flyways in the Americas — the Pacific, Central, Mississippi and Atlantic flyways — a scheme formalised in the 1930s by Frederick Lincoln of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service for waterfowl management. Globally, nine major flyways are usually listed: the East Atlantic, West Pacific, East Asian–Australasian, Central Asian, West Asian–East African, East Asian–East African (sometimes combined), Mediterranean–Black Sea, Trans-Saharan, and the Americas routes. The East Asian–Australasian Flyway alone supports more than 50 million migratory waterbirds, including species that travel between Arctic Russia and Australia.

Stopover sites

Migrating birds do not fly their routes non-stop; they rely on chains of stopover sites — wetlands, mudflats, estuaries and forests — where they rest and refuel. Many of these sites, such as the Yellow Sea tidal flats used by shorebirds on the East Asian–Australasian Flyway, are bottlenecks where loss of habitat affects entire populations. International frameworks including the Ramsar Convention on wetlands and agreements under the Convention on Migratory Species coordinate protection along flyways.

Threats

Flyway populations face habitat loss at breeding, stopover and wintering sites, hunting, collision with power lines and wind turbines, and climate change, which can shift habitats and desynchronise migration timing from food availability. Diseases such as avian influenza also spread along flyways. Flyway-scale conservation partnerships — for example the East Asian–Australasian Flyway Partnership — aim to manage networks of sites across national borders.
