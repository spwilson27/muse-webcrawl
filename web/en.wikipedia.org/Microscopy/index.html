Microscopy is the technical field of using microscopes to view objects and areas of objects that cannot be seen with the naked eye. It underpins modern biology, medicine, materials science and nanotechnology, and is conventionally divided into optical, electron and scanning probe methods.

Optical microscopy

Compound microscopes, developed around 1600 and refined by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek — who observed bacteria and other microorganisms — use visible light and lenses to magnify specimens, limited by diffraction to a resolution of about 200 nanometres. Variants include bright-field, phase-contrast, polarised-light, fluorescence and confocal microscopy. Super-resolution techniques such as STED and PALM/STORM, recognised by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, break the diffraction limit for light microscopy.

Electron microscopy

Electron microscopes use beams of electrons instead of light. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images thin samples at atomic resolution, while scanning electron microscopy (SEM) produces detailed three-dimensional-looking images of surfaces. Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), honoured by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, revolutionised structural biology by determining the structures of biomolecules in near-native states.

Scanning probe microscopy

Scanning probe microscopes, such as the scanning tunnelling microscope (STM) and atomic force microscope (AFM), scan a sharp tip across a surface to map it, achieving atomic resolution and even permitting manipulation of individual atoms.
