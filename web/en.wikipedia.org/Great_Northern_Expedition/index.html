Great Northern Expedition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Northern Expedition, also known as the Second Kamchatka Expedition (1733–1743), was an enormous Russian surveying enterprise commissioned by the Admiralty under Empress Anna and led by the Danish-born officer Vitus Bering. With some 3,000 participants and a budget consuming a large share of state revenue, it mapped most of the Arctic coast of Siberia, reached Alaska, and systematised knowledge of the North Pacific.

Operations

The expedition worked in several detachments. Naval parties charted the Arctic shoreline from the White Sea to Cape Dezhnev, proving that Asia and America are separated by the strait now named for Bering. The "American" detachment sailed from Kamchatka in 1741: Bering's ship St. Peter reached Alaska but was wrecked on Bering Island on the return, where Bering died; Alexei Chirikov's St. Paul separately reached the Alaskan coast and returned. The naturalist Georg Wilhelm Steller, shipwrecked with Bering, described Steller's sea cow and other species. An academic detachment — including Johann Georg Gmelin, Gerhard Friedrich Müller, and Stepan Krasheninnikov — travelled Siberia studying its peoples, flora, and geography, while other parties explored the Kuril Islands and sailed toward Japan.

Results

The expedition produced the first reliable maps of Siberia's coast and of the North Pacific, documented the Aleutian Islands and Alaska's southern shore, and opened the Russian-American fur trade. Its published maps, notably the 1745 atlas of the Russian Empire, transformed European geography; names such as the Bering Strait, Bering Sea, and Bering Island commemorate its leader.
