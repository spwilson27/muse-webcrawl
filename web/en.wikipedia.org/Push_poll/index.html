Push poll

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A push poll is an interactive marketing technique, most commonly employed during political campaigns, in which a person or organisation attempts to manipulate or alter prospective voters' opinions under the guise of conducting an opinion poll. Unlike genuine polls, push polls are designed not to collect data but to spread negative or misleading information about a candidate or position to a large number of voters.

Characteristics

Push polls typically contact tens of thousands of voters — far more than a scientific sample requires — and ask loaded or suggestive questions, for example whether the respondent would still support a candidate "if you knew" some damaging (often false or unproven) claim. Because the aim is persuasion rather than measurement, results, if any, are not analysed or published, and the sponsor is often concealed behind a plausible-sounding research firm name.

Professional and legal status

Polling organisations such as the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) condemn push polling as unethical and distinguish it from legitimate "message testing" in real polls. Several U.S. states have enacted disclosure laws requiring push poll calls to identify their sponsor, though broad bans have raised free-speech objections.

Notable examples

The term became prominent in the United States during the 2000 Republican presidential primaries, when voters in South Carolina reported calls asking whether they would support John McCain if they knew he had fathered an illegitimate black child — a smear associated with the campaign of George W. Bush, though its authorship was never conclusively established.
