Cyrillic script

The Cyrillic script is a writing system used for various languages across Eurasia, and is the designated national script in various Slavic, Turkic, Mongolic, and Iranic-speaking countries in Eastern Europe, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and North Asia. It was developed in the late 9th century in the First Bulgarian Empire and is traditionally attributed to the disciples of Saints Cyril and Methodius.

History

After the mission of Cyril and Methodius to Great Moravia in 863, their disciples, including Clement of Ohrid, developed a simpler script based largely on Greek uncial letterforms, named in honour of Cyril. It spread from Bulgaria to Kievan Rus' and Serbia. Peter the Great's civil script reform of 1708–1710 regularised the letterforms for Russian printing. Under the Soviet Union, Cyrillic was extended to dozens of non-Slavic languages; after 1991, some states — such as Kazakhstan and Moldova — moved toward Latin alphabets, with mixed progress.

Languages

Cyrillic is official for Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Bulgarian, Serbian, Macedonian, Montenegrin, and Bosnian (alongside Latin), and for many languages of Russia, Central Asia, and Mongolia. National variants differ in their letter inventories: Russian uses 33 letters, Ukrainian 33 with Ґ, Serbian 30, and Bulgarian 30.

Letterforms and typography

Several Cyrillic letters famously resemble Latin letters while having different values (false friends): В = V, Н = N, Р = R, С = S, Х = Kh. Italic and cursive forms can differ markedly from print forms, notably for т, д, and г in Russian handwriting.
