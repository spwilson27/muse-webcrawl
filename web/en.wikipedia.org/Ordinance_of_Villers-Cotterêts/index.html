Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts

The château of Villers-Cotterêts, where Francis I signed the ordinance in 1539.

The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (French: Ordonnance de Villers-Cotterêts) is a legislative decree signed by King Francis I of France at Villers-Cotterêts in August 1539. It is famous for mandating the use of the French language in official documents, and it remains one of the oldest pieces of French legislation still partly in force.

Contents

The ordinance contains 192 articles, most of them concerned with reforming ecclesiastical jurisdiction, curbing abuses, and regulating notaries and civil registration — it required parishes to keep registers of baptisms, an early foundation of the French civil registry. Its best-known provisions, Articles 110 and 111, required that all judgments, contracts, and official acts be pronounced, recorded, and delivered "en langage maternel françois" — in the French mother tongue — rather than in Latin.

Language and the state

The provision was partly a practical measure against the ambiguity of legal Latin, but it also served royal centralisation: imposing the king's French on administration helped bind the realm together and marginalised regional languages such as Occitan, Breton, and Basque over the long term. The ordinance is often cited as a founding moment of French linguistic policy, and its language articles were never repealed; later laws, including the 1992 constitutional amendment declaring French the language of the Republic, continue its tradition.

Other provisions

The ordinance also restricted workers' associations and banned confréries of journeymen, an early step in the regulation of guilds and labour combinations in France.
