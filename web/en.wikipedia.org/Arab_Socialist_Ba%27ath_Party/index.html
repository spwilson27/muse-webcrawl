The Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party (Arabic: حزب البعث العربي الاشتراكي, Ḥizb al-Baʿth al-ʿArabī al-Ishtirākī; "Ba'ath" meaning "resurrection" or "renaissance") was a political party founded in Syria by Michel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, and associates of Zaki al-Arsuzi. The party espoused Ba'athism, an ideology combining Arab nationalist, pan-Arab, and Arab socialist interests. Its motto, "Unity, Freedom, Socialism" (Waḥda, Ḥurriyya, Ishtirākiyya), expressed the goals of a single unified Arab state, freedom from foreign control, and a socialist economic order.

History

The party was formally founded at a congress in Damascus on April 7, 1947, merging Aflaq and al-Bitar's Arab Ba'ath Movement with al-Arsuzi's Arab Ba'ath. In 1952 it merged with Akram al-Hawrani's Arab Socialist Party to form the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party. Branches spread across the Arab world, notably to Iraq.

Ba'athists seized power in Iraq and Syria in 1963 coups. A further Syrian coup in 1966 split the movement into rival Syrian-dominated and Iraqi-dominated parties, each claiming the legacy of the original National Command. The Syrian branch ruled Syria from 1963 until the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024; the Iraqi branch ruled Iraq from 1968 until the 2003 invasion, under Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr and then Saddam Hussein.

Ideology

Aflaq's writings defined Ba'athism around the Arab nation as an eternal entity destined for "resurrection" through unity. The party professed secularism (though Aflaq regarded Islam as a defining expression of the Arab spirit), state-led socialism, and opposition to imperialism and communism alike. In practice, the ruling branches became authoritarian single-party regimes organized around security services and, eventually, family dynasties.
