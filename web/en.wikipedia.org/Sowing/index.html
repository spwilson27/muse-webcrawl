Sowing

Sowing is the process of planting seeds. An area that has had seeds planted in it is described as a sown area. Sowing is one of the oldest agricultural activities, dating to the beginnings of settled farming in the Neolithic period, and the timing, depth and spacing of seeds strongly affect the success of the resulting crop.

Methods

Traditionally, seed was sown by hand, either by broadcasting — scattering seed over a prepared field — or by dibbling seeds into holes at intervals. Hand sowing is simple but uneven and wasteful of seed. Mechanised sowing began with the seed drill, developed in China and popularised in Britain by Jethro Tull in the early eighteenth century; a drill opens furrows, deposits seed at a controlled depth and rate, and covers it, giving even spacing and better germination. Modern precision planters place individual seeds at exact intervals, and in no-till agriculture drills sow directly into untilled soil.

Considerations

Successful sowing depends on sowing at the right season and soil temperature, at a depth appropriate to the seed size (a common rule is two to three times the seed's diameter), with adequate soil moisture and good seed-to-soil contact. Seeds sown too deeply may exhaust their reserves before emerging; seeds too shallow may dry out or be eaten. Seed rates are chosen to give an optimal plant population, and seed may be treated with fungicides or insecticides before sowing.

Cultural significance

Sowing appears frequently in religious and literary imagery. The Parable of the Sower in the New Testament uses seed falling on different soils as a metaphor for responses to teaching, and the phrase "reap what you sow" has become a general proverb about the consequences of one's actions.
