The Raskol (Russian: raskol, "schism") was the splitting of the Russian Orthodox Church in the mid-seventeenth century, when a large body of believers — the Old Believers or Old Ritualists — rejected the liturgical and textual reforms imposed by Patriarch Nikon and refused submission to the official church. The schism, formalised by the councils of 1666–1667, which anathematised the old rites and their adherents, remains unhealed to this day.

The reforms

Beginning in 1653, Nikon, with Tsar Alexis's support, revised Russian service books against Greek originals: the sign of the cross was to be made with three fingers rather than two, the name "Jesus" spelled Iisus instead of Isus, "hallelujah" sung three times instead of two, and processions made to move counter-clockwise. To opponents led by Archpriest Avvakum, such changes to the inherited forms of prayer — believed salvific in themselves — were heresy and a sign of the Antichrist.

Resistance and persecution

The Old Believers ranged from peasants and Cossacks to the boyarynya Feodosia Morozova, whose defiance is depicted in Surikov's famous painting. The fortified Solovetsky Monastery resisted a government siege for eight years (1668–1676). Avvakum, exiled to the Arctic, was burned at the stake in 1682; some communities chose mass self-immolation over submission. The schism divided further into priestly (popovtsy) and priestless (bespopovtsy) branches as the pre-schism clergy died out.

Later history

Peter the Great replaced persecution with punitive double taxation and beard taxes, and the Old Believers became a distinct estate, often prosperous merchants, while remaining outside the state church. Limited toleration came in 1905. In 1971 the Moscow Patriarchate lifted the anathemas on the old rites, declaring them equal in honour, but most Old Believer churches did not return to communion, and the Raskol endures as a defining episode in Russian religious history.
