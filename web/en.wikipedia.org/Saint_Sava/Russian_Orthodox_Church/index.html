The Russian Orthodox Church, also known as the Moscow Patriarchate, is an autocephalous Eastern Orthodox church in communion with other Eastern Orthodox patriarchates. It is the largest of the Orthodox churches and one of the largest Christian denominations in the world, claiming some 100 million adherents, primarily in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and Moldova, as well as a worldwide diaspora.

History

Kievan and Muscovite periods

Christianity came to Kievan Rus' in 988, when Prince Vladimir was baptised and began the Christianisation of his realm under the Patriarchate of Constantinople. After the Mongol devastation of Kyiv, the metropolitan's seat moved north, eventually to Moscow. In 1448 the Russian church effectively became independent, and in 1589 Metropolitan Job became the first Patriarch of Moscow.

Schism and synodal period

The reforms of Patriarch Nikon in the 17th century provoked the Raskol, the schism of the Old Believers. In 1721 Peter the Great abolished the patriarchate and placed the church under a state-controlled Holy Synod. The patriarchate was restored in 1917 under Patriarch Tikhon, only for the church to suffer severe persecution under the Soviet regime, with tens of thousands of clergy and faithful killed and most churches closed or destroyed.

Post-Soviet revival

After 1988, and especially following the fall of the USSR in 1991, the church experienced a major revival: thousands of parishes reopened, monasteries were restored, and the church regained a prominent public role in Russia. Patriarch Alexy II led the church from 1990 to 2008, succeeded by Patriarch Kirill in 2009. In 2018–19, the grant of autocephaly to the Orthodox Church of Ukraine caused a rupture between Moscow and Constantinople.

Structure

The church is governed by the Holy Synod under the Patriarch, with dioceses headed by bishops across Russia and abroad. Its principal cathedral is the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour in Moscow, originally built in the 19th century, demolished in 1931, and rebuilt in the 1990s.
