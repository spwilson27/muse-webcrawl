Byzantine Empire

CapitalConstantinople
Duration330 – 1453
LanguagesLatin (early); Greek (from 7th century)
ReligionEastern Orthodox Christianity
Notable emperorsJustinian I; Heraclius; Basil II; Alexios I Komnenos

The Byzantine Empire, also called the Eastern Roman Empire, was the continuation of the Roman Empire in the Greek-speaking East, centred on Constantinople. Founded when Constantine I made the city his capital in 330 and surviving the fall of the West in the fifth century, it endured for more than a thousand years until the Ottoman conquest of 1453. Its inhabitants called themselves Rhomaioi — Romans — and their state Romania.

Early centuries

Justinian I (r. 527–565) reconquered North Africa, Italy and part of Spain, codified Roman law in the Corpus Juris Civilis, and built Hagia Sophia, but his wars and the Plague of Justinian strained the empire. Heraclius (r. 610–641) defeated Persia only to lose Syria, Palestine and Egypt to the Arab conquests; Greek replaced Latin as the language of government, and the empire reorganised into military districts (themes) and survived repeated Arab sieges of the capital (674–678, 717–718).

Macedonian and Komnenian eras

Under the Macedonian dynasty (867–1056), especially Basil II "the Bulgar-Slayer", the empire recovered territory in the Balkans, Anatolia and Syria and enjoyed a cultural renaissance. The conversion of the Slavs by Cyril and Methodius and of the Rus' (988) extended Byzantine religious influence across Eastern Europe. Defeat by the Seljuk Turks at Manzikert (1071) cost most of Anatolia; the Komnenian emperors partly restored the position with Western crusading help, but the empire never regained its former strength.

Decline and fall

The Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople in 1204 and partitioned the empire among Latin rulers and Venice. Michael VIII Palaiologos recovered the capital in 1261, but the restored empire was a small state beset by Serbs, civil wars and the advancing Ottomans. Constantinople fell to Mehmed II on 29 May 1453; the last emperor, Constantine XI, died in the defence, and the Empire of Trebizond and the Despotate of the Morea followed by 1461.

Legacy

Byzantium preserved Roman law and Greek learning, transmitted them to both the Islamic world and Renaissance Europe, shaped the Orthodox Christianity and alphabets of the Slavs, and remains central to the identities of Greeks, Serbs, Russians and other peoples of south-eastern Europe.
