East–West Schism

Also calledGreat Schism of 1054

The East–West Schism, or Great Schism, was the final break of communion between the Latin Church of the West and the Greek Church of the East, dividing Christianity into the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches. Conventionally dated to 1054, when papal legates and the Patriarch of Constantinople excommunicated one another, the schism was in reality the culmination of centuries of estrangement and was sealed by later events, above all the sack of Constantinople in 1204.

Causes

Political division between the Byzantine Empire and the Latin West, linguistic divergence between Greek and Latin, and differing customs — leavened against unleavened Eucharistic bread, clerical celibacy, fasting rules — hardened over centuries. Two doctrinal-juridical disputes proved decisive: the western addition of the filioque clause to the Nicene Creed, teaching that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son; and the Roman claim to universal jurisdiction, which the East countered with a conciliar model of five patriarchates (the pentarchy).

The events of 1054

In 1054 Pope Leo IX sent legates led by Cardinal Humbert to Constantinople to settle disputes in southern Italy and to answer Patriarch Michael Cerularius's criticisms. On 16 July 1054, during the liturgy in Hagia Sophia, Humbert laid a bull of excommunication against Cerularius on the altar. A Constantinopolitan synod responded by anathematising the legates. Leo IX had in fact died before the legates acted, casting doubt on their authority, and at first few contemporaries treated the quarrel as final.

Consolidation and attempted reunions

Relations continued intermittently, but the Norman conquests, the Crusades and especially the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople (1204) and the imposition of Latin hierarchies made the breach effectively permanent. Imperial reunion councils at Lyons (1274) and Florence (1438–39) were accepted by Byzantine emperors for political survival but rejected by clergy and people at home.

Modern thaw

In 1965 Pope Paul VI and Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras lifted the mutual anathemas of 1054, and popes and patriarchs have met regularly since; theological dialogues continue, though full communion has not been restored. The schism remains the largest division within Christianity by population affected after the Reformation.
