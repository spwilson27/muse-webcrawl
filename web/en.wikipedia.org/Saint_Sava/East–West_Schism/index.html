The East–West Schism, also known as the Great Schism or the Schism of 1054, is the break of communion between the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church, which has lasted since 1054. It was the culmination of centuries of theological and political differences between the Latin West and the Greek East, including disputes over the filioque clause in the Nicene Creed, the use of unleavened bread in the Eucharist, and the authority of the pope of Rome over the other patriarchs.

Background

Divisions within the Church began early: the empire's split into eastern and western halves, language differences between Greek and Latin, and competing claims of Rome and Constantinople all contributed. The Photian schism of the 9th century was an early warning. By the 11th century, Norman conquests in southern Italy brought Latin and Greek churches into direct friction over liturgical practice.

The events of 1054

In 1054, legates of Pope Leo IX, led by Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida, travelled to Constantinople. After disputes with Patriarch Michael I Cerularius, Humbert placed a papal bull of excommunication on the altar of Hagia Sophia on 16 July 1054 — ironically after Leo IX had already died, raising questions about the legates' authority. Cerularius responded by excommunicating the legates. Although the excommunications applied only to individuals, they came to symbolise a permanent rupture, deepened by the sack of Constantinople in the Fourth Crusade (1204).

Aftermath and reconciliation attempts

Attempts at reunion at the Councils of Lyons (1274) and Florence (1438–39) failed amid popular resistance in the East. In 1965, Pope Paul VI and Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras I mutually lifted the excommunications of 1054, a landmark gesture, but full communion has not been restored. Dialogue between the two churches continues.
