Constantinople (Greek: Konstantinoupolis, "City of Constantine") was the capital of the Roman and Byzantine Empire from its dedication in 330 CE until 1453, and thereafter the capital of the Ottoman Empire. Built on the site of the Greek colony of Byzantium, it commanded the straits between Europe and Asia and the trade between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean; today it is Istanbul.

Foundation

The emperor Constantine I chose Byzantium as his new eastern capital — "New Rome" — and dedicated it on 11 May 330. Enlarged walls enclosed seven hills; forums, palaces, the Hippodrome and great churches followed, above all Justinian I's Hagia Sophia (537), the largest cathedral in the world for nearly a thousand years.

Medieval height

Defended by the massive Theodosian Walls, the city withstood sieges by Persians, Avars, Arabs, Rus' and Bulgars, and grew into medieval Christendom's largest and wealthiest city, a centre of trade, scholarship and Orthodox Christianity. Its decline began with the catastrophic sack of 1204 by the Fourth Crusade; recovered by Michael VIII Palaiologos in 1261, the shrunken city-state lingered until the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II conquered it on 29 May 1453 after a 53-day siege, ending the Byzantine Empire.

Ottoman and modern era

As the Ottoman capital, the city — officially Kostantiniyye and popularly Istanbul — was repopulated and adorned with mosques such as the Süleymaniye. The capital moved to Ankara in 1923, and the name Istanbul was made official in 1930.
