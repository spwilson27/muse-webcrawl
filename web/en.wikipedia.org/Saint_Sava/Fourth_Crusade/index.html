Fourth Crusade

Date1202–1204
Called byPope Innocent III
LeadersBoniface of Montferrat; Enrico Dandolo of Venice
Original objectiveRecover Jerusalem via Egypt
OutcomeSack of Constantinople; Latin Empire founded

The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) was a Latin Christian armed expedition called by Pope Innocent III to recover Jerusalem from Muslim control by invading Egypt. Instead, a sequence of debts, diversions and political bargains led the crusaders, transported by Venice, to conquer two Christian cities: Zara (Zadar) in 1202 and the Byzantine capital Constantinople in 1204. The crusade never reached the Holy Land.

The Venetian bargain

By the Treaty of Venice (1201) the republic agreed to transport 33,500 men for 85,000 marks. Far fewer crusaders arrived, and the army could not pay. The blind, elderly doge Enrico Dandolo proposed suspending the debt if the crusaders helped Venice retake Zara from Hungary; despite papal prohibition, the city was stormed in November 1202 and the army excommunicated.

Constantinople

The fugitive Byzantine prince Alexios Angelos then offered 200,000 marks, supplies and 10,000 soldiers if the crusaders restored his father, Isaac II. The fleet reached Constantinople in June 1203 and installed Alexios IV, but he could not fulfil his promises and was murdered in a coup in January 1204. The crusaders resolved to seize the city; after storming it on 12 April 1204 they subjected it to a three-day sack of notorious savagery, then partitioned the Byzantine Empire (Partitio terrarum imperii Romaniae), electing Baldwin of Flanders as Latin Emperor.

Consequences

The sack shattered Byzantium, though Greek successor states at Nicaea, Epirus and Trebizond resisted, and Nicaea recovered the city in 1261. The crusade diverted crusading energy from the Holy Land, enriched Venice with colonies and relics, and entrenched the schism between Latin and Greek Christians for centuries.
