Corn flakes

Corn flakes are a packaged cereal product formed from small toasted flakes of corn, usually seasoned with sugar and salt. They were invented by John Harvey Kellogg and his brother Will Keith Kellogg in Battle Creek, Michigan, in the 1890s, and became the founding product of the Kellogg Company.

History

The flakes emerged from experiments at the Battle Creek Sanitarium, where the Kelloggs served a bland vegetarian diet to patients. According to the best-known account, cooked wheat left standing went stale and, when rolled, produced thin flakes that toasted well; the process was soon adapted to corn. Will Keith Kellogg founded the Battle Creek Toasted Corn Flake Company in 1906, adding sugar to the recipe against his brother's wishes, and built the product into a mass-market success.

Production

Modern corn flakes are made from milled corn grits cooked with flavouring and sweeteners, then rolled into flakes, toasted, and often fortified with vitamins and minerals. They are typically served cold with milk.

Variations

Frosted variants coated with sugar are sold in many markets, along with honey, fruit, and chocolate versions. Generic and store-brand corn flakes replicate the original product widely.
