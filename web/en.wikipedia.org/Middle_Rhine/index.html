Middle Rhine

The Middle Rhine (German: Mittelrhein) is the roughly 120-kilometre section of the River Rhine in Germany between Bingen and Bonn, cutting through the Rhenish Slate Mountains. Famous for its steep vineyard-covered slopes, medieval castles and legends such as the Lorelei, the Upper Middle Rhine Valley between Bingen and Koblenz is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Geography

The river carved its gorge through uplifted Devonian slate, creating a narrow valley with few crossing points. Notable features include the Bingen Hole rapids, the Lorelei rock near Sankt Goarshausen, and the confluences of the Nahe, Moselle (at Koblenz's Deutsches Eck), Lahn and Ahr. Towns include Bingen, Bacharach, Oberwesel, Sankt Goar, Boppard, Koblenz, Andernach, Remagen and Bonn.

History and culture

The valley was a Roman frontier zone and later a densely fortified corridor: more than forty castles and fortresses — Marksburg, Burg Pfalzgrafenstein, Burg Katz and others — line the heights, many built to levy tolls on river trade. In the nineteenth century the Rhine Gorge became the quintessential landscape of the Romantic movement, inspiring poets such as Heinrich Heine, whose Lorelei poem became one of Germany's best-known songs, and painters including J. M. W. Turner.

Wine and economy

The Middle Rhine is also one of Germany's thirteen wine regions (Mittelrhein), producing mainly Riesling from terraced slate vineyards among the steepest in the world. Tourism, river cruises and shipping remain economically important; the Rhine is one of Europe's busiest inland waterways, though the gorge's curves and shallows constrain vessel size.
