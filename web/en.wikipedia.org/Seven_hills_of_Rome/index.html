Seven hills of Rome

The hills on the east bank of the Tiber formed the core of ancient Rome.

The seven hills of Rome are the hills east of the river Tiber around which the ancient city of Rome grew: the Palatine, Aventine, Capitoline, Quirinal, Viminal, Esquiline and Caelian hills. Together they occupied the heart of the ancient city and remain woven into its geography and mythology.

The hills

The Palatine, traditionally the site of Rome's founding by Romulus, became the location of the imperial palaces — the word "palace" itself derives from its name. The Capitoline, the smallest of the seven, held the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus and served as the citadel and religious centre of the city. The Aventine was associated with the plebeians and later with temples of foreign cults, while the Quirinal, Viminal and Esquiline formed a ridge of residential districts to the northeast; the Esquiline also served as a burial ground before becoming gardens and elite housing. The Caelian, southeast of the Colosseum valley, was a wealthy residential quarter.

History and significance

The separate hilltop settlements are thought to have merged during the early first millennium BC, a union remembered in the festival of the Septimontium, celebrated on 11 December for the inhabitants of the seven montes. The Servian Wall later enclosed the hills within a single circuit. "City of Seven Hills" became an enduring epithet for Rome, and the phrase has been applied to many other cities built on seven hills, from Istanbul to Lisbon.
