Sodium fluoride is an inorganic compound with the formula NaF. It is a colourless or white solid that is a source of the fluoride ion in diverse applications. It is less expensive and less hygroscopic than the related salt potassium fluoride. Sodium fluoride occurs naturally as the rare mineral villiaumite.

Structure and properties

NaF crystallises in the cubic rock-salt (sodium chloride) structure, in which Na+ and F− ions occupy octahedral coordination sites. It is moderately soluble in water and only slightly soluble in most organic solvents. Like other fluorides, it reacts with strong acids to release hydrogen fluoride; in water it hydrolyses slightly to give a mildly basic solution.

Production

Sodium fluoride is prepared by neutralising hydrofluoric acid with sodium carbonate or sodium hydroxide. Much of it is obtained as a by-product of the manufacture of superphosphate fertiliser, in which fluorapatite rock treated with sulfuric acid generates fluorosilicic acid that can be converted to NaF.

Uses

Fluoride salts are used in water fluoridation and in dental products such as toothpaste to prevent dental caries; fluoride promotes remineralisation of enamel and formation of acid-resistant fluorapatite. Sodium fluoride is also used as a cleaning agent ("laundry sour"), in metallurgy and glass etching, in the manufacture of other fluorides, and historically as an insecticide and wood preservative. In medical imaging, fluorine-18-labelled sodium fluoride is used in PET bone scans.

Safety

In high doses sodium fluoride is toxic: the estimated lethal dose for an adult is on the order of 5–10 grams, acting through binding of calcium and magnesium ions and inhibition of enzymes such as enolase. Chronic excessive intake causes dental and skeletal fluorosis. Ordinary exposure from fluoridated water and toothpaste is regarded by health authorities as safe and beneficial, though recommendations emphasise supervising young children's toothpaste use.
