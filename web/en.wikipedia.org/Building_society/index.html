Building society

A building society is a financial institution owned by its members as a mutual organisation. Building societies offer banking and related financial services, especially savings accounts and mortgage lending. They originated in 18th-century Britain, where groups of workers pooled savings so that each member in turn could buy or build a house; when all members were housed, early "terminating" societies wound up, while later "permanent" societies continued indefinitely.

History and structure

The first recorded building society was Ketley's, founded in Birmingham in 1775. Legislation beginning with the Building Societies Act 1874 gave the movement a formal framework, and societies became central to financing Britain's suburban housing growth. Unlike banks owned by shareholders, a mutual building society is owned by its savers and borrowers, who have voting rights — typically one member, one vote — and profits are retained or returned through better rates rather than dividends.

Demutualisation and today

From 1989, beginning with Abbey National, many large British societies converted to banks ("demutualised"), often distributing windfall shares to members; several of these, such as Northern Rock and Halifax (HBOS), later failed or were absorbed during the 2008 financial crisis. Societies that remained mutual, such as Nationwide — the largest — argue the model delivers long-term member value. Building societies are also found in Ireland, Australia, New Zealand and other Commonwealth countries.
