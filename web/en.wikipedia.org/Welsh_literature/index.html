Welsh literature is the literature written in the Welsh language and, in modern usage, Welsh writing in English. It is one of the oldest continuous literary traditions in Europe: Welsh-language poetry survives from the 6th century, in the work of Taliesin and Aneirin, and the great prose tales of the Mabinogion were composed in the Middle Ages.

Medieval literature

The early period produced heroic and elegiac poetry attributed to the Cynfeirdd ("earliest poets"), including the Gododdin. In the later Middle Ages the Gogynfeirdd, court poets of the Welsh princes, were succeeded by the poets of the gentry, of whom Dafydd ap Gwilym (fl. 1340–1370) is regarded as the greatest Welsh poet. Medieval prose includes the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, native tales, laws, and chronicles.

Early modern and modern periods

William Morgan's Welsh translation of the Bible (1588) standardised literary Welsh and is credited with helping the language survive. Hymn-writers such as William Williams Pantycelyn led an 18th-century religious revival in verse. The 19th century saw the growth of the eisteddfod and Welsh-language periodicals.

Welsh writing in English

In the 20th century a distinct tradition of Welsh writing in English emerged, including the poetry and stories of Dylan Thomas, the industrial novels of the South Wales Valleys, and the poetry of R. S. Thomas, who wrote in English while championing the Welsh language.
