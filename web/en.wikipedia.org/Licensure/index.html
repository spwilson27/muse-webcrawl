Licensure

Licensure is the process by which a governmental or government-authorised agency grants permission to an individual to engage in a given occupation after verifying that the applicant has attained the minimal degree of competency required to ensure the public's health, safety, and welfare. A licence is a time-limited, revocable privilege rather than a right.

Purpose and scope

Licensure is the most restrictive form of occupational regulation: practising without a licence is unlawful. It is applied to professions where incompetence poses substantial risk, including medicine, nursing, law, engineering, architecture, pharmacy, teaching, and accountancy. Requirements typically combine accredited education, supervised experience, examinations, background checks, and continuing education.

Administration

In the United States, licensure is primarily a state function, administered by licensing boards composed partly of practitioners. Well-known examinations include the USMLE for physicians, the NCLEX for nurses, state bar examinations for attorneys, and the Fundamentals of Engineering exam leading toward the Professional Engineer (PE) licence. Similar systems operate internationally, though in many countries professional associations or national ministries play a larger role.

Criticism

Economists and policymakers debate licensure's costs: it can restrict labour supply, raise prices, hinder interstate mobility, and disproportionately burden immigrants and low-income workers. Reforms include universal licence recognition between states, sunrise reviews of proposed licences, and substituting certification or registration for full licensure in lower-risk occupations. Notably, Milton Friedman argued that licensure mainly serves practitioners' economic interests.
