Social isolation

Social isolation is a state of complete or near-complete lack of contact between an individual and society. It differs from loneliness, which is a subjective feeling of being alone: a person can be socially isolated without feeling lonely, and lonely while surrounded by people. Social isolation can affect individuals of all ages, though symptoms may differ by age group.

Causes

Contributing factors include bereavement, retirement, disability and chronic illness, mobility problems, unemployment, domestic violence, and geographic remoteness. Life transitions such as moving to a new community or the loss of a spouse are common triggers, particularly among older adults.

Health effects

Research links chronic social isolation to increased risks of depression, anxiety, dementia, cardiovascular disease, and premature mortality. A widely cited meta-analysis by Holt-Lunstad and colleagues (2010) found that stronger social relationships were associated with a 50% increased likelihood of survival, an effect comparable to quitting smoking. Isolation is also associated with poorer sleep, weakened immune function, and cognitive decline.

Measurement and interventions

Scales such as the Lubben Social Network Scale and the UCLA Loneliness Scale are used to assess isolation and loneliness. Interventions include befriending services, community groups, "social prescribing" by health professionals, and technology-based contact — though the COVID-19 pandemic, which forced widespread physical isolation, heightened concern that digital substitutes are only partial remedies.
