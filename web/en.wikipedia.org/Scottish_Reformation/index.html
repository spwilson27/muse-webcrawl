Scottish Reformation

John Knox, the leading figure of the Scottish Reformation.

The Scottish Reformation was the process by which Scotland broke with the Papacy and developed a predominantly Calvinist national Kirk (the Church of Scotland) in the mid-16th century. It culminated in 1560, when the Parliament of Scotland renounced papal authority, forbade the Mass, and adopted a Protestant confession of faith.

Background

Criticism of the late medieval Scottish church — over clerical wealth, pluralism, and moral failings — combined with the spread of Lutheran and then Calvinist ideas from continental Europe. Early reformers such as Patrick Hamilton were burned for heresy in 1528. Protestantism became entangled with politics: opposition to the French alliance and to the regency of Mary of Guise, mother of Mary, Queen of Scots, gave the reform movement armed noble backing in the Lords of the Congregation.

The Reformation Parliament

John Knox, returned from exile in Geneva in 1559, preached the movement into being. With English military support under the Treaty of Berwick, the reformers prevailed, and the Parliament of August 1560 adopted the Scots Confession, drafted by Knox and five colleagues. Knox's First Book of Discipline proposed a national programme of parish churches and schools, though its financial provisions were only partly implemented.

Aftermath

The return of the Catholic Mary, Queen of Scots in 1561 set up decades of tension, resolved only after her forced abdication in 1567. The Kirk gradually developed its presbyterian polity of kirk sessions, presbyteries, and general assemblies, in recurring conflict with the crown over episcopacy — a struggle that continued into the covenanting wars of the 17th century. The Reformation reshaped Scottish education, worship, and national identity.
