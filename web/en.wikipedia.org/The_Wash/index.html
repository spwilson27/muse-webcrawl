The Wash

LocationEast coast of England, between Norfolk and Lincolnshire
TypeBay / estuary
RiversWitham, Welland, Nene, Great Ouse
AreaApprox. 620 km² of intertidal flats
DesignationSite of Special Scientific Interest; Ramsar site; National Nature Reserve

The Wash is a shallowly square-mouthed bay and estuary in the northwest corner of East Anglia on the east coast of England, where Norfolk meets Lincolnshire. It is one of Britain's broadest estuaries, fed by the rivers Witham, Welland, Nene and Great Ouse, and is formed by a network of tidal channels cutting through vast flats of sand and mud exposed at low water.

Geography

The Wash extends roughly 20 miles (32 km) inland from the North Sea between Gibraltar Point in Lincolnshire and Hunstanton in Norfolk. Its giant tidal ranges leave enormous intertidal mudflats that have been progressively reclaimed over centuries, so that the modern coastline is largely a line of artificial sea walls; the lost Roman-era coastline lay well inland, near the fen edge towns of King's Lynn, Boston, Wisbech, and Spalding.

Wildlife

The Wash is one of Europe's most important feeding areas for waders and wildfowl, supporting internationally significant numbers of birds such as knot, oystercatcher, bar-tailed godwit, and pink-footed geese. Large areas are protected as national nature reserves, including the RSPB reserves at Snettisham and Frampton Marsh and the reserve at Gibraltar Point. Common seals haul out on the sandbanks.

History and legend

Tradition holds that King John lost part of the Crown Jewels in 1216 when his baggage train was caught by the rising tide crossing the Wash near Sutton Bridge, an event recorded by chroniclers though the treasure has never been found. During the Second World War the estuary and its shores were heavily defended. The Wash is also crossed by the outfall channels of the fenland rivers, whose drainage reshaped the region from the seventeenth century onward.
