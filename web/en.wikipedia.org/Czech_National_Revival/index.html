Czech National Revival

The National Theatre in Prague, opened in 1881, was built with nationwide public donations under the slogan "The nation to itself".

The Czech National Revival (Czech: České národní obrození) was a cultural, linguistic and ultimately political movement of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries that sought to revive the Czech language, culture and national identity within the Habsburg monarchy. After the Battle of White Mountain (1620) and the ensuing recatholicisation and Germanisation, Czech had retreated to the countryside and the peasantry while German dominated government, higher education and urban life. The revival reversed that decline and laid the foundations of the modern Czech nation.

Phases and figures

Historians conventionally divide the revival into phases. The defensive phase (1770s–1805) saw scholars such as Gelasius Dobner and Josef Dobrovský — whose German-language grammar of Czech appeared in 1809 — study the language historically. The offensive phase (1805–1840s) produced Josef Jungmann's monumental five-volume Czech–German dictionary (1834–39), translations of world literature proving Czech could carry high culture, and František Palacký's great History of the Czech Nation, the movement's historical charter. Pavel Jozef Šafárik's work on Slavic antiquities placed the Czechs within a broader Slavic awakening.

Cultural populists such as the playwright Josef Kajetán Tyl (author of the song that became the Czech anthem, "Kde domov můj") and the novelist Božena Němcová, whose Babička (The Grandmother, 1855) idealised Czech rural life, carried the revival beyond scholars to the broader public. Karel Havlíček Borovský forged a combative Czech political journalism before his exile to Brixen.

Institutions and politics

The revival built durable institutions: the National Museum (1818), the Matice česká publishing foundation (1831), Czech-language schooling networks, and the Sokol gymnastics movement (1862), which married physical culture to national consciousness. Crowning the era, the National Theatre was funded by public subscription across the Czech lands. Politically, the movement entered the revolutions of 1848 (the Prague Slavic Congress, chaired by Palacký), then organised through the National Party of the Old Czechs and later the more radical Young Czechs, pressing for Bohemian state rights and Czech–German equality inside Austria-Hungary.

Legacy

By 1900 Czech had regained parity in Bohemian public life, Charles University had been split into Czech and German institutions (1882), and a mass national movement existed that would underpin the creation of Czechoslovakia in 1918. Critics then and since have debated the revival's myths — including the forged Královédvorský and Zelenohorský manuscripts, celebrated as ancient Czech epics before being exposed — but its achievement in transforming a peasant vernacular into a complete modern literary language is regarded as one of Europe's most successful national revivals.
