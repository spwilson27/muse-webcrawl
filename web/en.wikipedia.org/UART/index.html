UART

A universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter (UART) is a computer hardware device for asynchronous serial communication in which the data format and transmission speeds are configurable. It sends data bits one by one, from the least significant to the most significant, framed by start and stop bits so that precise timing is handled by the communication channel.

Operation

The UART converts bytes of data from parallel form (from a CPU or peripheral) into a serial bit stream for transmission, and reassembles received serial bits into parallel bytes. Each frame typically consists of a start bit, five to nine data bits, an optional parity bit, and one or more stop bits. The transmitter and receiver must agree in advance on the bit rate (baud rate), data format, and parity.

History

Early UARTs were discrete devices; Gordon Bell of DEC designed early UART circuits in the 1960s. Western Digital's WD1402A, introduced around 1971, is often cited as an early single-chip UART. Later popular devices included the 8250 and 16550 UARTs used in IBM PC serial ports; the 16550 added FIFO buffers.

Applications

UARTs are used in RS-232 serial ports, GPS modules, Bluetooth and microcontroller interfaces, modems, and embedded debugging consoles. Related synchronous/asynchronous devices are called USARTs.
