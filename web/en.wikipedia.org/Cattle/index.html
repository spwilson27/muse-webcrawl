Cattle (Bos taurus) are large, domesticated, bovid ungulates widely kept as livestock. They are prominent modern members of the subfamily Bovinae and the most widespread species of the genus Bos. Females are called cows and males bulls; cattle are raised for their meat (beef and veal), milk and dairy products, leather, and as draught animals.

Domestication

Cattle descend from the wild aurochs (Bos primigenius), domesticated in two main events: one in the Fertile Crescent about 10,500 years ago, giving rise to taurine cattle, and another in the Indus Valley about 8,000 years ago, giving rise to zebu (indicine) cattle with their characteristic shoulder humps. The last wild aurochs died in Poland in 1627.

Biology

Cattle are ruminants with a four-chambered stomach — the rumen, reticulum, omasum and abomasum — adapted to fermenting plant material. They typically weigh between 500 and 1,000 kg depending on breed and sex, and can live for 18–22 years, though production animals are usually slaughtered or culled much earlier. Cattle have a gestation period of about nine months and normally give birth to a single calf.

Husbandry and breeds

Hundreds of breeds have been developed for different climates and purposes, including the Holstein Friesian for milk, the Aberdeen Angus and Hereford for beef, the Brahman for heat tolerance, and the Highland for hardiness. Cattle farming ranges from extensive pastoralism on rangelands to intensive feedlot and housed dairy systems.

Economic and environmental impact

Livestock products supply a large share of the world's calories and protein, and cattle are a major source of livelihoods worldwide. Cattle production is also a significant source of greenhouse-gas emissions, particularly methane from enteric fermentation, and of land-use change, making it a focus of environmental debate.
