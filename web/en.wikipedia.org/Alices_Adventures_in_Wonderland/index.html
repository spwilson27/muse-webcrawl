Alice's Adventures in Wonderland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Author: Lewis Carroll

Illustrator: John Tenniel

Published: 1865

Genre: Children's fantasy; literary nonsense

Followed by: Through the Looking-Glass (1871)

Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (also known simply as Alice in Wonderland) is an 1865 English children's novel by Lewis Carroll, the pen name of Charles Lutwidge Dodgson. It tells of a young girl named Alice who falls through a rabbit hole into a fantasy world populated by peculiar, anthropomorphic creatures. The tale is famous for its playful logic, wordplay and nonsense verse, and it is considered one of the finest examples of the literary nonsense genre.

Origins and plot

The story originated on a boat trip on the Thames on 4 July 1862, when Dodgson entertained Alice Liddell and her sisters with an improvised tale; he later gave Alice a handwritten version, Alice's Adventures Under Ground, before expanding it for publication with illustrations by John Tenniel. In the novel, Alice follows the White Rabbit down a rabbit hole and encounters the Caterpillar, the Cheshire Cat, the Mad Hatter's tea party, the Duchess, and the Queen of Hearts, whose croquet game and trial of the Knave of Hearts form the climax — before Alice wakes and realises it was all a dream.

Themes and influence

Filled with parodies of Victorian schoolroom verse, mathematical and logical jokes, and satire of the adult world's conventions, the book delighted both children and adults. It has never been out of print, has been translated into well over a hundred languages, and has inspired countless adaptations for stage, film and other media, most famously Disney's 1951 animated film. Its sequel, Through the Looking-Glass, and What Alice Found There (1871), introduced the Jabberwock and Tweedledum and Tweedledee. Phrases such as "mad as a hatter", "Cheshire cat grin" and "down the rabbit hole" have passed into everyday English.
