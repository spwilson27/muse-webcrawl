Information behaviour

Information behaviour is a field of information science that studies how people need, seek, manage, give and use information in different contexts — including work, scholarship, health and everyday life. The term encompasses the totality of human behaviour in relation to sources and channels of information, including both active and passive information seeking and information use.

Key concepts

T. D. Wilson, a central figure in the field, defined information behaviour as the totality of human behaviour in relation to sources and channels of information, and distinguished it from narrower terms: information seeking behaviour (the purposive search for information to satisfy a goal), information searching behaviour (interactions with retrieval systems) and information use behaviour (incorporating found information into one's knowledge base). An information need arises when a person recognises a gap or anomaly in their state of knowledge, a notion developed in Robert Taylor's question-negotiation model and Nicholas Belkin's "anomalous state of knowledge" (ASK) hypothesis.

Models

Several influential models describe the process. Brenda Dervin's sense-making approach treats information seeking as bridging a gap in a situation. Carol Kuhlthau's information search process model, derived from studies of students, describes stages — initiation, selection, exploration, formulation, collection and presentation — accompanied by characteristic feelings from uncertainty to confidence. David Ellis proposed behavioural characteristics such as starting, chaining, browsing, differentiating, monitoring and extracting. Wilson's models of 1981 and 1996 situate seeking within personal, social and environmental contexts and include intervening variables and activating mechanisms drawn from stress/coping and risk/reward theory.

Everyday life information seeking

Research by Reijo Savolainen and others extended the field beyond professional and academic users to everyday life information seeking (ELIS), examining how people orient themselves through hobbies, consumption, health concerns and local news. Related phenomena include information avoidance, serendipitous information encountering, information sharing and collaborative information behaviour in groups and organisations.
