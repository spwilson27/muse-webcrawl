Aphid

Aphids are small sap-sucking insects in the superfamily Aphidoidea, within the order Hemiptera. Common names include greenfly and blackfly. About 5,000 species have been described, many of them serious pests of agriculture and horticulture because they weaken plants by feeding on sap and transmit plant viruses.

Biology

Aphids have soft, pear-shaped bodies, usually 1 to 10 millimetres long, with long antennae and two tubes called cornicles projecting from the abdomen. They feed through piercing mouthparts (stylets) on the phloem sap of plants. Excess sugar is excreted as honeydew, which attracts ants; many ant species farm aphids, protecting them from predators in return for honeydew.

Reproduction

Aphids have complex life cycles that often alternate between asexual and sexual generations. In spring and summer, females typically reproduce by parthenogenesis, giving birth to live daughters that are already pregnant — a telescoping of generations that allows explosive population growth. As conditions worsen or day length shortens, winged forms and sexual generations appear.

Control

Natural predators such as ladybirds, lacewing larvae, hoverfly larvae and parasitoid wasps keep aphid numbers in check. Gardeners also use water jets, insecticidal soaps and selective insecticides, though aphids readily evolve resistance.
