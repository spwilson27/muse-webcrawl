History of Schleswig-Holstein

The history of Schleswig-Holstein, Germany's northernmost state, centers on the centuries-long intertwining of the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein and the famous "Schleswig-Holstein question" — the complex dispute between Danish and German claims that twice led to war in the 19th century.

Early history

The region between the Eider River and the Danish borderlands was contested between Danes, Saxons, and Franks from the early medieval period. By the Treaty of Ribe in 1460, the Danish king Christian I was elected duke of Schleswig and count of Holstein, promising that the two territories should remain "forever undivided" (op ewig ungedeelt) — though Schleswig was a Danish fief while Holstein belonged to the Holy Roman Empire.

The Schleswig-Holstein question

As nationalism grew in the 19th century, Danish liberals sought to incorporate Schleswig into Denmark while German nationalists demanded a united German Schleswig-Holstein. The dispute caused the First Schleswig War (1848–1851), which ended inconclusively, and the Second Schleswig War of 1864, in which Prussia and Austria defeated Denmark. By the Treaty of Vienna (1864) Denmark ceded both duchies.

Prussian province to modern state

After the Austro-Prussian War of 1866, Schleswig-Holstein became a Prussian province. Following World War I, the 1920 Schleswig plebiscites divided Schleswig: the north voted to join Denmark, the south to remain German, establishing the current border. After World War II, the British occupation authorities created the state (Land) of Schleswig-Holstein in 1946, which joined the Federal Republic of Germany in 1949.
