Hiawatha (also known as Ayenwatha, Haiëñ'wa'tha, or in Onondaga Ha-yo-went'-ha) was a Native American leader and co-founder of the Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee). According to Iroquois oral tradition, he joined the Great Peacemaker, Deganawida, in persuading the warring Five Nations — the Mohawk, Onondaga, Oneida, Cayuga and Seneca — to unite under the Great Law of Peace.

Traditional account

In the founding narrative, Hiawatha was an Onondaga (in some versions Mohawk) chief grieving the deaths of his daughters, killed through the sorcery of the Onondaga chief Tadodaho. The Peacemaker consoled him with strings of wampum — the origin of the condolence ceremony central to Haudenosaunee governance — and together they carried the message of peace from nation to nation. Hiawatha's eloquence won over the nations one by one, and he "combed the snakes" from Tadodaho's hair, converting the last and most formidable opponent; Tadodaho became the presiding chief of the Grand Council at Onondaga. The confederacy's founding is dated by scholars variously from the 12th to the 16th century.

Historical evidence

The traditions were recorded from the 18th century onward, notably by Lewis Henry Morgan and in the versions collected by Horatio Hale and J. N. B. Hewitt. The wampum belt known as the Hiawatha Belt, with its pattern of linked squares and a central pine tree, symbolizes the union of the Five Nations and remains the basis of the Haudenosaunee flag. Modern Haudenosaunee regard Hiawatha as a historical statesman whose work underpins their continuing confederacy.

In popular culture

Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's epic poem The Song of Hiawatha (1855) borrowed the name but drew its stories largely from Ojibwe legends of the culture hero Nanabozho, gathered by Henry Rowe Schoolcraft — a conflation often criticized but enormously influential on American place names and imagery.
