Cubism is an early-20th-century avant-garde art movement pioneered by Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque in Paris. It revolutionised European painting and sculpture by abandoning single-point perspective and depicting subjects from multiple viewpoints at once, breaking objects into geometric planes. The name arose after the critic Louis Vauxcelles described Braque's 1908 landscapes as composed of "cubes".

Phases

In its first, "Analytical" phase (c. 1908–1912), Picasso and Braque worked in near-monochrome, dissecting forms into overlapping, fragmented facets that hover at the edge of legibility. The later "Synthetic" phase (from about 1912) reintroduced brighter colour, simpler shapes and found materials: Picasso's Still Life with Chair Caning (1912) incorporated oilcloth and rope, and the artists developed collage and papier collé. Influences included Cézanne's geometric treatment of nature, African tribal masks and Iberian sculpture.

Artists and influence

Other major Cubists included Juan Gris, Fernand Léger, Robert and Sonia Delaunay, Albert Gleizes and Jean Metzinger, whose 1912 treatise Du "Cubisme" was the movement's first theoretical text. Cubism profoundly shaped subsequent modern art, feeding into Futurism, Suprematism, De Stijl, Abstract art and modern architecture and design.
