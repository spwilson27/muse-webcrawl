Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Fourth Amendment is part of the Bill of Rights, ratified in 1791.

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures and requires that warrants be supported by probable cause. It is part of the Bill of Rights and was adopted in response to the abuse of general warrants and writs of assistance in the colonial era, which had allowed British officials to search homes and businesses at will.

The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.

Scope

The Amendment applies to government action, not to searches by private individuals. A "search" occurs when the government violates a person's reasonable expectation of privacy, or physically intrudes on a constitutionally protected area to gather information. A "seizure" of property is a meaningful interference with a possessory interest, while a seizure of a person occurs when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave — including arrests and brief investigative stops.

The warrant requirement and exceptions

Searches conducted without a warrant are presumptively unreasonable, but the Supreme Court has recognized many exceptions, including consent searches, searches incident to arrest, the automobile exception, plain view, exigent circumstances, and brief "stop and frisk" detentions supported by reasonable suspicion (Terry v. Ohio, 1968). Arrests inside a home generally require a warrant.

Remedies

The principal remedy for a violation is the exclusionary rule, under which evidence obtained illegally — and evidence derived from it, the "fruit of the poisonous tree" — is inadmissible in criminal proceedings. The rule was applied to federal cases in Weeks v. United States (1914) and incorporated against the states in Mapp v. Ohio (1961). Landmark decisions also include Katz v. United States (1967), establishing the reasonable-expectation-of-privacy test, and Riley v. California (2014), requiring a warrant to search the contents of a mobile phone seized during an arrest.
