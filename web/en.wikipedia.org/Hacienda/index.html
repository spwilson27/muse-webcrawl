Hacienda

A hacienda (Spanish: [aˈsjenda] or [aˈθjenda]) is an estate — most often in the former Spanish Empire — originating as a large landholding worked for agriculture, livestock raising, mining, or manufacturing under a single owner, the hacendado. Haciendas were the dominant rural institution in much of colonial and post-colonial Latin America and the Philippines.

History

The system grew out of Spanish land grants and the encomienda, evolving in the 17th and 18th centuries into self-sufficient estates with a great house, chapel, workers' housing, stores, and workshops. Labour was supplied by resident workers (peones) often bound by debt peonage, and in some regions by enslaved people. Haciendas produced crops such as wheat, sugar, coffee, henequen, and wine, and raised cattle on vast ranges.

Decline

Land reform movements in the 20th century — above all the Mexican Revolution and the agrarian reforms that redistributed hacienda land to communal ejidos, and similar programmes in Bolivia, Peru, Chile, and elsewhere — dismantled most of the great estates. Many surviving hacienda buildings have been converted into hotels, museums, and event venues. Comparable estates are called fazendas in Brazil and estancias in the Southern Cone.
