Caravanserai

A caravanserai (also caravansary or caravansera; from Persian kārwānsarā, "palace of caravans") was a roadside inn where travellers — caravans of merchants, pilgrims and couriers — could rest and recover from the day's journey. Caravanserais supported the flow of commerce, information and people across the trade routes of Asia, North Africa and South-Eastern Europe, especially along the Silk Road, the Royal Road of Persia and the routes of the Ottoman and Mughal empires.

Architecture

A typical caravanserai was a square or rectangular walled enclosure with a single monumental gateway, wide and tall enough for loaded camels to pass, that could be barred at night. Around an open courtyard ran arcades, stables and raised platforms where merchants unloaded goods, together with small cells for lodging, a prayer room and a bath. A well or cistern supplied water, and fodder and provisions were sold. Larger imperial foundations, such as the Seljuk hans of Anatolia — the great Sultan Han near Aksaray (1229) is among the finest — were built in stone with carved portals, corner towers and a mosque raised over the courtyard.

Organisation and history

Caravanserais were commonly founded by rulers, viziers and wealthy merchants as charitable endowments (waqf); guests could usually stay free for up to three nights, with food, fodder and even medical care provided, the upkeep paid from the endowment's rents on shops and agricultural land. Networks were spaced roughly a day's journey — about 30 to 40 kilometres — apart. Herodotus already noted relay stations on the Persian Royal Road; the institution reached its height under the Seljuks, Ottomans, Safavids and Mughals, whose roads were studded with hundreds of such inns, from the Balkans to Bengal.

Urban equivalents, called khan or han, served as combined warehouse, hostel and exchange within cities such as Aleppo, Bursa and Cairo. The word survives in names of bazaars and in the South Asian sarai. With the coming of railways and motor roads the caravanserai declined, though many have been restored as museums, hotels and cultural centres, and several are protected as UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
