Broken Arrow (nuclear)

Broken Arrow is a United States military code word designating an accidental event involving nuclear weapons, warheads, or components that does not create the risk of nuclear war. In the taxonomy of U.S. incident terms — which includes NUCFLASH for incidents that could trigger war and Bent Spear for significant but lesser mishaps — a Broken Arrow covers events such as the accidental or unexplained detonation of a weapon's non-nuclear components, radioactive contamination, the loss of a nuclear weapon, or a public hazard from a nuclear accident.

Record of incidents

The U.S. Department of Defense has officially recognised at least 32 Broken Arrow incidents between 1950 and 1980, and independent analysts have catalogued more. Well-known cases include the 1950 loss of a Mark 4 bomb near Vancouver Island, British Columbia; the 1958 Mars Bluff incident in South Carolina, in which a bomb's conventional explosives detonated on a farm; the 1961 Goldsboro accident in North Carolina, where a crashing B-52 released two hydrogen bombs, one of which came close to full arming; the 1966 Palomares incident in Spain, where four weapons fell after a mid-air collision, spreading plutonium contamination; and the 1968 Thule Air Base crash in Greenland, in which four bombs were destroyed by fire.

Legacy

No Broken Arrow has produced a nuclear yield, thanks to permissive action links, one-point safety design, and other safeguards strengthened in response to the accidents. Declassified summaries of the incidents, together with investigations into lost weapons never recovered — including a hydrogen bomb lost off Tybee Island, Georgia, in 1958 — remain a touchstone of debate over nuclear weapons safety and the airborne-alert operations of the Cold War.
