The Boeing B-29 Superfortress was an American four-engined propeller-driven heavy bomber designed by Boeing and flown primarily by the United States Army Air Forces during World War II and the United States Air Force during the Korean War. It was one of the largest aircraft operational during the war, featuring a pressurized cabin, dual bomb bays, and an analog computer-controlled fire-control system allowing one gunner to direct remotely operated turrets.

Development

The B-29 program, among the most expensive of the war, was driven by the need for a bomber with intercontinental range. The prototype first flew in September 1942, but persistent problems with its Wright R-3350 engines — prone to overheating and fires — plagued early operations. B-29s were built by Boeing at Renton and Wichita and under license by Bell and Martin.

World War II operations

B-29s operated exclusively in the Pacific Theater. After an initial campaign from bases in China (Operation Matterhorn), the bombers moved to the Mariana Islands, from which they conducted the massive firebombing campaign against Japanese cities, including the March 1945 raid on Tokyo. In August 1945, the B-29 Enola Gay dropped the atomic bomb "Little Boy" on Hiroshima, and Bockscar dropped "Fat Man" on Nagasaki — the only nuclear weapons used in armed conflict.

B-29s also dropped the Pumpkin bombs in rehearsal missions and conducted extensive aerial minelaying of Japanese shipping lanes (Operation Starvation).

Postwar service

The type served as a bomber, tanker (KB-29), weather reconnaissance aircraft, and mothership for experimental aircraft such as the Bell X-1. The Soviet Union reverse-engineered interned B-29s to produce the Tupolev Tu-4. The last B-29s were retired from U.S. service in 1960; two remain airworthy today, FIFI and Doc.
