Planet formation

Planet formation is the process by which planets form from a protoplanetary disk of gas and dust surrounding a young star. The prevailing model is the nebular hypothesis: interstellar cloud material collapses under gravity, conservation of angular momentum flattens it into a rotating disk, and solid particles within the disk accrete into planetesimals and eventually planets.

Stages

Dust grains collide and stick, growing from micrometres to pebbles; pebble accretion and gravitational instability may speed growth to kilometre-sized planetesimals. These collide to form planetary embryos. Inside the disk's frost line, rocky terrestrial planets form by mutual collisions; beyond it, cores of rock and ice grow massive enough (~10 Earth masses) to accrete hydrogen and helium gas, becoming gas giants by core accretion — or, in the disk instability model, by direct gravitational collapse of the gas.

After formation

Young systems experience migration (e.g. hot Jupiters moving inward), late heavy bombardment by leftover planetesimals, and giant impacts — such as the hypothesised collision that formed Earth's Moon — before settling into stable configurations.

Observational evidence

Millimetre-wavelength images from ALMA show rings and gaps in protoplanetary disks (such as HL Tauri) interpreted as signatures of forming planets, and the thousands of known exoplanets constrain formation models, notably the prevalence of super-Earths and planetary system architectures unlike our own.
