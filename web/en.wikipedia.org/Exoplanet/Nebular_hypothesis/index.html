Nebular hypothesis

The nebular hypothesis is the most widely accepted model of the formation of the Solar System. It holds that the Sun and planets formed about 4.6 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of a giant molecular cloud — the solar nebula. Versions were proposed by Emanuel Swedenborg, Immanuel Kant (1755), and Pierre-Simon Laplace (1796).

The model

As a cloud fragment collapsed under gravity, conservation of angular momentum caused it to spin faster and flatten into a rotating protoplanetary disk. Most mass gathered at the centre to form the Sun. Within the disk, dust and gas accreted into planetesimals and then planets. Temperature gradients explain the composition gradient of the Solar System: refractory rocky material condensed close in, while beyond the frost line, ices of water, ammonia, and methane allowed the giant planets to grow much larger cores and capture gas envelopes.

Challenges and refinements

The classical model long had difficulty explaining the angular momentum distribution (the Sun rotates slowly despite holding most of the mass) and, after the 1990s, the discovery of exoplanets — hot Jupiters and eccentric giants — that did not match Solar System expectations. Modern refinements include planetary migration (as in the Nice model and Grand Tack hypothesis) and pebble accretion, which speeds core growth.
