Exoplanet

An exoplanet or extrasolar planet is a planet outside the Solar System. The first confirmation of detection of an exoplanet orbiting a main-sequence star came in 1995, when 51 Pegasi b—a gas giant orbiting the Sun-like star 51 Pegasi—was discovered by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, who were awarded the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery. The first confirmed exoplanets of any kind had been detected around the pulsar PSR B1257+12 in 1992.

Detection methods

Most exoplanets have been found by indirect methods: the transit method (measuring the slight dimming of a star as a planet passes in front of it) and the radial velocity method (measuring the star's wobble via Doppler spectroscopy). Direct imaging has captured a small number of young, massive planets. The Kepler space telescope, launched in 2009, discovered the majority of known exoplanets.

Habitability

The habitable zone—sometimes called the "Goldilocks zone"—is the region around a star where liquid water could exist on a planet's surface. Notable potentially habitable exoplanets include the TRAPPIST-1 system, Proxima Centauri b, and Kepler-452b.

Types

Known exoplanets include hot Jupiters (gas giants orbiting very close to their stars), super-Earths, mini-Neptunes, and a small number of Earth analogues. Rogue planets—planets not bound to any star—have also been discovered.
