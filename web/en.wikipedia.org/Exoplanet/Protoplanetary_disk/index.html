Protoplanetary disk

A protoplanetary disk is a rotating circumstellar disk of dense gas and dust surrounding a young newly formed star, a T Tauri star, or a Herbig Ae/Be star. It is the remnant of the collapsing cloud core that formed the star, and it is the birthplace of planets: within the disk, material accretes over millions of years into planetesimals and planets.

Structure and composition

Disks are typically a few hundred astronomical units in radius and consist of about 99% gas (mostly hydrogen and helium) and 1% dust by mass. They are flared, with temperatures ranging from thousands of kelvin near the star to tens of kelvin at the edge. As described by the nebular hypothesis, ices condense beyond the frost line, changing the solid material available for planet building.

Observations

The Hubble Space Telescope imaged disks silhouetted against the Orion Nebula ("proplyds"), and the ALMA radio array has produced detailed images of ring and gap structures — notably around HL Tauri in 2014 — interpreted as the signatures of forming planets sweeping up material. Direct observation of an accreting protoplanet, PDS 70 b, confirmed planet formation in action.

Lifetime

Disks disperse through accretion onto the star, photoevaporation by stellar radiation, and planet formation itself; most disappear within about 3–10 million years, setting the deadline for giant-planet formation.
