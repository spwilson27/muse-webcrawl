Accretion (astrophysics)

In astrophysics, accretion is the accumulation of particles into a massive object by the gravitational attraction of further matter, typically gaseous matter in an accretion disk. Accretion explains the formation of stars and planets from molecular clouds, and powers some of the most luminous objects in the universe, including active galactic nuclei and X-ray binaries.

Planet formation

Within a protoplanetary disk, dust grains collide and stick, growing into pebbles, planetesimals, and eventually protoplanets. In the core-accretion model of giant-planet formation, a solid core of roughly 5–10 Earth masses gathers enough gravity to rapidly accrete a massive hydrogen-helium envelope from the disk before it disperses.

Accretion disks

Because infalling gas carries angular momentum, it flattens into a rotating disk rather than falling directly onto the central object. Friction and magnetic turbulence within the disk transport angular momentum outward, letting matter spiral inward and heating the disk — in black-hole systems, to X-ray-emitting temperatures near the event horizon.

Accretion onto compact objects

White dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes in binary systems accrete from companion stars; the resulting luminosity underlies phenomena such as novae, Type Ia supernovae (in some models), and quasars. The theoretical maximum steady luminosity for spherically symmetric accretion is the Eddington limit.
