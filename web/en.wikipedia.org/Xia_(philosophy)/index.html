Xia (philosophy)

Xia (Chinese: 俠; pinyin: xiá) is a Chinese philosophical and social concept denoting the knight-errant: a wandering hero, typically a skilled swordsman, who uses martial ability to right wrongs, protect the weak and uphold a personal code of honour — often in defiance of corrupt officials and unjust laws. The figure occupies a celebrated place in Chinese history, philosophy and literature.

Origins and philosophical debate

Historian Sima Qian devoted a collective biography, the "Biographies of the Wandering Xia" (Youxia liezhuan), in his Records of the Grand Historian (c. 90 BC) to commoner heroes such as Zhu Jia and Guo Jie, praising their integrity and generosity: "their words were always trustworthy, their actions always decisive, and having made a promise they always fulfilled it". Philosophers were divided: the Legalist Han Feizi condemned the xia as one of the "five vermin" of the state, since they "use arms to violate prohibitions", while Confucians such as Xunzi deplored their disregard for ritual law, yet many Confucians admired their yi (義, righteousness) and selflessness.

Characteristics

The xia's code typically includes altruism and humility, keeping one's word even at the cost of one's life, gratitude and vengeance on behalf of benefactors, disregard for wealth and personal safety, and aid to the oppressed. Unlike the Confucian gentleman, whose virtue operates through ritual and office, the xia's virtue is proved in action, frequently outside or against the legal order — a tension at the heart of the concept.

Cultural legacy

The xia became the hero of the wuxia genre of fiction and film — from Tang-dynasty tales of wonder through Jin Yong's twentieth-century novels to cinema such as Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon — in which the jianghu, the imagined community of martial wanderers, operates by the xia code. The concept is sometimes compared with the European knight-errant and the Japanese rōnin, though its philosophical grounding in yi is distinctive.
