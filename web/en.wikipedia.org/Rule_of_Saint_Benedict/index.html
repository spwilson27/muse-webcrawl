The Rule of Saint Benedict (Latin: Regula Sancti Benedicti) is a book of precepts written in Latin c. 530 by St Benedict of Nursia for monks living communally under the authority of an abbot. Comprising a prologue and 73 chapters, it sets out both the spiritual foundations of monastic life — obedience, humility and silence — and its practical organisation: the daily round of prayer (the Liturgy of the Hours), manual work, meals, clothing, discipline and the election of the abbot.

Content

The Rule divides the monk's day between prayer, sacred reading (lectio divina) and work, summarised in the motto ora et labora ("pray and work"). Benedict prescribed eight daily offices and emphasised moderation — in food, drink and sleep — so that, in his words, "in all things God may be glorified". Monks take vows of stability, conversion of life and obedience, and hold all property in common.

Influence

Adopted gradually across western Europe, the Rule became the dominant guide to monastic life in the Latin Church, reinforced by Charlemagne's reforms and the Benedictine reforming movements of Cluny and Cîteaux. The Benedictine family of orders, and later the Camaldolese, Cistercians and Trappists, all follow it. Its influence extended beyond monasteries into Western ideas of ordered communal life, time-discipline and hospitality.

Manuscripts and text

The oldest surviving manuscript (Codex Sangallensis 914, c. 820) is preserved at St Gall. The Rule continues to be lived by thousands of monks and nuns worldwide and is also followed, in adapted forms, by lay oblates.
