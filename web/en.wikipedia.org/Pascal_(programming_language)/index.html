Pascal (programming language)

Pascal is an imperative and procedural programming language, designed by Niklaus Wirth as a small, efficient language intended to encourage good programming practices using structured programming and data structuring. It is named after the French mathematician, philosopher and physicist Blaise Pascal. Pascal was developed around 1970 and based on the earlier ALGOL language family.

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662), the language's namesake.

History

Wirth published the language definition in 1970, and Pascal quickly became popular in teaching. The UCSD Pascal system of the late 1970s made the language widely available on microcomputers, and Borland's Turbo Pascal, released in 1983, became one of the most successful development tools of the personal-computer era. Pascal strongly influenced later languages, including Modula-2, Ada, and Oberon, and its object-oriented dialect Object Pascal, developed at Apple and extended in Borland's Delphi, remains in use through the Free Pascal and Lazarus projects.

Features

Pascal features strong static typing, structured control constructs, nested procedures and functions, sets, records with variants, pointers, and user-defined data types with subranges and enumerations. Programs are divided into a heading, declarations, and a statement part enclosed by begin and end.

program HelloWorld;
begin
  writeln('Hello, World!');
end.

Standards

The language was standardized as ISO 7185 in 1983, with an Extended Pascal standard (ISO 10206) following in 1990. Notable compilers include Free Pascal, GNU Pascal, and Delphi's Object Pascal compiler.
