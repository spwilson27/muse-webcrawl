Nut (goddess)

SymbolSky, stars, water pot, celestial cow
ParentsShu and Tefnut

Nut (Ancient Egyptian: Nwt) is the goddess of the sky, stars, and the heavens in ancient Egyptian religion. She is one of the oldest deities in the Egyptian pantheon, a member of the Ennead of Heliopolis, daughter of the air god Shu and the moisture goddess Tefnut, sister and wife of the earth god Geb, and mother of Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys.

Mythology

Nut is depicted as a woman arched over the earth on her hands and feet, her star-covered body forming the vault of the sky, supported by Shu who holds her apart from Geb. Each evening she swallows the sun god Ra, who travels through her body (the Duat) during the night and is reborn from her at dawn. According to myth, Ra forbade Nut to give birth on any day of the year; the god Thoth gambled with the moon and won five extra (epagomenal) days, on which Nut bore her children. Nut also appears protectively on coffins and tomb ceilings — notably in the tombs of the Valley of the Kings — spreading her wings over the deceased and receiving the dead into the sky.

Depictions

Her symbols include a water pot (the nw jar that writes her name) worn on her head, the sycamore tree, and the celestial cow form in which she lifts Ra into the heavens, as told in the Book of the Heavenly Cow. Astronomical ceilings depicting Nut and the constellations are among the most striking survivals of Egyptian temple art, as at Dendera.
