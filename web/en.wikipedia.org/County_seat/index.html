County seat

A county seat is an administrative center, seat of government, or capital city of a county or civil parish. The term is used principally in the United States and Canada. A county seat is usually, though not always, an incorporated municipality. The location of the county government buildings — above all the courthouse — is what gives the place its status.

United States

In the United States, counties are the primary subdivisions of most states, and the county seat is where the county court, board of commissioners, sheriff, clerk, and records are based. Historically, the choice of seat was sometimes fiercely contested between rival towns — the "county seat wars" of the 19th-century frontier, particularly in Kansas, occasionally turned violent. In the eastern states the seat was often placed within a day's horseback ride of all residents. Some counties have two seats, usually for historical or geographic reasons; in a few, the "shire town" of Vermont or the "parish seat" of Louisiana serves the same function.

Counties that are consolidated city-counties may have no separate seat, and independent cities in Virginia function as their own county equivalents. Alaska's boroughs use the term "borough seat," while in Canada the equivalent in some provinces is a "county town."

Elsewhere

Comparable terms include chef-lieu in France, Kreisstadt in Germany, and the county town in the United Kingdom and Ireland, where the historic administrative centre of a county may host its courts and council.
