Proton–proton chain

The proton–proton chain, also commonly called the p–p chain, is one of two known sets of nuclear fusion reactions by which stars convert hydrogen to helium. It dominates in stars with masses less than or equal to that of the Sun, whereas the CNO cycle, the other known reaction set, is dominant in stars more massive than the Sun.

Process

In general, proton–proton fusion can occur only if the kinetic energy of the protons is high enough to overcome their mutual electrostatic repulsion. The chain begins with two protons fusing to form diprotium (2He), which decays by beta-plus decay into deuterium, emitting a positron and an electron neutrino. Deuterium then fuses with another proton to form helium-3, and two helium-3 nuclei finally fuse to form helium-4, releasing two protons.

Energy output

The complete chain releases about 26.7 MeV of energy per helium-4 nucleus produced, carried away partly by gamma rays and positrons and partly by neutrinos, which escape the star almost unimpeded. In the Sun, the proton–proton chain produces roughly 98–99% of the luminosity. Neutrinos from the chain have been detected directly by experiments such as Borexino.

Branches

The chain has several branches (pp I, pp II, pp III, and the rare pep branch), which differ in how helium-3 is consumed and produce neutrinos of different energies.
