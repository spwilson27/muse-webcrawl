g factor (psychometrics)

The g factor is extracted from the correlations among cognitive tests

The g factor (also known as general intelligence, general mental ability, or general intelligence factor) is a construct from psychometrics concerning the statistical finding that performance on cognitive tasks of all kinds tends to be positively correlated. These positive correlations suggest a common underlying factor that influences performance on every sort of mental test.

History

The g factor was first described by the English psychologist Charles Spearman in 1904, who noticed that schoolchildren's grades across seemingly unrelated subjects were positively correlated. Using the newly developed technique of factor analysis, Spearman proposed a two-factor theory: every test measures a general factor, g, shared by all tests, plus a specific factor unique to that test. The rival view of multiple independent abilities was later advanced by L. L. Thurstone, but subsequent research showed that Thurstone's primary abilities were themselves correlated, implying a higher-order general factor — a reconciliation reflected in modern hierarchical models such as the Cattell–Horn–Carroll theory.

Significance

Scores on g-loaded tests correlate with a wide range of outcomes, including educational attainment, job performance (particularly in complex occupations), and various health measures. The construct is closely related to IQ scores, which are heavily g-loaded. While the statistical reality of g is broadly accepted, its nature remains debated: it may reflect a single underlying cognitive capacity, the overlap of many distinct processes, or a formative statistical summary rather than a unitary mental entity.
