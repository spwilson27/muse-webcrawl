The Roman Temple of Évora (Portuguese: Templo Romano de Évora), often called the Temple of Diana, is an ancient temple in the historic centre of Évora, Portugal. It is one of the most significant surviving Roman monuments in the country.

History

The temple was built in the first century AD, probably around the time of Augustus, in the forum of the Roman city of Liberalitas Julia Ebora. It stood on the highest ground in the city, beside what later became the cathedral. Contrary to the popular name, there is no evidence the temple was dedicated to Diana, goddess of the hunt; the attribution is a later legend, and scholars believe it served the imperial cult or a deity such as Venus or Jupiter.

The building survived because it was repeatedly reused: during the Middle Ages it was incorporated into a fortified tower of Évora castle, and for centuries it served as a butcher's shop in the town slaughterhouse. These additions, removed in a nineteenth-century restoration, had in fact protected the columns. In the fifth century the invading Germanic peoples damaged parts of the structure, and its podium and materials were quarried, yet fourteen columns remain upright.

Description

The temple stands on a high granite podium roughly 25 metres long. Fourteen Corinthian columns of local granite survive with their capitals of Estremoz marble, supporting portions of the architrave. Originally the temple was surrounded by a portico and a reflecting pool. Today it anchors Largo do Conde de Vila Flor, flanked by the Évora Museum and the Pousada dos Lóios, and is the emblem of the city.
