The Alentejo is a geographical, historical, and cultural region of south-central and southern Portugal. Its name derives from além do Tejo — "beyond the Tagus" — the great river that forms its northern boundary. It is the largest region of mainland Portugal by area yet one of the least densely populated.

Geography and landscape

The region is characterised by gently rolling plains covered in golden wheat fields, olive groves, vineyards, and the open oak savanna known as montado, where cork oaks and holm oaks are grazed by cattle, sheep, and the black Iberian pig. Summers are among the hottest in Europe, frequently exceeding 40 °C, and the pace of life is traditionally slow — the Alentejanos are sometimes teased by other Portuguese for their unhurried manner.

History

The area preserves some of Iberia's oldest monuments, including the Cromlech of the Almendres near Évora, among the largest megalithic complexes in Europe. Roman, Visigothic, and Moorish rule left aqueducts, castles, and whitewashed hill towns; the region was retaken by Portuguese forces during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Évora, the regional capital, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and the fortified town of Elvas guards the Spanish frontier.

Economy and culture

Agriculture dominates: the Alentejo produces roughly half of the world's cork supply, much of Portugal's wine and olive oil, and cereals that earned it the title "breadbasket of Portugal." The Alqueva Dam, completed in 2002, created one of Europe's largest artificial lakes and expanded irrigation. The polyphonic singing tradition of cante alentejano was inscribed by UNESCO on its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2014.
