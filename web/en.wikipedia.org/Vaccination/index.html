Vaccination

Vaccination is the administration of a vaccine to help the immune system develop immunity from a disease. Vaccines contain a microorganism or virus in a weakened, live or killed state, or proteins or toxins from the organism. By stimulating the body's adaptive immunity, vaccination helps prevent sickness from an infectious disease. When a sufficiently large share of a population has been vaccinated, herd immunity results. Vaccination is widely considered the most effective method of preventing infectious diseases, and widespread immunity due to vaccination is largely responsible for the worldwide eradication of smallpox and the elimination of diseases such as polio, measles, and tetanus from much of the world.

History

The earliest hints of inoculation—deliberately exposing a person to material from smallpox sores—date to roughly the 10th century in China, where insufflation of powdered scabs was practised; variolation later spread through the Ottoman Empire to Europe, where Lady Mary Wortley Montagu promoted it in Britain in the 1720s. In 1796 the English physician Edward Jenner showed that inoculation with cowpox protected against smallpox, coining the term from vacca (Latin for cow). Louis Pasteur extended the principle in the 19th century, developing vaccines against anthrax and rabies and the germ theory underpinning them. The 20th century brought vaccines against diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, yellow fever, polio (Salk's inactivated vaccine in 1955 and Sabin's oral vaccine), measles, mumps and rubella.

How vaccination works

A vaccine presents antigens—substances recognised as foreign—to the immune system without causing the disease itself (or causing only a much milder form). The adaptive immune system responds by producing antibodies and memory B and T cells; if the real pathogen is later encountered, the response is faster and stronger, usually preventing illness. Vaccine platforms include live-attenuated, inactivated, subunit and conjugate vaccines, toxoids, viral-vector vaccines, and mRNA vaccines, the latter deployed at scale for the first time during the COVID-19 pandemic. Adjuvants, delivery schedules and booster doses vary by product.

Programmes and impact

The World Health Organization's Expanded Programme on Immunization, launched in 1974, standardised childhood vaccination schedules worldwide. The WHO declared smallpox eradicated in 1980—the first disease eliminated globally by vaccination. Polio has been eliminated from all but a few endemic areas. Public health authorities such as the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control publish recommended schedules for children, adolescents and adults. The effectiveness of vaccines is monitored continuously, and rare adverse events are recorded by pharmacovigilance systems.

Herd immunity and hesitancy

When coverage is high, transmission chains break even for those who cannot be vaccinated, an effect called herd (or community) immunity. Vaccine hesitancy—reluctance or refusal despite availability—was listed by the WHO among the top threats to global health in 2019. A discredited 1998 paper by Andrew Wakefield falsely linked the MMR vaccine to autism; the paper was retracted and Wakefield struck off the UK medical register, but the scare contributed to measles resurgences in several countries.
