Second Italian War of Independence

The Second Italian War of Independence, also known in Italy as the Franco-Austrian War, was fought in 1859 by the French Empire and the Kingdom of Sardinia (Piedmont) against the Austrian Empire. It was a decisive step in the Risorgimento, the unification of Italy: Piedmont gained Lombardy, and the war set in motion the collapse of Austrian and ducal rule across northern and central Italy.

Origins

Piedmontese prime minister Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, secured French support at the secret meeting of Plombières (1858), where Napoleon III agreed to help expel Austria from Italy in return for Nice and Savoy. Cavour provoked Austria into issuing an ultimatum and invading Piedmont in April 1859, bringing France into the war.

Course of the war

French troops under Napoleon III won the Battle of Magenta (4 June), opening the way to Milan, and the huge Battle of Solferino (24 June), the largest battle since Leipzig, ended in Franco-Piedmontese victory at heavy cost. The carnage at Solferino moved the Swiss observer Henri Dunant to write A Memory of Solferino, leading to the founding of the Red Cross and the first Geneva Convention. Alarmed by Prussian mobilisation and casualties, Napoleon III abruptly concluded the Armistice of Villafranca (11 July), to Cavour's fury — he resigned — but the settlement stood: Austria ceded Lombardy, while keeping Venetia.

Consequences

Revolutions in Tuscany, Modena, Parma and the Romagna led to their annexation by Piedmont after plebiscites in 1860, and France received Nice and Savoy under the Treaty of Turin. Within two years Garibaldi's expedition to Sicily and Naples completed most of Italian unification.
