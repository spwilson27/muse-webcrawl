A continent is a large, continuous landmass on Earth, conventionally identified by geography, geology and culture rather than by a single strict definition. The most common scheme, taught in most English-speaking countries, recognises seven continents: Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe and Australia (Oceania).

Definitions and conventions

There is no universally accepted list. Many European and Latin American traditions teach six continents, combining Europe and Asia as Eurasia, or the Americas as one continent; Olympic symbolism uses five inhabited continents. Europe is a peninsula of Eurasia separated from Asia by convention — the Ural Mountains, Ural River, Caucasus and Turkish straits — rather than by water. Geologists define continents by continental crust and continental shelves, which yields yet another picture, including largely submerged landmasses such as Zealandia.

Geology

Continents consist of ancient cratons surrounded by younger mountain belts, and they move over geological time through plate tectonics. Past supercontinents such as Pangaea, Gondwana and Rodinia assembled and broke apart in cycles, redistributing land and oceans and shaping climate and evolution.

Population and extent

Asia is by far the largest and most populous continent; Antarctica is a continent by landmass but has no permanent population. Continents cover about 29% of Earth's surface. The boundaries and names of continents carry cultural and political weight — for example in disputes over whether Oceania, Australasia or "Australia" best names the smallest continental region.
