Electric locomotive

An electric locomotive is a locomotive powered by electricity from overhead lines, a third rail, or on-board energy storage such as a battery or flywheel. Electric locomotives are quieter, cleaner at the point of use, and usually more powerful and quicker to accelerate than diesel locomotives, though they require expensive electrification infrastructure.

A modern electric locomotive under overhead catenary.

Power supply

Most electric locomotives collect current from an overhead catenary by means of a pantograph, or from a third rail by a contact shoe. Railways use many different systems—common ones include 25 kV AC at 50 Hz, 15 kV AC in parts of central Europe, and 1,500 V or 3,000 V DC—so multi-system locomotives are needed for cross-border work. Modern locomotives use power electronics to drive AC traction motors and often feed braking energy back into the supply by regenerative braking.

History

The first electric locomotive was demonstrated by Werner von Siemens in Berlin in 1879. Main-line electrification spread in the early 20th century, especially in mountainous countries such as Switzerland, where tunnels and abundant hydroelectric power favoured it, and on dense suburban networks. Famous classes include the Swiss "Crocodiles," the Pennsylvania Railroad's GG1, and today's high-speed trains such as the TGV and Shinkansen, which are electric multiple units.

Advantages and disadvantages

Electric traction offers high power-to-weight, low operating and maintenance costs, no local emissions, and regenerative braking. Its drawbacks are the capital cost of electrification, vulnerability to power or infrastructure failures, and incompatibility between national electrical systems.
