Spice

A spice is a seed, fruit, root, bark, or other plant substance primarily used for flavoring or coloring food. Spices are distinguished from herbs, which are the leaves, flowers, or stems of plants used for flavoring or as a garnish. Spices are sometimes used in medicine, religious rituals, cosmetics, or perfume production.

History

The spice trade developed throughout the Indian subcontinent and East Asia by as early as 2000 BCE with spices including cinnamon, cardamom, and turmeric. The Egyptians used herbs for embalming. Early spice use was closely connected to magic, medicine, religion, tradition, and preservation. In the story of Genesis, Joseph was sold into slavery by his brothers to spice merchants. In ancient Indian society, spice could be provided as a post-meal digestive aid.

Spices were among the most demanded and expensive products available in Europe in the Middle Ages, the most common being black pepper, cinnamon, cumin, nutmeg, ginger, and cloves. Given the medieval medicine main theory of humorism, spices were judged to be precious and were used for both medicinal and culinary purposes. The search for spices and the sea routes to India greatly accelerated European exploration, contributing to the Age of Discovery.

Production

India produces 75 of the 109 varieties listed by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and accounts for half of the global trading in spices. Bangladesh, Indonesia, China, Pakistan, and Nepal are other major producers.

Common spices

Common spices include black pepper, cinnamon, turmeric, cardamom, ginger, nutmeg, cloves, cumin, coriander, paprika, saffron, and mustard seed. Many blends, such as garam masala, curry powder, and Chinese five-spice powder, are famous worldwide.

Classification

Spices may be classified by the part of the plant used: seeds (such as cumin and coriander), fruits (black pepper, cardamom), bark (cinnamon, cassia), roots and rhizomes (ginger, turmeric), flower buds (cloves), and stigmas (saffron).
