Mark Twain

Samuel Langhorne Clemens (30 November 1835 – 21 April 1910), known by his pen name Mark Twain, was an American writer, humorist, entrepreneur, publisher and lecturer. He was praised as the "greatest humorist the United States has produced", and William Faulkner called him "the father of American literature". His novels include The Adventures of Tom Sawyer (1876) and its sequel, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884), the latter often called the "Great American Novel".

Life

Twain was born in Florida, Missouri, two weeks after the closest approach of Halley's Comet, and grew up in Hannibal on the Mississippi River, which inspired St. Petersburg in his fiction. He was apprenticed to a printer, worked as a typesetter, and then trained as a steamboat pilot — the source of his pen name, the leadsman's cry "mark twain" meaning two fathoms of safe water. Brief service with a Confederate militia unit was followed by travel west, where his humorous story "The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County" (1865) made his name. The Innocents Abroad (1869) established him as a best-selling author and lecturer.

Later years

Twain married Olivia Langdon in 1870 and lived for many years in Hartford, Connecticut, where he wrote his major works, and at Quarry Farm in Elmira, New York. Poor investments, especially in the Paige typesetting machine, drove him into bankruptcy in the 1890s; he repaid his creditors in full after a worldwide lecture tour. His later writing grew darker and more anti-imperialist. He died in Redding, Connecticut, in 1910, a day after Halley's Comet returned, as he had predicted. His autobiography, published 100 years after his death in 2010, became a bestseller.
