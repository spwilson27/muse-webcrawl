Greater flamingo

Scientific namePhoenicopterus roseus
FamilyPhoenicopteridae
Height110–150 cm
Wingspan140–170 cm
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

The greater flamingo (Phoenicopterus roseus) is the largest and most widespread species of flamingo, found in shallow wetlands around the Mediterranean, in parts of Africa, the Middle East and southern Asia. It was long treated as a subspecies of the American flamingo but is now generally recognised as a separate species.

Description

Standing up to 150 cm tall, the greater flamingo has pale pinkish-white plumage with contrasting crimson wing coverts and black flight feathers, a pink bill tipped with black, and long pink legs. Its pink colour derives from carotenoid pigments in the algae and small crustaceans it eats; birds deprived of these pigments fade to white. Juveniles are grey-brown and take several years to acquire adult colour.

Feeding

The species feeds with its head upside down in shallow water, sweeping its specially adapted bill from side to side. Rows of lamellae act as a sieve, trapping blue-green algae, diatoms, insect larvae and small crustaceans while water is expelled by the tongue. Flocks often feed together in lines or arcs, stamping their feet to stir up food.

Behaviour and breeding

Greater flamingos are highly social, living in flocks that can number in the tens of thousands. Breeding is colonial and synchronised: pairs build a conical mud nest mound and lay a single chalky white egg, which both parents incubate for about four weeks. Chicks are fed a red "crop milk" and gather in crèches guarded by a few adults. Breeding sites include the Camargue in France, Fuente de Piedra in Spain, coastal lagoons in Turkey and Iran, and soda lakes in the African Rift Valley.

Distribution and conservation

Populations are mobile, moving between wetlands in response to water levels and food. The species is listed as Least Concern overall, though Mediterranean and West Asian populations are sensitive to disturbance, water abstraction and the loss of salt-pan and lagoon habitats. Ringing studies show individuals can live more than 40 years in the wild.
