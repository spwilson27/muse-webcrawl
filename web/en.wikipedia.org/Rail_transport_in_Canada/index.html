Rail transport in Canada comprises a network of roughly 49,000 kilometres of track operated mainly by two transcontinental freight railways, Canadian National (CN) and Canadian Pacific Kansas City (CPKC), together with dozens of regional and short-line railways, the national passenger service Via Rail, and urban commuter and transit systems.

History

Canada's first railway, the Champlain and St. Lawrence, opened in 1836 near Montreal. Railways were central to Confederation: the promise of an Intercolonial Railway and a Pacific line helped unite the provinces. The Canadian Pacific Railway, completed in 1885 with the driving of the Last Spike at Craigellachie, British Columbia, bound British Columbia to the Dominion and became an enduring national symbol. The 20th century brought consolidation, the creation of CN as a Crown corporation (1919–1923), privatisation of CN in 1995, and in 2023 the merger of Canadian Pacific with Kansas City Southern.

Operations

Freight dominates Canadian rail: bulk commodities such as grain, coal, potash and containers move in long unit trains, and Canadian railways carry a high share of national freight tonne-kilometres. Passenger services include Via Rail's transcontinental Canadian, corridor trains between Quebec City and Windsor, and commuter systems in Toronto (GO Transit), Montreal (Exo) and Vancouver (West Coast Express). Safety regulation and major incidents, such as the 2013 Lac-Mégantic disaster, have shaped modern operating rules.
