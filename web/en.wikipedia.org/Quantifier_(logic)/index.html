Quantifier (logic)

In logic, a quantifier is an operator that specifies how many individuals in the domain of discourse satisfy an open formula. For instance, the universal quantifier ∀ ("for all") in the first-order formula ∀x P(x) expresses that everything in the domain satisfies the property denoted by P. Similarly, the existential quantifier ∃ ("there exists") expresses that something in the domain satisfies the property. Quantifiers are central to modern mathematical logic and to the semantics of natural languages.

Types of quantifiers

The two most common quantifiers are the universal and existential quantifiers, which are interdefinable using negation: ∀x P(x) is equivalent to ¬∃x ¬P(x). Other quantifiers include the uniqueness quantifier ∃! ("there exists exactly one"), counting quantifiers ("there are at least k"), and the generalised quantifiers of model theory and linguistics, such as "most" and "many", which cannot be defined in ordinary first-order logic. Quantifiers rank by the range of the variables they bind: first-order quantifiers range over individuals, while second-order quantifiers range over properties, sets or relations.

History and semantics

The modern theory of quantification began with Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) and was developed by Peano, Russell and others. In model theory, the truth of a quantified formula is defined relative to a structure and an assignment of values to variables; the scope of a quantifier, and the distinction between free and bound variables, determine how nested quantifiers of mixed type are evaluated. Scope ambiguities are also a major topic in the study of natural language, where sentences like "every student read a book" admit multiple readings.
