Group selection

Group selection is a proposed mechanism of evolution in which natural selection acts at the level of the group, rather than at the level of the individual organism or gene. Under group selection, traits that benefit a group — even at a cost to individual fitness — could evolve if groups possessing those traits out-reproduce, out-survive, or split into daughter groups more successfully than groups lacking them.

History

Charles Darwin invoked group-level reasoning for social traits, including in humans, in The Descent of Man. The idea was popularised in the 20th century by V. C. Wynne-Edwards, whose 1962 book Animal Dispersion in Relation to Social Behaviour argued that animals restrain their reproduction for the good of the population. This provoked strong criticism: George C. Williams, John Maynard Smith, and others showed that most such phenomena could be explained by individual- and gene-level selection, and that within-group selection usually undermines group-beneficial traits unless groups reproduce and are inherited as units.

Modern views

Contemporary work on multilevel selection theory, associated with biologists such as David Sloan Wilson and E. O. Wilson, treats group selection as a mathematically legitimate component of selection alongside kin selection, though the two frameworks are debated: many results obtained with inclusive fitness (kin selection) models can be reformulated in multilevel terms. Group selection is considered most plausible where groups are small, variation between groups is high, and migration between groups is limited — conditions found in some social insects, microbes, and in hypotheses about human cultural group selection.
