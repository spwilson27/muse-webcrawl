Eridu
Location: Tell Abu Shahrain, Dhi Qar Governorate, Iraq
Founded: c. 5400 BC
Culture: Sumerian
The archaeological site of Eridu (Tell Abu Shahrain) in southern Iraq

Eridu (Sumerian: 𒉌𒂖, NUN.KI; Akkadian: irîtu) was an ancient Sumerian city in what is now Tell Abu Shahrain, in Dhi Qar Governorate, Iraq. Long considered the earliest city in southern Mesopotamia, it was founded around 5400 BC near the Persian Gulf. In Sumerian tradition, Eridu was the first city in the world: the Sumerian King List begins, "After the kingship descended from heaven, the kingship was in Eridug."

History

Eridu was the cult centre of the water god Enki (Akkadian Ea), whose temple, the E-Abzu, stood on the site of a sequence of ever-larger shrines reaching back to the Ubaid period. Excavated temple levels show a small chapel of about 5500 BC developing into a substantial tripartite temple — a sequence central to understanding early Mesopotamian religious architecture. The city flourished through the Ubaid and Uruk periods but declined as the coastline and the Euphrates shifted; an unfinished ziggurat attributed to the Ur III king Amar-Sin attests later royal attention.

Archaeology

The site was first excavated in 1855 by John George Taylor and more fully by Iraqi archaeologists Fuad Safar and Seton Lloyd in 1946–1949. The temple sequence of 18 superimposed levels became a type-site for the Ubaid period. In 2016 Eridu was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the Ahwar of Southern Iraq listing.

Mythology

In Sumerian myth, Eridu was created as the home of Enki, god of the sweet waters of the Abzu. Adapa, the sage of Eridu, figures in the myth of Adapa and the Food of Life, and the city opens the antediluvian section of the King List, whose first king of Eridu, Alulim, was credited with a reign of 28,800 years.
