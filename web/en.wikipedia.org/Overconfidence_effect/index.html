Overconfidence effect

The overconfidence effect is a well-established cognitive bias in which a person's subjective confidence in their judgements is reliably greater than the objective accuracy of those judgements. It is one of the most robust findings in the psychology of judgement and decision-making.

Types

Research distinguishes three main forms of overconfidence. Overestimation is believing one's performance or ability is better than it actually is. Overplacement is believing one ranks higher than others — for example, surveys consistently find that a large majority of drivers rate themselves as above average. Overprecision is excessive certainty about the accuracy of one's beliefs, visible when confidence intervals people give are far too narrow.

Evidence

In classic calibration studies, participants who say they are 90% sure of an answer are typically right only about 70–80% of the time. Overprecision is the most consistent form: in one study, 99% confidence intervals captured the true value only about 40–60% of the time. Overconfidence has been documented among physicians, engineers, entrepreneurs, financial forecasters, and drivers.

Consequences

Overconfidence contributes to the planning fallacy (underestimating how long tasks will take), excessive stock market trading, entry of doomed businesses, and failures of risk assessment in engineering and medicine. At the same time, moderate overconfidence may have adaptive benefits, encouraging ambition, perseverance, and social confidence.

Debiasing

Techniques shown to reduce overconfidence include considering the opposite, reference-class forecasting (using base rates from similar past cases), and premortem exercises that imagine a plan has already failed.
