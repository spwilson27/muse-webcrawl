Fast radio burst

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In radio astronomy, a fast radio burst (FRB) is a transient radio pulse of length ranging from a fraction of a millisecond to about three seconds, caused by some high-energy astrophysical process not yet fully understood. Although enormously energetic at their sources — releasing in milliseconds as much energy as the Sun emits in days — the signals are extremely weak by the time they reach Earth. The first FRB was discovered by Duncan Lorimer and his student David Narkevic in 2007 in archival data from the Parkes radio telescope; it is known as the Lorimer Burst.

Characteristics

FRBs show a characteristic dispersion: their high-frequency components arrive before their low-frequency components, indicating that the waves have passed through large quantities of ionised plasma over cosmological distances — most FRBs originate outside the Milky Way, in distant galaxies. Some sources are one-off events, while others repeat, sometimes with periodic windows of activity. The first repeater, FRB 121102, was localised to a dwarf galaxy about three billion light-years away.

Origin

The leading explanation for many FRBs is emission from magnetars — young neutron stars with extremely powerful magnetic fields. In April 2020, the Galactic magnetar SGR 1935+2154 produced a bright radio burst (FRB 200428) accompanied by an X-ray burst, the first FRB detected from within the Milky Way and the first linked to an identified source. Other proposed mechanisms include neutron star mergers, black hole interactions and exotic compact objects, and more than one formation channel may exist. The Canadian CHIME telescope has catalogued hundreds of FRBs, making statistical studies of their host galaxies and environments possible.
