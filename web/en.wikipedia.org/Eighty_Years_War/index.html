Eighty Years' War

The Eighty Years' War (1568–1648) was the Dutch revolt against Spanish Habsburg rule, leading to the independence of the Dutch Republic.

The Eighty Years' War or Dutch Revolt (Dutch: Nederlandse Opstand) (c. 1566/1568–1648) was an armed conflict in the Habsburg Netherlands between disparate groups of rebels and the Spanish government. The causes of the war included the Reformation, centralisation, taxation and the rights and privileges of the nobility and cities.

Origins

Discontent with Philip II of Spain's religious persecution of Protestants and his centralizing policies provoked the Iconoclastic Fury of 1566 and, in response, the Duke of Alba's Council of Troubles. William of Orange led the first invasion in 1568, conventionally taken as the war's start. The Sea Beggars' capture of Brielle in 1572 turned the revolt into a sustained conflict in Holland and Zeeland.

Course of the war

After periods of Spanish recovery under Alexander Farnese, the Dutch Republic under Maurice of Nassau won a string of victories in the 1590s. The Twelve Years' Truce (1609–1621) amounted to de facto recognition of the Republic. The war resumed as part of the wider Thirty Years' War, with Dutch victories at sea and the capture of 's-Hertogenbosch and Breda.

Peace of Münster

The Peace of Münster, signed on 30 January 1648 as part of the Peace of Westphalia, formally recognized the independence of the Dutch Republic. Spain retained the Southern Netherlands (roughly modern Belgium).

Consequences

The war laid the foundations of the Dutch Golden Age: the Republic became a leading commercial, maritime and colonial power, while the conflict reshaped the political map of the Low Countries into the Netherlands and Belgium.
