Candidiasis

Candidiasis is a fungal infection caused by yeasts of the genus Candida, most commonly Candida albicans. Candida species normally live harmlessly on the skin and mucous membranes, but can overgrow to cause infections ranging from superficial conditions — oral thrush and vaginal yeast infections — to life-threatening invasive disease of the bloodstream and organs, chiefly in immunocompromised people.

Types

Oral candidiasis (thrush) presents as creamy white plaques on the tongue and mouth, common in infants, denture wearers, inhaled-steroid users, and people with HIV/AIDS. Vulvovaginal candidiasis affects most women at least once, causing itching and discharge; pregnancy and antibiotics predispose. Cutaneous infection occurs in moist skin folds (intertrigo) and under nails. Invasive candidiasis — candidemia and deep-organ infection — is a major hospital-acquired infection with high mortality, associated with central venous catheters, intensive care, abdominal surgery, and neutropenia.

Causes and diagnosis

Over 200 Candida species exist; alongside C. albicans, species such as C. glabrata, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis, and the multidrug-resistant C. auris (identified in 2009) are clinically important. Diagnosis rests on microscopy, culture, and, for invasive disease, blood cultures and biomarkers such as beta-D-glucan.

Treatment

Superficial infections respond to topical agents (nystatin, clotrimazole) or a single dose of fluconazole for vaginal infection. Invasive disease is treated first-line with echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin), with azoles or amphotericin B as alternatives, and removal of infected catheters. Resistance, especially in C. auris and C. glabrata, is a growing concern.
