Task force

A task force is a temporary grouping of personnel, units, or organizations assembled to accomplish a specific, limited objective, after which the group is normally disbanded and its members return to their parent organizations. The term originated in naval warfare and has spread to government, law enforcement, business, and international organizations.

Military usage

The United States Navy popularized the term during the Second World War, designating ad hoc fleets by number — such as Task Force 16, which launched the Doolittle Raid — and combining them under task fleets. A task force is composed of task groups and task units, is tailored to its mission (carrier strike, amphibious landing, convoy escort), and gives commanders flexibility without permanently reorganizing the fleet. Allied and NATO navies adopted the system, and armies use the term similarly for combined-arms groupings, e.g. battalion-sized "task forces" built around an armoured or infantry headquarters.

Civil and organizational usage

Governments appoint task forces to address defined problems — disaster response, epidemics, organized crime, financial fraud — pooling staff from multiple agencies; law-enforcement task forces commonly unite federal, state, and local officers. In corporations and universities, a task force is a cross-functional team charged with investigating an issue and recommending action, distinguished from a standing committee by its temporary charter and single purpose.

Characteristics

Effective task forces are defined by a clear mandate, a single accountable leader, dedicated resources, and a deadline or sunset condition. Their temporary nature is both their strength — focus and flexibility — and their weakness, since implementation of recommendations may stall once the group dissolves.
