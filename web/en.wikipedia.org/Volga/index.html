Volga

The Volga (Russian: Волга) is the longest river in Europe, flowing 3,531 km (2,194 mi) through central Russia to the Caspian Sea. Rising in the Valdai Hills northwest of Moscow at an elevation of only 228 m, it drains a basin of about 1.36 million km² — most of European Russia — and is widely regarded as Russia's national river, known in Russian culture as "Mother Volga".

Course

From its source the Volga flows east past Tver, Yaroslavl and Nizhny Novgorod, where it is joined by the Oka, then turns south at Kazan, receiving its largest tributary, the Kama. It continues past Samara, Saratov and Volgograd (formerly Stalingrad) before splitting into the vast Volga Delta at Astrakhan and emptying into the Caspian Sea, the world's largest inland water body, some 28 m below sea level.

Importance

The river carries about two-thirds of Russia's inland waterway freight, linked by canals to the Baltic, White and Black seas. A chain of large hydroelectric dams built in the Soviet era — including those at Volgograd and Zhigulevsk — produces power and regulates navigation, though it has disrupted sturgeon migration to spawning grounds; the delta remains a major source of caviar-bearing beluga sturgeon. Historically the Volga was a great trade route connecting Scandinavia and Rus' with Persia, and its banks saw the battles of Stalingrad (1942–43), a turning point of the Second World War.
