Taft–Hartley Act

Full title: Labor Management Relations Act, 1947
Enacted: 23 June 1947 (over presidential veto)
Sponsors: Sen. Robert A. Taft; Rep. Fred A. Hartley Jr.
Amends: National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act)

The Labor Management Relations Act, 1947, better known as the Taft–Hartley Act, is a United States federal law that restricts the activities and power of labour unions. Sponsored by Senator Robert A. Taft and Representative Fred A. Hartley Jr., it was passed by Congress over the veto of President Harry S. Truman on 23 June 1947.

Provisions

The Act amended the pro-union National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (the Wagner Act). It prohibited jurisdictional strikes, wildcat strikes, solidarity and political strikes, and secondary boycotts; it outlawed closed shops and allowed states to pass "right-to-work" laws banning union shops (Section 14(b)); it required union officers to sign non-communist affidavits; and it empowered the President to seek injunctions against strikes imperilling national health or safety, with an 80-day cooling-off period.

Reception and legacy

Organised labour denounced Taft–Hartley as the "slave-labour bill" and repeatedly sought its repeal, but it remains in force and continues to shape American labour relations, including continuing debate over right-to-work laws and union security.
