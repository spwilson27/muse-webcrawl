Compromise of 1877

The Compromise of 1877, also known as the Wormley Agreement or the Tilden–Hayes Compromise, was an informal, unwritten deal that settled the disputed 1876 U.S. presidential election. It resulted in the United States federal government pulling the last troops out of the southern United States and formally ended the Reconstruction Era. Through the compromise, Republican Rutherford B. Hayes was awarded the White House over Democrat Samuel J. Tilden on the understanding that Hayes would remove the federal troops whose support was essential for the survival of the remaining Republican state governments in Florida, Louisiana and South Carolina.

Background

In the election of 1876, Tilden won the popular vote and 184 electoral votes, one short of a majority, while Hayes had 165. Twenty electoral votes from Florida, Louisiana, South Carolina and Oregon were disputed, amid widespread fraud and violence against Black voters. Congress created an Electoral Commission, which voted along party lines, 8–7, to award all disputed votes to Hayes.

Terms

Democrats agreed to accept the commission's verdict in exchange for Republican promises: withdrawal of remaining federal troops from the South, support for internal improvements and a transcontinental railroad, appointment of a Southern Democrat to the cabinet, and legislation to help industrialise the South.

Consequences

Hayes kept his pledge to withdraw the troops. With federal protection gone, Southern Democrats ("Redeemers") consolidated control, and the disenfranchisement of African Americans through Jim Crow laws followed over the next decades. Historians regard the compromise as the end of Reconstruction and a turning point in American race relations.
