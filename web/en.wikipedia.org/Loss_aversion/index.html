Loss aversion

Loss aversion is a concept in behavioral economics and decision theory describing the tendency for people to prefer avoiding losses to acquiring equivalent gains: losing $100 feels worse than gaining $100 feels good. It is a central component of prospect theory, developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979.

Evidence and magnitude

In prospect theory's value function, the curve is steeper in the loss domain than in the gain domain. Kahneman and Tversky estimated that losses are weighted roughly twice as heavily as gains (a loss aversion coefficient near 2.25 in later experiments). Loss aversion helps explain the endowment effect — people demand more to give up an object than they would pay to acquire it — the status quo bias, and the disposition effect in investing, where people hold losing stocks too long and sell winners too early.

Criticism and debate

Some researchers have questioned whether loss aversion is a universal and robust principle, arguing that apparent loss aversion can depend on experimental framing, attention, and the size of stakes, and that in some contexts gains and losses are treated symmetrically. The concept nonetheless remains influential in economics, marketing, and policy design.
