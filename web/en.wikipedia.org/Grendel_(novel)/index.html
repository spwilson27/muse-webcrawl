Grendel is a 1971 novel by the American author John Gardner. It is a retelling of the Anglo-Saxon epic poem Beowulf from the perspective of the monster Grendel, who is portrayed as a lonely, articulate and philosophically tormented antihero rather than a simple embodiment of evil.

Plot

The novel is narrated in the first person by Grendel, following twelve years of his war with the Danes of King Hrothgar's hall, Heorot. Grendel observes human society from the shadows — its violence, its religion, its poetry-making "Shaper" who sings seductive fictions of order and heroism — and alternates between longing to join it and contemptuously raiding it. He receives a bleak, nihilistic education from the ancient dragon, whose vision of a meaningless, entropic universe drives Grendel to embrace his role as monster. The novel ends with the arrival of the hero (Beowulf, never named directly, called "the stranger") and Grendel's fatal encounter with him.

Themes

The book's twelve chapters are loosely associated with the signs of the zodiac and with stages of philosophical thought. It explores the power of art and myth to impose meaning, the conflict between heroism and nihilism, language, and isolation. Gardner described it as an examination of "all of the major philosophical positions of the Western world"; it is frequently taught alongside Beowulf in schools and universities and is Gardner's best-known work.

Adaptations

The novel was adapted as the animated film Grendel Grendel Grendel (1981) and as an opera, Grendel (2006), by Elliot Goldenthal and Julie Taymor.
