The Potawatomi are an Indigenous people of the Great Lakes region of North America, historically centred in the lands around Lake Michigan and Lake Huron in what are now Michigan, Wisconsin, Indiana, Illinois, and Ontario. Their autonym is Bodéwadmi, "Keepers of the Fire," reflecting their role in the Council of Three Fires, the alliance with the Ojibwe (the elder brother) and the Odawa (the middle brother), in which the Potawatomi were the youngest brother charged with keeping the council fire. The Potawatomi language belongs to the Algonquian family and is closely related to Ojibwe.

History

Oral traditions and archaeology trace the Neshnabé migration from the Atlantic coast to the Great Lakes. In the 17th century, fleeing Iroquois attacks during the Beaver Wars, Potawatomi communities took refuge around Green Bay and the Door Peninsula in Wisconsin, where they became major middlemen in the French fur trade. They allied with the French, fought in Pontiac's War and — after 1763 — largely with the British, then with the Northwestern confederacy of the young United States' wars; Potawatomi warriors participated in the War of 1812, including the Fort Dearborn fight at Chicago. Through a long series of treaties (1795–1833), culminating in the 1833 Treaty of Chicago, the Potawatomi ceded nearly all their remaining lands east of the Mississippi.

Removal and survival

Most Potawatomi were removed west to Kansas and Indian Territory (Oklahoma) in the 1830s. The removal of the Indiana Potawatomi in 1838 — the Potawatomi Trail of Death, a forced march of some 660 miles from Twin Lakes, Indiana, to Kansas, during which more than 40 people died — is commemorated by markers along the route. Some communities avoided removal: the Pokagon Band, whose leader Leopold Pokagon negotiated a provision to remain in Michigan; the Forest County Potawatomi in Wisconsin; and groups that crossed into Canada, founding communities such as Walpole Island First Nation. In the 20th century many bands won federal recognition or its restoration, and today there are nine federally recognised Potawatomi tribes in the United States plus several First Nations in Canada. The Citizen Potawatomi Nation in Oklahoma, with tens of thousands of citizens, is the largest.

Culture

Traditionally, Potawatomi villages practised a seasonal round of corn-bean-squash agriculture, fishing, hunting, and maple sugaring, organised in clan (doodem) kinship networks. The Midewiwin (Grand Medicine Society) was an important religious institution, now maintained by some communities alongside Christian practice. Language revitalisation efforts are under way across the Potawatomi nations, as the language is critically endangered, with fluent first-language speakers numbering only in the dozens.
