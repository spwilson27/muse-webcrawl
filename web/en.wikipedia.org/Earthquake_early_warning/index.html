Earthquake early warning

An earthquake early warning (EEW) system is a system that detects an earthquake in progress and issues alerts before the damaging shaking reaches populated areas. Such systems exploit the fact that the first seismic waves to arrive, the P-waves, travel faster and cause little damage compared with the slower S-waves and surface waves that follow.

Principle

Sensors near the epicentre detect the P-waves and transmit data electronically — at nearly the speed of light — to processing centres that estimate the quake's magnitude and the distribution of expected shaking. Alerts can then reach more distant locations seconds to more than a minute before strong shaking, depending on the distance from the epicentre.

Systems worldwide

Japan's nationwide system, operated by the Japan Meteorological Agency since 2007, is the most extensive. Mexico has operated a seismic alert system for Mexico City since the 1990s, warning of large earthquakes off the Pacific coast. In the United States, the ShakeAlert system provides warnings along the West Coast. Taiwan, South Korea, China, and several other countries also operate or are developing systems.

Limitations

Early warning cannot predict earthquakes before they begin; it can only race the seismic waves after rupture starts. Warning time is zero or negative near the epicentre, and rapid magnitude estimates may underestimate the size of the very largest earthquakes.
