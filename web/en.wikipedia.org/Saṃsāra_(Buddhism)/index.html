Saṃsāra (Buddhism)

Saṃsāra (Sanskrit: संसार; Pali: saṃsāra) in Buddhism is the beginningless cycle of repeated birth, mundane existence, and dying again that all beings pass through. It is characterized by duḥkha (suffering, dissatisfaction) and by impermanence, and it is driven by ignorance (avidyā) and the defilements of greed, hatred, and delusion. Liberation from saṃsāra—nirvana—is the goal of Buddhist practice.

Nature of the cycle

Buddhist cosmology describes rebirth into six realms (gati): the realms of gods (devas), demigods (asuras), humans, animals, hungry ghosts (pretas), and hells (naraka). Rebirth is determined by karma, the moral quality of one's intentional actions, yet Buddhism denies that any permanent soul (ātman) transmigrates; the doctrine of anattā (non-self) holds that what continues is a causal stream of consciousness, conventionally compared to one candle lighting another. The mechanism of the cycle is analysed in the twelve links of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda): conditioned by ignorance, formations arise, and so through feeling, craving, and clinging to becoming, birth, ageing, and death.

Saṃsāra and nirvana

The Four Noble Truths diagnose existence within saṃsāra as duḥkha, identify craving as its origin, affirm its cessation, and prescribe the Noble Eightfold Path as the way out. In Mahayana thought, especially following Nagarjuna, saṃsāra and nirvana are said to be not different in ultimate nature—both empty of inherent existence (śūnyatā)—differing only in whether that emptiness is realized. The bodhisattva, out of compassion, is said to remain engaged with the world rather than seek private escape from the cycle.

The wheel of life

The bhavacakra, or wheel of life, is a visual summary of saṃsāra common in Tibetan Buddhism: the six realms arranged in a wheel held by Yama, lord of death, with the three poisons—ignorance, attachment, and aversion, pictured as a pig, a snake, and a bird—biting each other's tails at the hub. The Buddha stands outside the wheel, pointing to the moon of nirvana, indicating that liberation lies beyond the cycle.
