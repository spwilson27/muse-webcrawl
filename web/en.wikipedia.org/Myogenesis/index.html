Myogenesis


Labelled image: stages of skeletal muscle fiber formation

Myogenesis is the formation of skeletal muscle tissue, particularly during embryonic development. Muscle fibers generally form through the fusion of precursor myoblasts into multinucleated fibers called myotubes. It is one of the earliest differentiation processes to occur in the developing embryo and is governed by a family of transcription factors known as the myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs).

Stages

In early development, cells of the somites — segmented blocks of paraxial mesoderm lying either side of the neural tube — are committed to the myogenic lineage. Under signals such as Wnt and Sonic hedgehog, somitic cells express the paired-box transcription factors Pax3 and Pax7. These cells delaminate and migrate to sites of future muscle, where they begin expressing the MRFs MyoD and Myf5.

The myogenic regulatory factors — MyoD, Myf5, myogenin and MRF4 (Myf6) — are basic helix-loop-helix transcription factors that activate the muscle gene expression program. Proliferating myoblasts withdraw from the cell cycle, align, and fuse into primary myotubes. Myogenin expression drives terminal differentiation, including expression of contractile proteins such as myosin heavy chain and actin. Secondary myotubes later form alongside the primary scaffold, and the maturing fibers become innervated and vascularized.

Molecular regulation

Myogenesis is restrained by negative regulators including the Notch signaling pathway, which keeps progenitor cells in an undifferentiated, proliferative state, and growth factors such as myostatin (GDF-8), a member of the TGF-β superfamily that limits muscle mass. Muscle satellite cells, resident beneath the basal lamina of adult fibers, retain Pax7 expression and mediate postnatal growth and regeneration after injury by re-entering the myogenic program.
