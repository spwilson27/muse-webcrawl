History of Poland (1918–1939)

The history of Poland from 1918 to 1939 covers the period of the Second Polish Republic, established after World War I following 123 years of partition among Russia, Prussia, and Austria. The interwar state faced the tasks of unifying three former partition zones, fighting border wars, and surviving between Germany and the Soviet Union before the joint invasion of September 1939 began World War II.

Rebirth of the Polish state

On 11 November 1918 Józef Piłsudski assumed power in Warsaw, and Poland regained independence. The Treaty of Versailles and the Polish–Soviet War (1919–1921) settled much of the new state's borders; the Polish victory at the Battle of Warsaw in August 1920 halted the Red Army's advance. The March Constitution of 1921 established a parliamentary republic.

The May Coup and Sanacja

Political fragmentation and economic crisis led Piłsudski to stage the May Coup of 1926, after which Poland was governed by the authoritarian-leaning Sanacja regime. Piłsudski dominated Polish politics until his death in 1935, pursuing a policy of balance between Germany and the USSR, formalized in non-aggression pacts with both in 1932 and 1934.

Society and economy

Interwar Poland was a multinational state: ethnic Poles made up roughly two-thirds of the population of about 35 million, alongside large Ukrainian, Jewish, Belarusian, and German minorities. The Great Depression hit the largely agricultural economy hard. Achievements included the construction of the port of Gdynia and the Central Industrial Region (COP).

Road to war

After Hitler's rise, Polish foreign minister Józef Beck sought to preserve independence through balanced diplomacy. Germany's demands regarding Danzig and the Polish Corridor in 1938–1939, followed by the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of August 1939, sealed Poland's fate. Germany invaded on 1 September 1939 and the Soviet Union on 17 September, partitioning the country between them.
