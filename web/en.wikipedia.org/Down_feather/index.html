Down feather

The down of birds is a layer of fine feathers found under the tougher exterior contour feathers. Down feathers have a short, vestigial shaft (rachis), a few long barbules, and no hooklets, giving them a fluffy structure that traps air close to the bird's body and provides excellent thermal insulation. Because of this, down from ducks and geese has long been used by humans as a soft, warm filling for bedding and clothing.

Structure and function

Unlike contour feathers, down barbs lack the barbules and hooks that zip a feather into a smooth vane, so a down cluster forms a three-dimensional tangle of filaments that holds a large volume of still air. In adult birds down keeps the body warm, particularly for waterfowl swimming in cold water; in chicks of precocial species, natal down provides their only insulation. Powder down, a specialised type found in herons and parrots, disintegrates into fine keratin powder used in preening and waterproofing.

Human use

Eiderdown, gathered from the nests of common eiders (where the female lines the nest with down plucked from her breast), is the most prized filling, famed for lightness and cohesion; Iceland is its historic source. Commercial down is mostly a byproduct of the meat industry, collected from slaughtered geese and ducks, sorted, washed, and rated by fill power — the volume an ounce of down occupies, with high-quality goose down reaching 700–900 cubic inches per ounce. Down fills duvets (doonas/quilts), pillows, parkas, and sleeping bags prized by mountaineers. Because down clumps and loses insulation when wet, synthetic alternatives and hydrophobically treated down are used for damp conditions. Animal-welfare concerns about live-plucking and force-feeding have prompted standards such as the Responsible Down Standard.
