Honor society

An honor society is an organisation, most common in American education, that recognises excellence among peers, usually students who rank highly in scholarship or leadership. Membership is typically by invitation and is marked by insignia, cords or pins worn at graduation.

Honor society members often wear distinctive cords or stoles at graduation.

History

The oldest American honor society is Phi Beta Kappa, founded in 1776 at the College of William & Mary as a social and literary society; it evolved into a scholastic honor society for the liberal arts and sciences. Its Greek-letter model inspired both fraternities and later discipline-specific societies.

Types

General societies such as Phi Beta Kappa (arts and sciences) and Phi Kappa Phi (all disciplines) sit alongside field-specific societies: Sigma Xi (scientific research), Tau Beta Pi (engineering), Beta Gamma Sigma (business) and many others. High-school students are recognised by the National Honor Society, founded in 1921. The Association of College Honor Societies, founded in 1925, certifies legitimate societies.

Membership and criticism

Selection is generally based on grade-point thresholds and class rank, sometimes combined with service or character requirements. Critics note that some commercial "honor societies" charge fees with minimal selectivity, prompting advisories distinguishing them from established, campus-based societies.
