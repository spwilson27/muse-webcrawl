Disruptive innovation

In business theory, disruptive innovation is innovation that creates a new market and value network or enters at the bottom of an existing market and eventually displaces established market-leading firms, products, and alliances. The term was defined and the phenomenon analysed by the American scholar Clayton M. Christensen and his collaborators beginning in 1995.

The theory

Christensen distinguishes disruptive innovations from sustaining innovations, which improve products along dimensions that existing customers already value. Disruptive products initially underperform on mainstream measures but offer other attributes — simplicity, convenience, lower price — that appeal either to the least profitable customers of an existing market (low-end disruption) or to people who previously lacked the money or skill to consume at all (new-market disruption).

Because established firms are motivated to serve their most profitable customers, they rationally ignore the foothold market. The entrant then improves its offering until it meets mainstream needs, at which point the incumbents' positions collapse. Christensen developed the theory from case studies of the disk drive industry, excavators, steel minimills and discount retailing in his 1997 book The Innovator's Dilemma.

Examples

Frequently cited examples include personal computers displacing minicomputers and mainframes, transistor radios displacing vacuum-tube models, digital photography displacing film, and streaming video displacing video rental stores. Not every successful new technology qualifies: Christensen argued that ride-hailing services such as Uber, which entered the mainstream market directly with a superior service, are not disruptive in the technical sense.

Criticism

The theory has been widely influential in business and public policy, but scholars have questioned its predictive power. A 2015 re-examination of the original case studies found that many did not fit the theory's key elements. Critics also note the term's loose popular usage, in which "disruptive" has become a synonym for any novel or successful innovation. Christensen responded by restating the theory's precise scope in "What Is Disruptive Innovation?", published in the Harvard Business Review in 2015.
