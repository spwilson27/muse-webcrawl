Arrhythmia, also known as cardiac arrhythmia or heart arrhythmia, is a group of conditions in which the heartbeat is irregular, too fast, or too slow. A heart rate above 100 beats per minute at rest is tachycardia, and below 60 is bradycardia. Many arrhythmias are harmless, but some predispose to stroke, heart failure, or sudden cardiac death.

Classification

Arrhythmias are classified by rate and by the site of the problem in the heart's electrical conduction system. Supraventricular arrhythmias arise in the atria or AV node and include atrial fibrillation (the most common sustained arrhythmia), atrial flutter, and paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia. Ventricular arrhythmias — ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation — are more immediately life-threatening. Bradyarrhythmias include sinus node dysfunction and heart block. Inherited channelopathies such as long QT syndrome and Brugada syndrome cause arrhythmias in structurally normal hearts.

Causes and diagnosis

Causes include coronary artery disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, valvular disease, thyroid disorders, electrolyte abnormalities, and drugs including alcohol and caffeine. Diagnosis rests on the electrocardiogram; longer-term monitoring uses Holter or patch monitors and implantable recorders, and an electrophysiology study can locate the abnormal circuit.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the type: rate- or rhythm-controlling drugs, anticoagulation to prevent stroke in atrial fibrillation, electrical cardioversion, catheter ablation of the arrhythmia focus, and implantable pacemakers or defibrillators (ICDs). Ventricular fibrillation requires immediate CPR and defibrillation.
