Dynorphins are a class of opioid peptides that arise from the precursor protein prodynorphin. When prodynorphin is cleaved by proprotein convertase 2, multiple active peptides are released, including dynorphin A, dynorphin B, and α/β-neoendorphin. Dynorphins exert their effects primarily through the kappa opioid receptor (KOR), though they have some affinity for mu and delta opioid receptors as well.

Discovery

Dynorphin was isolated in 1979 by Avram Goldstein and colleagues from porcine pituitary tissue; its name derives from the Greek dynamis (power), reflecting its extraordinary potency in bioassays — dynorphin A (1–17) was found to be far more potent than morphine in the guinea pig ileum preparation.

Functions

Dynorphins are widely distributed in the central nervous system, including the hypothalamus, striatum, hippocampus, and spinal cord. KOR activation produces analgesia but also dysphoria, aversion, and sedation, distinguishing dynorphin signaling from the euphoria associated with mu receptor agonists. The dynorphin/KOR system is heavily implicated in stress responses, mood regulation, addiction, and relapse: stress triggers dynorphin release, and KOR antagonists have been investigated as potential treatments for depression and substance use disorders.

Non-opioid actions

At high concentrations dynorphins also act at NMDA receptors and other non-opioid targets, effects thought to contribute to spinal cord injury pathology and chronic pain states in which dynorphin is upregulated.
