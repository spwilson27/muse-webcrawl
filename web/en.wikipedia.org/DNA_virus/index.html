DNA virus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A DNA virus is a virus whose genetic material is DNA. In the Baltimore classification, DNA viruses fall mainly into Group I (double-stranded DNA viruses) and Group II (single-stranded DNA viruses); a smaller set of double-stranded DNA viruses that replicate through an RNA intermediate (Group VII) includes the hepatitis B virus.

Group I: double-stranded DNA viruses

dsDNA viruses include most of the large, complex viruses of animals and the tailed bacteriophages. Hosts range from bacteria (e.g., phage T4 and lambda) to humans. Human pathogens in this group include the herpesviruses (herpes simplex, varicella-zoster, Epstein–Barr, cytomegalovirus), the poxviruses (variola, the cause of smallpox, and mpox), adenoviruses, and papillomaviruses and polyomaviruses, some of which are oncogenic. The giant viruses of amoebae — mimiviruses, pandoraviruses, and pithoviruses — are also dsDNA viruses, with genomes rivalling small bacteria.

Group II: single-stranded DNA viruses

ssDNA viruses are generally small, with circular genomes that are converted to double-stranded forms in the host cell and replicated by rolling-circle mechanisms. They include the parvoviruses (such as human parvovirus B19, cause of fifth disease, and the dependoviruses used as gene-therapy vectors — adeno-associated viruses), circoviruses of animals, and many plant pathogens such as geminiviruses.

Replication

Most DNA viruses replicate their genomes in the host nucleus using host DNA polymerases, giving them lower mutation rates than RNA viruses; some large viruses encode their own polymerases and proofreading enzymes, and herpesviruses can establish latency, persisting as episomes for the life of the host. Poxviruses are an exception that replicates entirely in the cytoplasm.
