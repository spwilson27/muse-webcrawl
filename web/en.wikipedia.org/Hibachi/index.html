Hibachi

A traditional hibachi

The hibachi (Japanese: 火鉢, "fire bowl") is a traditional Japanese heating device. It is a round, cylindrical or box-shaped open-topped container made of heatproof materials such as porcelain, metal or wood lined with ceramic, designed to hold burning charcoal.

Traditional use

Hibachi have been used in Japan since at least the Heian period, principally as a source of heat for warming hands and rooms rather than for cooking, although they were also used to boil water for tea or keep dishes warm. The charcoal ash bed was an economical and portable heat source in wooden Japanese houses, and ornate hibachi became decorative household furniture in the Edo and Meiji periods. Their use declined with the spread of modern heating in the 20th century.

Western usage of the term

In North America, "hibachi" came to mean something different: a small portable charcoal grill, or, in Japanese restaurants abroad, the flat iron griddle (more properly a teppan) around which chefs cook theatrically before seated guests — the style popularised by chains such as Benihana, founded in 1964. What Japanese cooks call a small charcoal grill for food is instead the shichirin. The confusion of terms is sometimes called the "hibachi style" of teppanyaki dining.
