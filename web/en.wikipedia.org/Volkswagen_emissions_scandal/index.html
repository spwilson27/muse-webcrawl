Volkswagen emissions scandal

The Volkswagen emissions scandal, also known as Dieselgate, began in September 2015 when the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) issued a notice of violation to the Volkswagen Group. Volkswagen had intentionally programmed its turbocharged direct-injection (TDI) diesel engines with "defeat device" software that recognised when a car was being emissions-tested and fully activated pollution controls only then; in normal driving, nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions were up to 40 times the legal limit.

Scope and discovery

Volkswagen admitted that about 11 million cars worldwide — including 500,000 in the United States — carried the software. The deception was uncovered after researchers at West Virginia University, commissioned by the International Council on Clean Transportation, measured real-world emissions far above laboratory results and worked with regulators to confront the company.

Consequences

The scandal forced the resignation of CEO Martin Winterkorn, collapsed Volkswagen's share price, and led to criminal prosecutions of executives and engineers. Volkswagen agreed to settlements and fines exceeding US$30 billion, including a $14.7 billion settlement in the US covering buybacks and environmental remediation, and later pleaded guilty to criminal charges. "Dieselgate" discredited diesel passenger cars in North America, widened emissions-testing reform in Europe (real-driving emissions tests), and accelerated Volkswagen's pivot to electric vehicles.
