Maya (religion)

Maya (Sanskrit: माया, māyā) is a concept in Indian religions, especially Hinduism, Jainism, Buddhism, and Sikhism, variously translated as "illusion", "magic", or "that which is ever-changing". At its most developed, in the school of Advaita Vedanta, Maya is the powerful creative force by which the world of multiplicity appears, veiling the true, unitary nature of Brahman and the Atman (self).

Etymology and early usage

In the Rigveda, māyā denotes supernatural power or craft — the ability of gods and demons to change form and create appearances. In the Upanishads, it develops toward the sense of cosmic illusion, famously in the Shvetashvatara Upanishad, which identifies nature (prakriti) as Maya and God as the wielder of Maya.

In Advaita Vedanta

For Adi Shankara (8th century CE), the world of name and form is superimposed (adhyāsa) on Brahman through ignorance (avidyā), which is identified with Maya. Maya is neither real nor unreal — it is anirvacaniya, indescribable — like the snake mistakenly seen in a rope: it appears while ignorance lasts and vanishes when knowledge (jnana) dawns. Liberation (moksha) comes from realising the identity of Atman and Brahman.

In Buddhism and other traditions

In early Buddhism, Maya is the name of the Buddha's mother; in Mahayana texts, "maya" illustrates the illusory, dreamlike character of phenomena, connected with emptiness (śūnyatā). In Jainism, maya is one of the four passions (kashaya): deceit. In Sikhism, Maya is the temporary allure of the material world that distracts from God.
