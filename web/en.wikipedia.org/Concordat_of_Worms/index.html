The Concordat of Worms (Latin: Concordatum Wormatiense), also known as the Pactum Callixtinum, was an agreement between Pope Callixtus II and Holy Roman Emperor Henry V signed on 23 September 1122 near the city of Worms. It ended the first phase of the power struggle between the papacy and the Holy Roman Empire, known as the Investiture Controversy.

Background

The Investiture Controversy began in 1076 when Pope Gregory VII clashed with Emperor Henry IV over who had the right to appoint ("invest") bishops and abbots — officials who were both church leaders and major landholders and administrators of the empire. The conflict produced excommunications, civil war in Germany, and Henry IV's famous penitential journey to Canossa in 1077. It continued under Henry V, who had forced Pope Paschal II to concede investiture rights in 1111, a concession the Church promptly repudiated.

Terms

The Concordat distinguished the spiritual and temporal aspects of a bishop's office. The emperor renounced investiture with the ring and crosier — the symbols of spiritual authority — and granted freedom of election to cathedral chapters. The pope, in return, conceded that in Germany elections could take place in the emperor's presence, and that the emperor could invest the bishop-elect with the regalia (lands and secular rights) by the sceptre before consecration; in Italy and Burgundy this temporal investiture followed consecration.

Significance

The settlement was a compromise that favoured the Church: it confirmed that bishops owed their spiritual office to the Church alone, weakening imperial control of the German Church. At the same time it preserved substantial imperial influence in Germany. The Concordat was ratified by the First Lateran Council in 1123 and became a landmark in the development of the distinction between spiritual and secular authority in medieval Europe.
