Full employment

Macroeconomic condition and policy goal

Full employment is the condition in an economy in which all available labour resources are being used in the most efficient way possible — conventionally, when unemployment has fallen to the level accounted for by frictional and structural factors, with no cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment remaining. It does not mean zero unemployment, since people are always moving between jobs or entering the workforce.

Definitions

Economists relate full employment to the NAIRU (non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment): the unemployment rate below which inflation tends to rise as employers bid up wages. Estimates vary by country and era — often placed between about 4% and 6% for the United States — and the concept is contested because the NAIRU cannot be observed directly. William Beveridge, in his 1944 report Full Employment in a Free Society, defined it as having more vacant jobs than unemployed workers, around 3% unemployment.

Policy

Following Keynes, many post-war governments adopted full employment as an explicit goal, pursued through fiscal and monetary policy; legislation such as the U.S. Employment Act of 1946 and the Humphrey–Hawkins Act of 1978, and the Federal Reserve's dual mandate, reflect this commitment. From the 1970s, concerns about inflation shifted emphasis toward price stability, and the Phillips curve trade-off between inflation and unemployment became central to central-bank practice. Some economists and the Modern Monetary Theory school advocate job guarantee programmes to achieve full employment directly.
