Waardenburg syndrome

Waardenburg syndrome is a group of rare genetic conditions characterised by varying degrees of congenital hearing loss and changes in pigmentation of the hair, skin, and eyes. It is named after the Dutch ophthalmologist Petrus Johannes Waardenburg, who defined the syndrome in 1951. The condition results from defects in the development of neural crest cells, which give rise to melanocytes (pigment cells) and structures of the inner ear.

Signs and symptoms

Hallmark features include a white forelock or premature greying of the hair, differently coloured eyes (complete or partial heterochromia iridum), and, in type I, dystopia canthorum — a widening of the inner corners of the eyes. Sensorineural hearing loss is present in a substantial fraction of cases and can affect one or both ears. Most people with the syndrome have normal intelligence and lifespan.

Types and genetics

Four main types are recognised. Type I (with dystopia canthorum) is caused by mutations in PAX3; type II (without dystopia) most often involves MITF and other genes; type III (Klein–Waardenburg) adds limb abnormalities and is also due to PAX3; type IV (Waardenburg–Shah) combines the pigmentary and hearing features with Hirschsprung disease (congenital absence of intestinal ganglion cells) and involves genes such as EDN3, EDNRB, and SOX10. Inheritance is usually autosomal dominant.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is clinical, using criteria such as those of the Waardenburg Consortium, and can be confirmed by genetic testing. Management focuses on detecting and treating hearing loss early (hearing aids or cochlear implants), sun protection for depigmented skin, and, in type IV, management of Hirschsprung disease.
