Herbert Spencer (27 April 1820 – 8 December 1903) was an English philosopher, psychologist, biologist, sociologist, and prominent classical liberal political theorist of the Victorian era. Spencer developed an all-embracing conception of evolution as the progressive development of the physical world, biological organisms, the human mind, and human culture and societies. He coined the expression "survival of the fittest", which he saw as also applying to human societies.

Life

Born in Derby and largely self-educated, Spencer worked as a railway engineer and then as a journalist, becoming sub-editor of The Economist. His first book, Social Statics (1851), argued from a "law of equal freedom" for radically limited government. Living on a small inheritance and writing for periodicals, he spent four decades producing his "Synthetic Philosophy" — a ten-volume system covering biology, psychology, sociology, and ethics.

Evolution and the Synthetic Philosophy

Spencer proposed a universal law of evolution: matter passes from an indefinite, incoherent homogeneity to a definite, coherent heterogeneity. He championed evolution before Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) and often favoured Lamarckian inheritance of acquired characteristics. His Principles of Biology (1864) introduced "survival of the fittest", a phrase Darwin later adopted. In sociology — a field he helped found — he drew an analogy between society and an organism, and traced social evolution from "militant" to "industrial" types.

Political thought

A staunch individualist, Spencer opposed state education, poor laws, and most government regulation, arguing in The Man Versus the State (1884) that expanding state power threatened liberty. His label "Social Darwinism" was applied by later critics, though Spencer's views predated Darwin and differed from the doctrine attached to his name.

Influence

In his lifetime Spencer was one of the most famous intellectuals in the world, read across Europe, America, and Asia. His reputation collapsed in the 20th century as his system fell out of fashion, but historians recognise his pioneering role in sociology and his influence on the idea of evolution in culture.
