Fresnel lens

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Fresnel lens (pronounced "fray-NELL") is a type of composite compact lens which reduces the amount of material required compared to a conventional lens by dividing the lens into a set of concentric annular sections. It was developed by the French civil engineer and physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel for use in lighthouses, where it allowed powerful lenses of large aperture and short focal length to be built without the mass and volume of a conventional design.

Design

The design mimics a conventional lens by keeping the curvature of each annular section — each ring behaves like an individual prism — while removing most of the material between surfaces. Each ring refracts light towards a common focal point, so the lens collimates or focuses light much like its conventional counterpart, though with some loss of image quality at the ring boundaries.

Lighthouse use

Fresnel lenses for lighthouses were classified by "orders" according to size and focal length, from the huge first-order lenses over 3.5 metres tall down to small sixth-order lenses. First installed at the Cordouan lighthouse in France in 1823, the design dramatically extended the range of lighthouse beams — large lenses could capture and project light visible for more than 30 kilometres — and was adopted worldwide.

Modern applications

Thin, flat Fresnel lenses moulded in plastic are now used as sheet magnifiers, in overhead projectors, traffic lights, car headlights, solar concentrators, and the screens of some rear-projection televisions. In lighthouses, many historic Fresnel lenses remain prized museum objects even where LED lamps have replaced them in service.
