Rain and snow mixed

Rain and snow mixed (often abbreviated as wintry mix in the United States) is precipitation composed of a mixture of rain and snow. It typically occurs during transitions between rain and snow situations, when surface temperatures hover near freezing and a shallow layer of warm air partially melts falling snowflakes before they reach the ground.

Partially melted snowflakes falling with rain near a surface temperature of 0 °C.

Formation

Snowflakes descend from cold air aloft and pass through a layer of above-freezing air that is too shallow or too weak to melt them completely. The partially melted flakes, sometimes refreezing into ice pellets (sleet in American usage) if a deep sub-freezing layer lies near the surface, produce the mixture. In present-weather reporting, METAR code uses "RASN" for the phenomenon, and the United Kingdom Met Office term sleet traditionally refers to this rain–snow mixture rather than to ice pellets.

Impacts

Wintry mix creates hazardous travel conditions because the type of precipitation can change quickly over short distances and because wet snow accumulates on trees and power lines. Forecasters emphasize that small changes in the vertical temperature profile can shift an area from rain to snow to mix, making such events difficult to predict precisely.

Terminology

The phrase "rain and snow mixed" is the formal National Weather Service present-weather description. Colloquial terms vary regionally; "wintry mix" covers combinations that may also include freezing rain and ice pellets.
