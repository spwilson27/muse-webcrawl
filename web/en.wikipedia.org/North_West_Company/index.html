The North West Company was a fur trading business headquartered in Montreal from 1779 to 1821. It competed with increasing success against the Hudson's Bay Company in what is now western Canada and the northwestern United States. At its peak in the early 19th century, the company employed some 6,000 people and controlled a large share of the North American fur trade.

History

After the British conquest of New France, independent Montreal traders — many of them Scots — moved into the interior fur trade. In 1779 sixteen trading firms combined for a season; a more durable partnership was organised in 1783–84 under figures including Simon McTavish. The company's agents, the French-Canadian voyageurs, travelled by birchbark canoe from Montreal to the great rendezvous at Grand Portage and later Fort William on Lake Superior, where wintering partners exchanged furs and trade goods.

Expansion and exploration

The company's wintering partners pushed the trade across the continent. Alexander Mackenzie, a North West Company partner, reached the Arctic Ocean in 1789 and the Pacific in 1793 — the first recorded crossing of North America north of Mexico. Simon Fraser explored the river named for him in 1808, and David Thompson mapped much of the Columbia River system. Forts were established as far west as New Caledonia and the Columbia District.

Rivalry and merger

Competition with the Hudson's Bay Company grew violent in the Red River Colony, culminating in the Battle of Seven Oaks in 1816. Faced with mounting costs and pressure from the British government, the two companies merged in 1821 under the Hudson's Bay Company name, ending the North West Company's independent existence. A modern Canadian retail company, The North West Company Inc., traces its lineage to the successor enterprise.
