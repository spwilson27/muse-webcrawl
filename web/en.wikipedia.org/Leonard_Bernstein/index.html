Leonard Bernstein

Leonard Bernstein (born Louis Bernstein; August 25, 1918 – October 14, 1990) was an American conductor, composer, pianist, music educator, author, and humanitarian. Considered to be one of the most important conductors of his time, he was the first American-born conductor to lead an American orchestra, as music director of the New York Philharmonic. Bernstein was also a well known composer whose best known works include the Broadway musical West Side Story, the operetta Candide, and three symphonies. His televised Young People's Concerts brought classical music to a mass audience.

Early life and debut

Bernstein was born in Lawrence, Massachusetts, to Ukrainian Jewish immigrant parents. He studied piano as a child, graduated from Harvard University in 1939, and trained in conducting at the Curtis Institute of Music under Fritz Reiner, also studying with Serge Koussevitzky at Tanglewood. In 1943 he became assistant conductor of the New York Philharmonic; on 14 November 1943, substituting at short notice for an ill Bruno Walter, he conducted the orchestra in a nationally broadcast concert and became famous overnight.

Career

Bernstein was music director of the New York Philharmonic from 1958 to 1969, leading celebrated Mahler and American-music performances and making numerous recordings. He was a frequent guest conductor of the world's leading orchestras, notably the Vienna Philharmonic and the Israel Philharmonic, and a central figure at the Tanglewood Festival. As a composer he moved between Broadway, Hollywood (On the Waterfront), ballet (Fancy Free), symphony and choral music (Chichester Psalms, 1965; Mass, 1971). His Young People's Concerts (1958–1972) and his Norton Lectures at Harvard, published as The Unanswered Question, made him the most prominent music educator of his era.

Later life and legacy

In December 1989 Bernstein conducted Beethoven's Ninth Symphony in Berlin to celebrate the fall of the Berlin Wall, substituting "Freiheit" (freedom) for "Freude" (joy) in the finale. He died of a heart attack in New York on 14 October 1990, days after announcing his retirement from conducting. His centenary in 2018 was marked by worldwide celebrations, and his life was dramatised in the 2023 film Maestro.
