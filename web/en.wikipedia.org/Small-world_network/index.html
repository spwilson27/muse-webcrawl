Small-world network


A small-world graph: clustered, with shortcuts

A small-world network is a mathematical graph in which most nodes are not neighbours of one another, but the neighbours of any given node are likely to be neighbours of each other, and most nodes can be reached from every other node by a small number of steps. Specifically, a network is said to show the small-world effect if its average shortest path length grows proportionally to the logarithm of the number of nodes, while its clustering coefficient remains high.

The Watts–Strogatz model

In 1998 Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz published a model that interpolates between a regular lattice and a random graph: starting from a ring lattice, each edge is rewired with some probability p. For small p, the graph keeps high clustering but its path length collapses — a small number of long-range "shortcut" edges suffice to make the world small. Their paper demonstrated small-world properties in the film-actor collaboration graph, the US power grid, and the neural network of the worm C. elegans.

Origins and examples

The concept grew out of Stanley Milgram's "six degrees of separation" experiments of the 1960s, in which letters reached a target person through a median of about six acquaintances. Small-world structure has since been reported in social networks, the World Wide Web, metabolic and protein interaction networks, and neural networks, and it influences how diseases and information spread through populations.
