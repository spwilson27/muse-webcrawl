Protestant Reformation

The Protestant Reformation, also called the Reformation, was a major movement within Western Christianity in 16th-century Europe that posed a religious and political challenge to the papacy and the Roman Catholic Church. Traditionally dated to begin on 31 October 1517, when the German monk Martin Luther circulated his Ninety-five Theses criticising the sale of indulgences, it ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, and it gave rise to the branch of Christianity known as Protestantism.

Causes

Precursors such as John Wycliffe and Jan Hus had criticised church doctrine and corruption centuries earlier, and movements such as the pre-Reformation debates over indulgences, conciliarism, and humanist biblical scholarship (notably Erasmus's Greek New Testament of 1516) prepared the ground. The printing press enabled the rapid spread of reforming pamphlets and vernacular Bibles. Political factors—princes seeking autonomy from emperor and pope, and resentment of ecclesiastical taxation—also drove rulers to protect reformers.

Course

Summoned before the Diet of Worms in 1521 and refusing to recant, Luther was excommunicated and outlawed; protected by Frederick the Wise of Saxony, he translated the New Testament into German. The movement divided into streams: the Lutheran churches in Germany and Scandinavia; the Reformed tradition of Zwingli in Zurich and Calvin in Geneva; the Radical Reformation of the Anabaptists; and the English Reformation under Henry VIII, which produced the Church of England. The Catholic response—the Counter-Reformation, including the Council of Trent (1545–1563)—reformed discipline while reaffirming doctrine.

Confessional conflict fuelled wars of religion across Europe, including the German Peasants' War (1524–1525), the French Wars of Religion (1562–1598), and the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648). The Peace of Augsburg (1555) had granted rulers the right to determine their territory's religion (cuius regio, eius religio); the Peace of Westphalia extended recognition to the Reformed faith and stabilised the confessional map.

Core doctrines

Protestant distinctives are often summarised by the Five solae: sola scriptura (Scripture alone as final authority), sola fide (justification by faith alone), sola gratia (salvation by grace alone), solus Christus (through Christ alone), and soli Deo gloria (glory to God alone). Protestants generally rejected the sacramental system of the medieval church beyond baptism and the Eucharist, clerical celibacy, monasticism, and the doctrine of purgatory.

Legacy

The Reformation permanently ended the religious unity of Western Europe, reshaped politics, education, and literacy (through catechisms and vernacular Bibles), and influenced capitalism, individualism, and modern states in ways debated by scholars since Max Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905). It remains the originating event of the world's Protestant churches, which today number hundreds of millions of adherents.
