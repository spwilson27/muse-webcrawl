Laity

In religious organisations, the laity consists of all members who are not part of the clergy — that is, not ordained ministers, priests, or other consecrated religious officials. A layperson (also layman or laywoman) is a member of a religious community who has not received holy orders. The term derives from the Greek laos, "people".

In Christianity

In the Catholic Church, canon law defines the laity as Christian faithful who are not in holy orders or a religious state; the Second Vatican Council's Lumen gentium taught that the laity share in the mission of the Church, particularly by sanctifying secular life. In many Protestant traditions the distinction is minimised under the doctrine of the priesthood of all believers, though lay elders and readers still serve formal roles. In Eastern Orthodoxy, laypeople participate in church governance and worship but cannot administer most sacraments.

In other traditions

Buddhism distinguishes the lay community (upāsaka and upāsikā) from the ordained sangha of monks and nuns; laypeople support the monastics materially and observe five precepts. In Judaism, the term distinguishes ordinary Israelites from the priestly class (kohanim).

Secular usage

By extension, "layperson" in everyday English denotes anyone without specialised knowledge of a subject — for example, a layman's terms explanation of science or law.
