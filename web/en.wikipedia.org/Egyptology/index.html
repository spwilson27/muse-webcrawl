Egyptology is the scientific study of ancient Egypt, covering its history, language, literature, religion, architecture, and art from the 5th millennium BC until the end of its native religious practices in the 4th century AD. Practitioners are called Egyptologists.

History of the discipline

Interest in ancient Egypt existed among the Egyptians themselves and in the classical world, but modern Egyptology began with Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798, whose scholars produced the monumental Description de l'Égypte. The discovery of the Rosetta Stone in 1799 enabled Jean-François Champollion to decipher Egyptian hieroglyphs in 1822, unlocking the written record. In the later 19th century, figures such as Auguste Mariette, who founded the Egyptian Antiquities Service, and Flinders Petrie, who introduced systematic stratigraphic excavation, placed the field on a scientific footing.

Scope

Egyptologists study texts in hieroglyphic, hieratic, demotic, and Coptic scripts; temple and tomb decoration; funerary practices and monuments such as the pyramids; and the material culture of daily life. Major collections are held by the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, the British Museum, the Louvre, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

Major discoveries

Landmark finds include the tomb of Tutankhamun (1922, Howard Carter), the royal cache at Deir el-Bahari, the sunken cities of Thonis-Heracleion, and the workers' town at Deir el-Medina. Contemporary Egyptology increasingly involves Egyptian scholars and addresses questions of provenance and repatriation of artifacts.
