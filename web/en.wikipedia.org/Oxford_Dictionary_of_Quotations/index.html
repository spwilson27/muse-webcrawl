Oxford Dictionary of Quotations

First published: 1941

Publisher: Oxford University Press

Editor (8th ed.): Elizabeth Knowles

8th edition: 2014

The Oxford Dictionary of Quotations is a reference book of quotations first published by Oxford University Press in 1941. Organised alphabetically by author, it collects more than 20,000 quotations from over 3,000 authors in recent editions, with a keyword index enabling readers to trace half-remembered lines. It is one of the best-known dictionaries of quotations in the English-speaking world, a successor in spirit to the tradition established by Bartlett's Familiar Quotations (1855).

History

The first edition of 1941 grew out of a suggestion made in 1927 and years of slip-collecting by Press staff and volunteers; publication was delayed by the outbreak of the Second World War, which also shaped its contents — it closed with wartime speeches by Winston Churchill. Successive editions appeared in 1953, 1979, 1992, 1999, 2004, 2009 and 2014. From the fourth edition (1992) the editors broadened the scope well beyond the traditional literary canon to include popular song, film lines, advertising slogans, political catchphrases and quotations from modern media figures.

Contents and method

Quotations are printed with their sources — book, play, speech or article — and, where relevant, notes on attribution and on common misquotations. The dictionary includes substantial sections from the Bible and the Book of Common Prayer, Shakespeare (the most quoted author), classical Greek and Latin writers in translation, and proverbs and anonymous sayings given separate sections. A companion volume, the Oxford Dictionary of Thematic Quotations, arranges material by subject, and there are specialist offshoots such as dictionaries of humorous, literary and proverbial quotations.
