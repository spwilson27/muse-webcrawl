Melatonin

Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) is a hormone produced by the pineal gland in animals that regulates the sleep–wake cycle. Its production is increased by darkness and suppressed by light detected by the retina, causing levels to rise in the evening, peak in the middle of the night and fall toward morning, thereby entraining the circadian rhythm.

Biosynthesis and function

Melatonin is synthesized from the amino acid tryptophan via serotonin. It was isolated in 1958 by Aaron Lerner's team at Yale, which named it for its ability to lighten amphibian skin by aggregating melanin granules; in mammals its principal role is signalling the length of the night to the body's circadian system, and in many species it also regulates seasonal rhythms of reproduction and physiology. Melatonin is also a potent antioxidant and is produced in small amounts in many other tissues and organisms, including plants.

Medical use

Synthetic melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement in some countries and as a prescription medicine in others. Evidence supports short-term use for jet lag and some sleep disorders, including delayed sleep phase disorder; prolonged-release melatonin is approved in Europe for insomnia in older adults. Common side effects include headache, dizziness and daytime drowsiness.
