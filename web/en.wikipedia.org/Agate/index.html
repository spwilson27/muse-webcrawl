Agate

CategoryChalcedony variety
FormulaSiO2 (silicon dioxide)
Mohs hardness6.5–7
LustreWaxy

Agate is a variety of chalcedony, a cryptocrystalline form of quartz, characterised by its fineness of grain and brightness of colour. It is most commonly found as nodules within volcanic rocks, where silica-rich waters deposit concentric layers along cavity walls, producing the distinctive banding from which the stone takes its patterns.

Formation

Agates form primarily within the vesicles of basaltic lava. After the lava solidifies, dissolved silica precipitates in successive layers; variations in impurities and conditions produce bands of different colours, frequently following the shape of the cavity. Many agates are hollow, with the innermost layer consisting of visible quartz crystals pointing toward a central void — a formation called a geode when the whole nodule is hollow.

Types

Named varieties reflect their patterns and localities: moss agate contains green or black dendritic inclusions resembling moss; fire agate shows iridescent flashes; Botswana agate is finely banded in pink and grey; lace agate has delicate, lacy bands; and onyx is agate with straight, parallel bands of black and white. Agate has been dyed since Roman times to enhance or alter its colour.

Uses

Agate has been used since antiquity for signet rings, seals, cameos, beads, and ornaments. Because it is hard and chemically resistant, it is also used industrially for knife-edge bearings in balances, mortar and pestle sets, and leather burnishing tools. The German town of Idar-Oberstein became a famous centre of agate cutting.
