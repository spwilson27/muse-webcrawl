Great Kantō earthquake

Date: 1 September 1923, 11:58 JST

Magnitude: 7.9 Mw

Epicentre: Sagami Bay, near Izu Ōshima

Casualties: est. 105,000–142,800 dead or missing

The Great Kantō earthquake struck the Kantō Plain on the Japanese island of Honshū at 11:58:44 JST on Saturday, 1 September 1923. The earthquake, with an estimated magnitude of 7.9, devastated Tokyo, the port city of Yokohama, and the surrounding prefectures of Chiba, Kanagawa and Shizuoka. It remains one of the deadliest natural disasters in Japanese history.

The disaster

The quake struck just before noon, when many households were cooking lunch over open flames. Fires broke out across the wooden-built city of Tokyo and merged into a firestorm; a fire whirl at a former army clothing depot in Honjo killed tens of thousands of people who had taken refuge there. The shaking, fires and a tsunami along the coast of Sagami Bay destroyed hundreds of thousands of buildings. Estimates of the death toll range from about 105,000 to more than 140,000, with many thousands more injured and over a million left homeless.

Aftermath

Martial law was declared. In the chaos, rumours that Koreans and others were committing crimes led to vigilante and military massacres of thousands of ethnic Koreans and others — the Kantō Massacre — a subject of continuing historical controversy. Reconstruction under Home Minister Gotō Shinpei rebuilt Tokyo with wider streets, parks and modern infrastructure, though budget cuts scaled back the grandest plans. September 1 is now observed in Japan as Disaster Prevention Day.

Geology

The earthquake resulted from rupture of the Sagami Trough, where the Philippine Sea Plate subducts beneath the Okhotsk Plate. Shaking lasted between four and ten minutes, and strong aftershocks followed for days.
