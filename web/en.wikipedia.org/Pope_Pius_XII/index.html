Pope Pius XII (born Eugenio Maria Giuseppe Giovanni Pacelli; 2 March 1876 – 9 October 1958) was head of the Catholic Church and sovereign of the Vatican City State from 2 March 1939 until his death on 9 October 1958. His reign of nearly twenty years covered the Second World War and the early Cold War, making him one of the most consequential popes of the twentieth century.

Before his election, Pacelli had a long career in the papal diplomatic service. He served as Apostolic Nuncio to Bavaria and then to Germany, as Cardinal Secretary of State under Pius XI, and negotiated concordats with several states, most notably the Reichskonkordat with Nazi Germany in 1933. He was widely regarded as a skilled diplomat and administrator, though critics later questioned his public silence regarding the Holocaust.

Papacy

Pius XII was elected on his 63rd birthday, 2 March 1939, on the third ballot of the conclave — one of the shortest conclaves of the modern era. His first encyclical, Summi Pontificatus (1939), condemned racism and totalitarianism in general terms. During the war he maintained Vatican neutrality while the Holy See engaged in extensive relief work and, according to many accounts, quietly assisted Jews and other persecuted people, including sheltering refugees in Roman religious institutions.

After the war, Pius XII became a prominent anti-communist voice. His 1949 decree of the Holy Office excommunicated Catholics who professed communist doctrine. In 1950 he issued the encyclical Humani Generis, addressing theological questions including evolution, and proclaimed the dogma of the Assumption of Mary in the apostolic constitution Munificentissimus Deus — the only exercise of papal infallibility since its definition in 1870.

Controversy and legacy

The debate over Pius XII's wartime record intensified after Rolf Hochhuth's 1963 play The Deputy accused him of indifference to the Holocaust. Defenders point to his behind-the-scenes diplomacy, the Church's rescue efforts, and praise he received from Jewish leaders of his era; critics argue that a public condemnation might have saved lives. The Vatican opened the archives of his pontificate to scholars in 2020. His cause for canonization was advanced by Benedict XVI, who declared him Venerable in 2009.
