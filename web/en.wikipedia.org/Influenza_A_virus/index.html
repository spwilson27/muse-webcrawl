Influenza A virus

Influenza A virus is a pathogen that causes influenza (flu) in birds and some mammals, including humans. It is a negative-sense, single-stranded RNA virus of the family Orthomyxoviridae, with a genome of eight segments. Wild aquatic birds are its natural reservoir. Influenza A is the only influenza type known to cause pandemics in humans.

Subtypes

Subtypes are classified by the variants of two surface proteins: haemagglutinin (H, 18 known types) and neuraminidase (N, 11 known types) — hence names such as H1N1 and H3N2, the subtypes currently circulating seasonally in humans, and H5N1 and H7N9, avian subtypes that occasionally infect people with high severity.

Antigenic change

The virus evolves rapidly by antigenic drift, the gradual accumulation of mutations that necessitates annual reformulation of flu vaccines. Its segmented genome also permits antigenic shift: when two viruses co-infect one cell, segments can reassort into a novel subtype to which humans have little immunity. Shifts caused the pandemics of 1918 (H1N1 "Spanish flu"), 1957 (H2N2), 1968 (H3N2) and 2009 (H1N1pdm09).

Disease and control

Seasonal influenza causes fever, cough, sore throat, myalgia and fatigue, and kills an estimated 290,000–650,000 people globally each year from respiratory causes. Antivirals such as oseltamivir (a neuraminidase inhibitor) can shorten illness if given early. Vaccination remains the main preventive measure, and global surveillance coordinated by the WHO monitors circulating strains, including avian viruses with pandemic potential.
