Sleep medicine

Sleep medicine is a medical specialty or subspecialty devoted to the diagnosis and therapy of sleep disorders, disturbances of sleep and sleep-related conditions. Its practitioners come from internal medicine, neurology, psychiatry, pediatrics, otolaryngology and family medicine, and it emerged as a distinct field in the second half of the twentieth century following the discovery of REM sleep and the development of polysomnography.

History

Scientific sleep research began in earnest with Nathaniel Kleitman's laboratory at the University of Chicago, and the 1953 discovery of rapid eye movement sleep by Eugene Aserinsky and Kleitman. William Dement founded one of the first sleep clinics at Stanford in the 1970s, initially focused on narcolepsy, and the recognition of obstructive sleep apnea as a common and treatable disorder — together with the introduction of CPAP therapy by Colin Sullivan in 1981 — drove rapid professionalization. Specialty societies, dedicated journals and formal certification examinations followed; in the United States, sleep medicine became an accredited subspecialty recognized across several parent boards.

Diagnostic methods

Core tools include the clinical sleep history and validated questionnaires such as the Epworth Sleepiness Scale; sleep diaries and wrist actigraphy, which estimate sleep-wake patterns over days to weeks; and polysomnography, the overnight laboratory recording of brain activity, eye movements, muscle tone, airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation and leg movements. Home sleep apnea testing provides a limited-channel alternative for suspected apnea, while the Multiple Sleep Latency Test objectively measures daytime sleepiness in suspected central hypersomnolence.

Scope of practice

Sleep physicians manage insomnia, sleep-related breathing disorders, hypersomnolence, circadian rhythm disorders, parasomnias and sleep-related movement disorders, frequently in collaboration with dentists (for oral appliances), surgeons, psychologists delivering behavioral sleep medicine, and respiratory therapists. The field maintains a standard nosology, the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, now in its third edition (with a text revision), published by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine.
