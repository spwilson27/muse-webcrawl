Glucose–alanine cycle

The glucose–alanine cycle, also known as the Cahill cycle after George Cahill, is a series of metabolic reactions by which amino groups and carbons from muscle are transported to the liver. It is closely analogous to the Cori cycle, which recycles lactate; the glucose–alanine cycle instead recycles alanine.

Mechanism

In skeletal muscle, amino acids derived from protein breakdown transfer their amino groups to pyruvate, forming the amino acid alanine. Alanine is released into the bloodstream and carried to the liver. There, the amino group is removed and ultimately excreted as urea, while the remaining carbon skeleton — pyruvate — is converted back into glucose by gluconeogenesis. That glucose returns via the blood to the muscle, where it can be used for energy or stored.

Significance

The cycle serves two purposes: it provides muscle with a continuing supply of glucose during fasting or prolonged exercise, and it safely transports nitrogen to the liver in the non-toxic form of alanine, since free ammonia is toxic. The glucose–alanine cycle becomes especially important during starvation, when muscle protein is broken down to supply gluconeogenic substrates.
