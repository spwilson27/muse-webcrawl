Inverse-square law

In science, an inverse-square law is any scientific law stating that the observed "intensity" of a specified physical quantity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the source of that physical quantity. The fundamental cause for this can be understood as geometric dilution corresponding to point-source radiation into three-dimensional space.

Formulation

Mathematically, intensity is proportional to 1/r², where r is distance. The law follows from geometry: a point source radiating equally in all directions covers the surface of a sphere of area 4πr², so the energy per unit area falls as the sphere grows. Doubling the distance therefore reduces the intensity to one quarter.

Occurrences

The best-known examples are Newton's law of universal gravitation, in which the gravitational force between two masses falls with the square of their separation, and Coulomb's law for the electrostatic force between charges. Light intensity, sound intensity in a free field, and radiation exposure from a point source follow the same pattern, as does the field strength of electric and magnetic dipole radiation in the far field. The inverse-square behaviour of gravity can be interpreted in general relativity as resulting from the geometry of spacetime, and Gauss's law shows that it is tied to the source's total flux being conserved.

History

Johannes Kepler inferred aspects of the law for light propagation; Ismaël Boulliau proposed in 1645 that gravity weakens as the inverse square of distance, an idea Newton placed on a firm mathematical basis in the Principia (1687), proving that elliptical planetary orbits follow from such a force. John Michell and Charles-Augustin de Coulomb established the corresponding law for electric and magnetic forces in the 18th century.

Applications

The law guides the design of lighting, radiography dose calculations, antenna and acoustics engineering, and safe distances from radiation sources. Deviations from a pure 1/r² dependence can signal absorption, scattering, extended sources, or, in fundamental physics, new phenomena such as a massive photon or extra spatial dimensions.
