Ceres (mythology)

In ancient Roman religion, Ceres (Latin: Cerēs) was a goddess of agriculture, grain crops, fertility and motherly relationships. She was originally the central deity in Rome's so-called plebeian or Aventine Triad, then was paired with her daughter Proserpina in what Romans described as "the Greek rites of Ceres". Her seven-day April festival Cerealia included the popular Ludi Ceriales (Ceres' games). She was also honoured in the May lustration of fields (Ambarvalia), at harvest-time, and during Roman marriages and funeral rites. Her Greek counterpart is Demeter.

Origins and cult

Ceres' name derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *ker-, "to grow" — the same root as Latin crescere and creare. She was among the oldest Roman deities, listed by the antiquarian Varro among the di indigetes, and was served in archaic cult by a flamen, the flamen Cerealis. In 493 BC her temple on the Aventine Hill — overlooking the Circus Maximus — was dedicated as the centre of the Aventine Triad with Liber and Libera, a plebeian counterweight to the patrician Capitoline Triad; the temple became headquarters of the plebeian aediles and a repository of senatorial decrees and popular laws. The grain supply of Rome (cura annonae) and the distributions of grain to the poor were closely associated with her cult.

Myth: Ceres and Proserpina

The central myth, retold by Ovid in the Metamorphoses and Fasti following Greek Demeter tradition, tells how Pluto abducted Ceres' daughter Proserpina while she gathered flowers in Sicily. The grief-stricken Ceres searched the earth with torches, neglecting the crops, until a compromise decreed that Proserpina spend part of the year in the underworld and part above — explaining the seasons. Roman versions localised the rape at Lake Pergusa near Enna in Sicily, a major centre of her worship.

Rites and festivals

The Cerealia in mid-April opened with horse races in the Circus Maximus; matrons in white carried torches recalling Ceres' search. Sows were her characteristic sacrifice. In the sacrum anniversarium Cereris, Roman matrons observed ritual abstinence. At the Ambarvalia, farmers purified their fields in procession. Ceres also guarded thresholds of life: brides brought her offerings, and the dead were presented to her, grain symbolising both sustenance and the seed buried to rise again.

Legacy

The word "cereal" derives from her name. She gives her name to the dwarf planet Ceres and the element cerium. Her image — a matron with wheat ears — long symbolised agriculture on coins, seals and state emblems.
