Lytic cycle

The lytic cycle is one of the two cycles of viral reproduction (the other being the lysogenic cycle). It results in the destruction (lysis) of the infected cell and its membrane. Viruses that reproduce only through the lytic cycle are known as virulent viruses, in contrast to temperate viruses, which can also enter lysogeny.

Stages

The cycle proceeds in stages: attachment, in which the virion binds to specific receptors on the host cell surface; penetration (entry), in which the viral genome enters the cell; biosynthesis, in which the host's machinery is redirected to replicate viral nucleic acid and synthesise viral proteins; maturation (assembly), in which new virions are assembled; and release, in which the cell bursts — often through the action of enzymes such as holin and endolysin in bacteriophages — liberating progeny viruses to infect new cells.

Bacteriophages

The lytic cycle is classically described for bacteriophages such as the T-even phages (e.g. T4) that infect Escherichia coli. A single infected bacterium may release on the order of one hundred new phage particles (the burst size) after a latent period of roughly twenty minutes.

Contrast with lysogeny

In the lysogenic cycle, the viral genome is incorporated into the host's chromosome as a prophage and replicated passively with the host, without killing it. Environmental stress can trigger induction, switching the prophage into the lytic cycle. Virulent phages lack this option and always lyse their hosts.
