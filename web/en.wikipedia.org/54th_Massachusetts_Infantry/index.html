The 54th Massachusetts Infantry Regiment was an infantry regiment that saw extensive service in the Union Army during the American Civil War. It was one of the first military units in the United States to be composed of African American enlisted men, with white officers, and it became famous for its gallant assault on Fort Wagner in 1863.

Formation

The regiment was authorized by Massachusetts Governor John A. Andrew in January 1863 after the Emancipation Proclamation allowed Black enlistment. Command was given to the 25-year-old Colonel Robert Gould Shaw, from a prominent abolitionist family. Recruits came from across the northern states and Canada; among them were two sons of Frederick Douglass. The men initially refused their pay for eighteen months in protest at being offered lower wages than white soldiers, until Congress equalized pay in 1864.

Fort Wagner

On 18 July 1863 the 54th led the assault on Battery Wagner on Morris Island, South Carolina, advancing across open sand under heavy fire. The regiment suffered roughly 270 casualties, including Colonel Shaw, who was killed and buried with his men in a mass grave by the Confederates. The regiment's bravery, widely reported, spurred further Black enlistment — about 180,000 African Americans eventually served in the Union forces.

Legacy

The regiment is commemorated by the Saint-Gaudens Memorial on Boston Common and was the subject of the 1989 film Glory. Sergeant William Harvey Carney, who rescued the national colours at Fort Wagner, later became the first African American to be awarded the Medal of Honor for his actions, though the award itself came in 1900.
