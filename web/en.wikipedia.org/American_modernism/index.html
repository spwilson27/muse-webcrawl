American modernism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

American modernism, much like the modernism movement in general, is a trend of philosophical thought arising from the widespread changes in culture and society in the age of modernity. American modernism is an artistic and cultural movement in the United States beginning at the turn of the 20th century, with a core period between World War I and World War II. Like its European counterpart, American modernism stemmed from a rejection of Enlightenment thinking, seeking to better capture reality in a new, industrialised world.

Characteristics

American modernists embraced experimentation, individualism, and a self-conscious break with tradition, while wrestling with distinctly American themes: industrialisation, urbanisation, immigration, race, and the tension between democratic ideals and modern alienation. Many leading figures were expatriates or worked between the United States and Europe.

Literature

In literature, modernism produced the poetry of Ezra Pound, T. S. Eliot (American-born though later British), Wallace Stevens, William Carlos Williams, H.D., and Marianne Moore; the fiction of Ernest Hemingway, F. Scott Fitzgerald, William Faulkner, Gertrude Stein, and John Dos Passos; and the drama of Eugene O'Neill. The Harlem Renaissance — with Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, and Jean Toomer — formed a central, distinctively African-American modernist stream.

Visual arts and architecture

Early American modernist painting was catalysed by the 1913 Armory Show, which introduced European avant-garde art to American audiences. Artists such as Marsden Hartley, Georgia O'Keeffe, Arthur Dove, Stuart Davis, and Charles Sheeler (with the Precisionists) developed American idioms, while photographers including Alfred Stieglitz and Paul Strand reshaped their medium. In architecture, Frank Lloyd Wright's organic architecture and, later, the International Style of the Bauhaus émigrés transformed the American built environment.

Music

Composers such as Charles Ives, Aaron Copland, George Gershwin, and Ruth Crawford Seeger forged modernist idioms from American folk, hymn, and jazz sources; jazz itself, and later bebop, became emblematic modernist forms.
