Concert pitch

Concert pitch is the pitch reference to which a group of musical instruments are tuned for a performance. It may vary between ensembles and has varied widely over musical history. The most common modern tuning standard uses 440 Hz for the note A above middle C (A4), a convention established as an international standard in 1939 and formalised by the International Organization for Standardization in 1955 and 1975 (ISO 16).

History

In earlier centuries there was no single standard: pitch varied from city to city and even between churches and theatres in the same city, sometimes by as much as a semitone or more. The invention of the tuning fork by John Shore in 1711 gave musicians a portable reference. In 1859 France legally adopted a standard of 435 Hz for A, the diapason normal, which spread through much of Europe before the higher 440 Hz standard prevailed in the twentieth century.

Variations in practice

Some orchestras, particularly in continental Europe, tune slightly sharp of 440 Hz — to 442 or 443 Hz — in the belief that it produces a brighter sound. Period-instrument ensembles performing Baroque music often tune to A = 415 Hz, roughly a semitone below modern pitch, while music of the Classical era is frequently played at A = 430 Hz. Wind instruments are built to a particular pitch, so changing the standard imposes real costs on players.

Transposing instruments

Because not all instruments read music at sounding pitch, sheet music for transposing instruments such as the clarinet, horn and trumpet is written at an interval from concert pitch. A conductor's score shows all parts at concert pitch so that the actual sounding notes can be compared.
