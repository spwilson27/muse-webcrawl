Mortgage-backed security

Mortgage-backed security (MBS)
Type: asset-backed security
Underlying assets: residential or commercial mortgages
Main issuers (US): Ginnie Mae, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac; private-label issuers
First agency pass-through: 1970 (Ginnie Mae)

A mortgage-backed security (MBS) is an asset-backed security whose cash flows are backed by the principal and interest payments on a pool of mortgage loans. Banks and other originators bundle mortgages and sell them to be securitized; investors receive payments as homeowners service their loans. Securitization frees lenders' capital to make new loans but separates origination from credit risk.


Pooling mortgages into tradable securities

Types

In the United States, agency MBS are issued or guaranteed by Ginnie Mae (a government agency) or the government-sponsored enterprises Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, carrying explicit or implicit backing against default. Private-label (non-agency) MBS lack such guarantees and were concentrated in riskier loans. Simple "pass-through" securities pay investors their pro-rata share of pool cash flows, while collateralized mortgage obligations (CMOs) slice the pool into tranches with different maturities and risk levels. CMBS are backed by commercial property loans. Key risks for investors include credit risk and prepayment risk—homeowners refinancing when rates fall.

Role in the 2008 financial crisis

During the 2000s US housing bubble, private-label MBS increasingly packaged subprime and Alt-A mortgages whose quality deteriorated. Complex structures such as CDOs built on MBS received inflated credit ratings, and when housing prices fell, defaults cascaded through the global financial system, contributing to the 2007–2008 financial crisis, the bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers, and the conservatorship of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac in September 2008. The Federal Reserve later bought large quantities of agency MBS in quantitative easing programs to support the housing market.

Market significance

The US MBS market is one of the largest bond markets in the world, with roughly $13 trillion outstanding, second only to US Treasuries; it underpins the American 30-year fixed-rate, freely prepayable mortgage, a product rare elsewhere.
