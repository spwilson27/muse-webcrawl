Human rights in China

Human rights in China
Governing frameworkConstitution of the PRC (1982); CCP leadership
Key issuesPolitical dissent, censorship, Xinjiang, Tibet, Hong Kong, death penalty

Human rights in China are periodically reviewed and reported on by international human rights organisations and foreign governments, whose assessments differ sharply from the position of the Chinese government. The government of the People's Republic of China maintains that it upholds human rights suited to China's national conditions, emphasising subsistence, development, and poverty alleviation as primary rights, while critics document extensive restrictions on freedom of expression, association, religion, and political participation.

Legal framework

The 1982 Constitution nominally guarantees freedoms of speech, press, assembly, and religion, but these are qualified by duties to the state and are subordinated in practice to the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party, written into the constitution itself. China signed the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights in 1998 but has never ratified it; it has ratified the ICESCR, with reservations. The judiciary is not independent of the Party.

Principal areas of concern

Political freedoms and dissent. Independent political parties are banned, and activists, lawyers, and journalists face prosecution under charges such as "subverting state power". The 1989 Tiananmen Square protests were suppressed by military force; discussion of them is censored. Prominent cases include the Nobel Peace Prize laureate Liu Xiaobo, who died in custody in 2017, and the 2015 "709 crackdown" on rights lawyers.

Censorship and surveillance. China operates the extensive internet censorship system known as the Great Firewall, requires real-name registration online, and has built large-scale surveillance systems. Officials frame these as necessary for stability and security.

Xinjiang. Since about 2017, credible reports by UN bodies, journalists, and researchers have described the mass detention of Uyghurs and other Turkic Muslims in internment camps in Xinjiang, together with coercive labour programmes, intrusive surveillance, and restrictions on religion and language. The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights concluded in 2022 that the abuses "may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity". China's government states the facilities were vocational training centres used to counter extremism and that they have closed.

Tibet, Hong Kong, and other issues. Tibetan areas face tight controls on religion and movement and periodic self-immolation protests. In Hong Kong, the National Security Law imposed in 2020 after the 2019 protests led to the closure of independent media and the prosecution of pro-democracy figures. China also executes more people than any other country (figures are a state secret), and concerns persist over forced confessions, residential surveillance at designated locations, and the treatment of religious groups including Falun Gong practitioners and unregistered churches.

Government position

The Chinese government issues its own human rights white papers emphasising historic reductions in poverty, rising life expectancy, and infrastructure development, and it accuses Western critics of applying double standards and interfering in internal affairs. It participates in the UN Human Rights Council's Universal Periodic Review and argues that human rights concepts must respect national sovereignty and cultural differences.
