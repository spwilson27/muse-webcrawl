The Anfal campaign (Arabic: حملة الأنفال), also known as the Kurdish genocide, was a genocidal counter-insurgency operation conducted by the Iraqi government of Saddam Hussein against the Kurdish population of northern Iraq during the closing stages of the Iran–Iraq War. Led by Ali Hassan al-Majid — who earned the nickname "Chemical Ali" — the campaign killed between 50,000 and 182,000 Kurds and destroyed thousands of villages.

The campaign

Named after al-Anfal, the eighth sura of the Qur'an ("The Spoils of War"), the operation combined ground offensives, mass deportations, the destruction of some 4,000 villages, firing-squad executions of captured men and boys, and the widespread use of chemical weapons. Its most notorious episode was the poison-gas attack on the city of Halabja on 16 March 1988, in which around 5,000 civilians died. Survivors were driven into concentration camps and "collective settlements", while vast areas of the Kurdish countryside were declared "prohibited zones" and Arabised.

Aftermath and recognition

Human Rights Watch documented the campaign as genocide in its 1993 report Genocide in Iraq. After the fall of Saddam Hussein in 2003, an Iraqi tribunal tried al-Majid and others for the Anfal; he was convicted of genocide and executed in 2010. Several governments, including those of the United Kingdom, Norway and Sweden, and the Iraqi parliament, have formally recognised the Anfal as genocide. Mass graves continue to be identified and victims repatriated for burial in Kurdistan.
