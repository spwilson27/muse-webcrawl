Aftermath of World War I


Europe after the post-war treaties

The aftermath of World War I saw drastic political, cultural, economic and social change across Europe, Asia, Africa and the Americas. Four empires collapsed — the German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman and Russian — and their defeat and territorial losses reshaped the map, while new nations were created or restored and old states altered.

Political consequences

The Treaty of Versailles (1919) imposed reparations and territorial losses on Germany, fuelling resentment that later contributed to the rise of Nazism. The Paris Peace Conference also produced the League of Nations, an intergovernmental organisation intended to prevent future wars, though it lacked the United States as a member and proved unable to stop the aggression of the 1930s. The Russian Revolution and civil war brought the Bolsheviks to power, leading to the creation of the Soviet Union in 1922. The Ottoman Empire was partitioned, and Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's resistance produced the Republic of Turkey in 1923.

Social and economic consequences

The war killed an estimated 9–10 million soldiers and millions of civilians, and was followed by the Spanish flu pandemic of 1918–20. Returning veterans, mass unemployment, inflation (including German hyperinflation in 1923) and political violence destabilised many states. Women's wartime labour contributed to expanded suffrage in several countries, including Britain, Germany and the United States.
