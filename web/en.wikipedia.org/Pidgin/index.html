Pidgin

A pidgin is a grammatically simplified means of communication that develops between two or more groups of people who do not have a language in common: typically, a mixture of elements from two or more languages, used for purposes such as trade, work or administration. Unlike a creole, a pidgin has no native speakers; it is learned as a second language.

Characteristics

Pidgins generally have a small vocabulary, drawn mainly from one language (the lexifier), simplified grammar with little inflection, and a restricted range of uses. Examples include Chinese Pidgin English, once used in trade between the British and Chinese, and the many English-based pidgins of West Africa and the Pacific.

From pidgin to creole

When a pidgin becomes the native language of a speech community — as when children grow up speaking it — it expands in vocabulary and grammar and becomes a creole, a process called creolisation. Tok Pisin of Papua New Guinea, which began as a pidgin and is now a creole and official language, illustrates this development.
