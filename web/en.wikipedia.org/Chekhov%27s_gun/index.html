Chekhov's gun

Chekhov's gun
TypeDramatic principle / narrative technique
OriginatorAnton Chekhov
Core ideaEvery element in a story must be necessary; irrelevant elements should be removed

Chekhov's gun (Russian: Чеховское ружьё) is a narrative principle that states that every element in a story must be necessary, and irrelevant elements should be removed. It is commonly stated as: if a gun is shown in the first act, it must be fired by the third.

Origin

The principle comes from the letters and notebooks of Russian playwright Anton Chekhov, who wrote variations of the idea several times between 1889 and 1904, insisting that a rifle hung on the wall in the first act must go off in the second or third — otherwise it should not be placed there.

Use and variations

The principle guides economy in storytelling: details introduced early create expectations the story should satisfy. Its inverse, the red herring, deliberately misleads. Writers such as Ernest Hemingway discussed and debated the rule, and it remains a standard concept in writing instruction.
