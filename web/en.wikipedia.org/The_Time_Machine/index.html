The Time Machine is a science fiction novella by English author H. G. Wells, published in 1895. It is generally credited with popularizing the concept of time travel by using a vehicle or device that permits an operator to travel purposely through time, and the term "time machine" coined by Wells is now almost universally used. The work is an early example of the dying earth subgenre and a seminal text of science fiction.

Plot

A Victorian scientist known only as the Time Traveller recounts to his dinner guests his journey to the year A.D. 802,701 aboard a machine of his own invention. He finds humanity divided into two species: the gentle, childlike Eloi, who live above ground in idle ease, and the brutish, subterranean Morlocks, who operate vast machinery underground and emerge at night to prey upon the Eloi. Befriended by an Eloi woman named Weena, the Traveller recovers his stolen machine from the Morlocks and escapes, travelling further into the far future, where he witnesses the dying Earth beneath a swollen red sun and crab-like creatures on a desolate beach. Returning home, he tells his story and departs again on a second journey, never to return.

Themes

The novella reflects Wells's socialist views and his anxieties about Victorian class division: the Eloi descend from the leisured upper class (the "Haves") and the Morlocks from the labouring "Have-Nots," pushed underground. It also engages with evolutionary theory and entropy, projecting the degeneration of humanity and the eventual cooling of the Earth.

Influence and adaptations

The Time Machine has been adapted many times, notably George Pal's 1960 film and the 2002 film directed by Simon Wells, the author's great-grandson. Its concepts — time travel by machine, the far-future division of humanity, and the entropic end of the Earth — have shaped science fiction ever since. The novella has never been out of print.
