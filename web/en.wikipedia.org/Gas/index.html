Gas

Image placeholder: particles in the gaseous state are widely separated

Gas is one of the four fundamental states of matter. The others are solid, liquid, and plasma. A gas may be made up of atoms (e.g. a noble gas like neon), elemental molecules made from one type of atom (e.g. oxygen), or compound molecules made from a variety of atoms (e.g. carbon dioxide). A gas mixture, such as air, contains a variety of pure gases.

Properties

What distinguishes a gas from liquids and solids is the vast separation of the individual gas particles. This separation usually makes a colourless gas invisible to the human observer. The particles move freely and rapidly, colliding with each other and the walls of their container, and a gas expands to fill whatever volume is available to it. Gases are compressible, and their state is described by macroscopic properties including pressure, volume, temperature, and amount of substance.

Gas laws

The behaviour of an ideal gas is summarised by the ideal gas law, PV = nRT, which combines Boyle's law (pressure inversely proportional to volume at fixed temperature), Charles's law (volume proportional to temperature at fixed pressure), and Avogadro's law. Real gases deviate from ideality at high pressures and low temperatures, described more accurately by equations such as the van der Waals equation.

Kinetic theory

The kinetic theory of gases explains macroscopic properties in terms of the motion of particles: temperature corresponds to average kinetic energy, and pressure results from collisions with container walls. Kinetic theory underlies concepts such as diffusion, effusion (Graham's law), and the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of molecular speeds.

Elemental and common gases

Elemental gases at standard temperature and pressure include hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, and chlorine (diatomic molecules) and the noble gases (monatomic). Familiar compound gases include carbon dioxide, methane, ammonia, and water vapour. The word "gas" was coined in the seventeenth century by the Flemish chemist Jan Baptist van Helmont, probably influenced by the Greek chaos.
