Deductive reasoning is the process of reasoning from one or more statements (premises) to reach a logically certain conclusion. Deductive reasoning goes in the same direction as the conditionals and links premises with conclusions. If all premises are true, the terms are clear, and the rules of deductive logic are followed, then the conclusion reached is necessarily true.

Validity and soundness

A deductive argument is valid when it is impossible for its premises to be true while its conclusion is false; validity concerns the argument's form, not the actual truth of its statements. A valid argument with all true premises is sound, and its conclusion must be true. The classic example is a syllogism: "All men are mortal. Socrates is a man. Therefore, Socrates is mortal."

History

Aristotle systematized deductive logic in the Organon (4th century BCE), centering on the categorical syllogism. Stoic logicians developed propositional inference. In the 19th and 20th centuries, George Boole, Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, and Alfred North Whitehead recast deduction in mathematical form, producing modern predicate logic and proof theory. Kurt Gödel's completeness theorem showed that every valid formula of first-order logic is provable.

Comparison with other reasoning

Inductive reasoning generalizes from observations to probable conclusions that new evidence can overturn; deduction preserves truth but does not extend content beyond the premises. Abductive reasoning infers the most likely explanation. Deduction is the backbone of mathematics, formal logic, computer science (type systems, program verification), and legal interpretation, wherever conclusions must follow with certainty from given rules.

Common fallacies

Formally invalid patterns include affirming the consequent ("If A then B; B; therefore A") and denying the antecedent. Such errors remain tempting in everyday reasoning because they mimic valid forms like modus ponens and modus tollens.
