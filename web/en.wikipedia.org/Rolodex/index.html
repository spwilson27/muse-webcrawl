A Rolodex is a rotating file device used to store business contact information, most commonly in the form of index cards mounted on a rotating axis. The name, a portmanteau of "rolling" and "index", became a generic trademark for rotary card files and, by extension, a person's collection of business contacts, as in the phrase "a deep Rolodex".

History

The Rolodex was invented by Danish engineer Hildaur Nielsen, an employee of the New York stationery firm Zephyr American, and was marketed from the 1950s. Cards are notched at the bottom so they can be clipped onto the rotating spindle; users flip through the cards, which remain open at the selected position because the cards lie flat once rotated past the top.

Zephyr American was acquired by the Insilco Corporation in 1986, and the Rolodex brand later passed to Newell Rubbermaid (now Newell Brands). Electronic versions, including software contact managers marketed under the Rolodex name, appeared in the 1980s and 1990s, but the paper rotary file remained a common office fixture long after digital address books became standard.

Design

The classic desktop model consists of a cylindrical frame covered by hundreds of cards, each typically holding one contact's name, address and telephone number, together with alphabetical divider cards. Larger floor-standing and wall-mounted versions, and covered models with a lid, were also produced.

In popular culture

The Rolodex became a symbol of the well-connected professional. In business slang, to "have a good Rolodex" means to possess a valuable network of contacts, and journalists and talent agents were often described by the depth of theirs.
