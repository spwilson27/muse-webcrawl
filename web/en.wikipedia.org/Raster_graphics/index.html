Raster graphics

In computer graphics and digital photography, a raster graphic (also called a bitmap) is an image made of a rectangular grid of pixels — small coloured dots — each stored as numerical colour values. Raster formats are the natural output of cameras, scanners and frame buffers, and they excel at representing photographs and complex imagery with subtle gradations of colour.

Resolution and colour depth

Image detail is governed by resolution (the number of pixels, e.g. 1920 × 1080) and colour depth (bits per pixel). Unlike vector graphics, raster images cannot be enlarged indefinitely without quality loss: scaling up reveals individual pixels ("pixelation"), because no additional detail was captured. Typical depths are 8 bits per channel (16.7 million colours for RGB) or higher for professional work.

File formats

Uncompressed formats such as BMP and TIFF store pixels directly. Lossless compression is used by PNG and GIF (the latter limited to 256 colours per frame but supporting animation), while JPEG applies lossy compression exploiting human vision, making it the standard for photographs on the web. Newer formats include WebP, AVIF and HEIF.

Editing

Raster images are edited with raster graphics editors such as Adobe Photoshop and GIMP, which manipulate pixels with brushes, filters, layers and colour adjustments. Operations like resizing use interpolation algorithms (nearest-neighbour, bilinear, bicubic, Lanczos) to estimate new pixel values.
