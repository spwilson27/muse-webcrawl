The Dead Sea (Arabic: al-Baḥr al-Mayyit; Hebrew: Yam ha-Melah, "Sea of Salt") is a hypersaline lake bordered by Jordan to the east and Israel and the West Bank to the west. Its surface, around 430 metres below sea level, is the lowest land elevation on Earth. With a salinity of about 34%, it is one of the world's saltiest bodies of water — too salty to support fish or most other life, hence its name — and its dense water makes bathers float effortlessly.

Geography and decline

The Dead Sea lies in the Jordan Rift Valley and is fed mainly by the Jordan River; it has no outlet, losing water only by evaporation. Since the mid-20th century, diversion of the Jordan's waters for irrigation and industry, together with mineral extraction, has caused the level to fall by more than a metre per year. The southern basin is now maintained as artificial evaporation ponds for potash and bromine production, while the northern basin continues to shrink, leaving sinkholes and abandoned resorts along its shores.

History and tourism

The region has been inhabited since antiquity; the nearby Qumran Caves yielded the Dead Sea Scrolls, and the Essenes are often associated with the area. Today the Dead Sea is known for health tourism, mineral-rich mud and salt products, and chemical industries operated by Jordan and Israel.
