Storegga Slide

The Storegga Slide was one of the largest known submarine landslides, occurring on the continental slope off the coast of Norway in the Norwegian Sea around 8,200 years ago (about 6225–6170 BC). The collapse of roughly 3,000 cubic kilometres of sediment generated a major tsunami in the North Atlantic.

The event

The slide occurred at the edge of the continental shelf near the Storegga escarpment. Sediments deposited during the last glacial period, including weak marine clays, are believed to have failed — possibly triggered by an earthquake and the dissociation of gas hydrates — creating a scar some 290 kilometres long, with debris spreading over an area larger than Scotland.

Tsunami

The resulting tsunami left deposits around the northern North Sea and Norwegian Sea coasts. Run-up reached an estimated 20 metres or more in the Shetland Islands and several metres in Scotland and Norway. The wave struck Doggerland, the low-lying land then connecting Britain to continental Europe, which was already being inundated by rising post-glacial seas; the Storegga tsunami may have devastated its remaining Mesolithic coastal communities and hastened Britain's final separation from the mainland.

Modern relevance

The slide area now lies above the Ormen Lange gas field. Studies commissioned before its development assessed the risk of future slides and concluded that the conditions that produced the Storegga event — rapid glacial sediment loading — no longer exist, so a recurrence on this scale is considered unlikely.
