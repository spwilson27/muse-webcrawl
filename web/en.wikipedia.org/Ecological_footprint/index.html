Ecological footprint

[Image: images/Ecological_footprint.png — Ecological footprint per person by country]

The ecological footprint is a measure of human demand on natural capital — the quantity of nature it takes to support a particular population or economy, expressed in global hectares of biologically productive land and water area. It tracks how much land is required to produce the resources consumed and to absorb the wastes generated, and compares that demand with the planet's ecological capacity to regenerate those resources (biocapacity).

Methodology

The method was developed in the early 1990s by Mathis Wackernagel and William Rees at the University of British Columbia. Footprint accounting, now maintained by the Global Footprint Network, sums six land-use categories: cropland, grazing land, fishing grounds, forest land, built-up land and carbon-uptake land (the forest area needed to sequester anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions not absorbed by the oceans). Results are expressed in global hectares (gha), hectares with world-average biological productivity.

Findings

According to the Global Footprint Network, humanity's total footprint exceeded Earth's biocapacity in the early 1970s, and by the 2020s demand stood at roughly 1.7 Earths. The date each year on which demand exhausts the year's regenerative supply is marked as Earth Overshoot Day. Per-capita footprints vary widely by country, generally rising with income; carbon emissions typically form the largest single component.

Criticism

Critics argue the footprint aggregates dissimilar land types, understates the role of technology, treats nuclear power inconsistently, and compresses many sustainability dimensions into a single land metric. Supporters respond that it is a conservative, transparent accounting framework intended for communication as much as analysis, complemented by measures such as the carbon footprint and water footprint.
