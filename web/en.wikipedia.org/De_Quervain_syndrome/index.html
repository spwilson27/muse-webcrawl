De Quervain syndrome

De Quervain syndrome (also de Quervain's tenosynovitis) is a painful condition affecting the tendons on the thumb side of the wrist — the abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis — where they pass through a fibrous tunnel (the first dorsal compartment) at the radial styloid. It was described by the Swiss surgeon Fritz de Quervain in 1895.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms are pain over the radial styloid, often radiating up the forearm, worsened by gripping, pinching, wringing or ulnar deviation of the wrist; swelling and a squeaking or catching sensation may be present. The Finkelstein test — tucking the thumb into the fist and bending the wrist toward the little finger — reproduces the pain and is the classic clinical manoeuvre, though it is not specific.

Causes

Current evidence suggests the condition is largely a degenerative thickening (myxoid change) of the tendon sheath rather than acute inflammation, aggravated by repetitive or forceful thumb and wrist movements. It is common in new mothers who repeatedly lift babies ("mother's thumb"), in pregnancy, and in workers and gamers with repetitive hand use. Anatomical variations, such as a separate compartment for the extensor pollicis brevis, are common and may predispose.

Treatment

First-line management is activity modification, a thumb-spica splint, and NSAIDs; many cases settle within weeks. Corticosteroid injection into the compartment is highly effective, resolving symptoms in a majority of patients, sometimes after a second injection. Persistent cases are treated surgically by releasing the first dorsal compartment, with care to protect the superficial branch of the radial nerve.
