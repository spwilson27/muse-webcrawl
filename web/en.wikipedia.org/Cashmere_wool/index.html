Cashmere wool, usually simply known as cashmere, is a luxury fibre obtained from cashmere goats and other breeds of goat raised in cold climates. It is the soft downy winter undercoat of the goat, distinct from the coarser outer guard hair. Cashmere is prized for its exceptional softness, lightness and warmth — often described as several times warmer than sheep's wool of the same weight — and the finest grade, pashmina from the Changthangi goats of Ladakh, is used for the famous Kashmir shawls.

Properties and standards

Cashmere fibres are very fine — under rules such as the US Wool Products Labeling Act, wool may be labelled cashmere only if the average fibre diameter does not exceed 19 micrometres and coarse fibres are limited — and have a high crimp and low scale profile, making fabrics unusually soft but also prone to pilling and abrasion. The down grows in winter and is shed naturally in spring; it is combed out by hand in the traditional method or shorn and mechanically dehaired. Each goat produces only about 150 grams of fibre a year, so supply is inherently limited.

History

Shawls woven from the fibre in Kashmir were famed in Asia for centuries and became a sensation in Europe from the late 18th century, when imported "cashmere shawls" were worn by fashionable women including Empress Joséphine. European mills in Scotland (notably in the Borders) and Italy built industries imitating and then processing genuine cashmere, and the fibre became a staple of luxury knitwear in the 20th century.

Production and issues

China and Mongolia dominate world raw-cashmere supply. The 21st-century mass-market expansion of inexpensive cashmere has raised concerns about adulteration and mislabelling, grassland degradation from exploding goat herds, and animal welfare, prompting certification and traceability schemes and testing regimes to detect blends with sheep's wool, yak hair or synthetics.
