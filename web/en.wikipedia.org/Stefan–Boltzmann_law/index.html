Stefan–Boltzmann law

The Stefan–Boltzmann law, also known as Stefan's law, describes the intensity of the thermal radiation emitted by matter in terms of its temperature. It states that the total energy radiated per unit surface area of a black body across all wavelengths per unit time (the radiant exitance) is directly proportional to the fourth power of the black body's thermodynamic temperature T:

j* = σT4

The constant of proportionality σ, called the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, has the value 5.670374419 × 10−8 W·m−2·K−4.

History

The law was deduced by Jožef Stefan in 1879 on the basis of experimental measurements made by John Tyndall, and derived theoretically by Stefan's student Ludwig Boltzmann in 1884 using thermodynamics and Maxwell's theory of radiation pressure. It was the first successful theoretical prediction of thermal radiation behaviour and a key step toward Planck's law.

Applications

The law is used in astronomy to determine the luminosity of stars from their radius and effective temperature (L = 4πR²σT4), to estimate the surface temperature of the Sun, and to calculate the equilibrium temperature of planets. Real materials are not perfect black bodies; their emission is described by multiplying σT4 by an emissivity factor between 0 and 1.

Derivation from Planck's law

The Stefan–Boltzmann law follows by integrating Planck's law of blackbody radiation over all frequencies; the Stefan–Boltzmann constant can then be expressed in terms of the Boltzmann constant, the Planck constant, and the speed of light.
