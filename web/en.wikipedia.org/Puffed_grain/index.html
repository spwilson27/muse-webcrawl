Puffed grain

Puffed grains are grains that have been expanded ("puffed") by processing. They are a component of many modern breakfast cereals as well as a traditional food in many parts of the world. Puffed grains can be made by heating whole grains under pressure and then suddenly releasing the pressure, causing the superheated steam inside the kernel to expand it dramatically — or by extrusion.

History

People have long made popped and parched grains; in India puffed rice (muri) is traditionally made by heating rice in hot sand. The modern industrial process was devised by American inventor Alexander P. Anderson, who discovered "gun puffing" in 1901 — heating grains in sealed tubes until internal pressure built, then releasing them — and demonstrated puffed wheat and rice at the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair, where they were marketed as "food shot from guns."

Production

In gun puffing, grains are heated in a pressure vessel (the "puffing gun") to around 200 °C and high pressure; when the vessel is opened, the sudden pressure drop flashes internal moisture to steam, expanding the kernel to several times its original volume. Extrusion puffing forces dough through a die with similar results and is widely used for snack foods.

Uses

Puffed rice, wheat, and corn underpin many breakfast cereals, cereal bars, and snacks; pressed puffed grain cakes (rice cakes) are a common low-calorie food. Popped sorghum and puffed millet are traditional in parts of Africa and Asia.
