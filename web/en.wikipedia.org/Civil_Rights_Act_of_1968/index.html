Civil Rights Act of 1968

EnactedApril 11, 1968
Signed byPresident Lyndon B. Johnson
Public law90-284
Key titlesFair Housing Act (Titles VIII–IX)

The Civil Rights Act of 1968 is a landmark United States federal law enacted during the civil rights movement. Signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson on April 11, 1968 — one week after the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr. — it is best known for its Titles VIII and IX, the Fair Housing Act, which prohibited discrimination in the sale, rental, and financing of housing.

Contents

Title I strengthened penalties for interfering with federally protected activities. Titles II through VII comprise the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, applying most of the Bill of Rights to tribal governments while recognising tribal sovereignty. Title X, the so-called "H. Rap Brown Act", made it a federal crime to cross state lines to incite a riot.

Fair Housing Act

The Act prohibited housing discrimination based on race, colour, religion, and national origin; sex was added in 1974, and disability and familial status in the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988. It followed earlier executive action, notably President Kennedy's 1962 order barring discrimination in federally assisted housing, and completed legislative goals left unmet by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Passage

Fair housing legislation had stalled in Congress for two years. King's assassination, and the unrest that followed in more than 100 cities, spurred the House to pass the Senate's version, which Johnson signed at a ceremony on April 11, 1968.
