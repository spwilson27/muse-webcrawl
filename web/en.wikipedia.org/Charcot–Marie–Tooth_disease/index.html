Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT) is a hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy of the peripheral nervous system characterised by progressive loss of muscle tissue and touch sensation across parts of the body. It is the most commonly inherited neurological disorder, affecting approximately one in 2,500 people. CMT is named after its discoverers, Jean-Martin Charcot and Pierre Marie in France and Howard Henry Tooth in England, who described it in 1886.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually begin in late childhood or early adulthood and progress slowly. Weakness starts in the feet and lower legs, producing foot drop, frequent ankle sprains, and a high-stepping gait. Muscle wasting of the lower legs can give an "inverted champagne bottle" appearance. Classic foot deformities include pes cavus (high arches) and hammer toes. Later, weakness and wasting may affect the hands, impairing fine motor skills. Sensory loss is usually milder than motor involvement. In most patients the disease does not affect life expectancy.

Genetics and classification

CMT is genetically heterogeneous, with mutations identified in more than 100 genes. It is classified by pattern of inheritance and by whether the primary pathology is demyelination or axonal loss:

  • CMT1 — autosomal dominant, demyelinating. The most common form, CMT1A, is caused by a duplication of the PMP22 gene on chromosome 17.
  • CMT2 — autosomal dominant, axonal; frequently caused by mutations in MFN2.
  • CMTX — X-linked forms, most often involving GJB1 (connexin 32).
  • CMT4 — autosomal recessive forms.

Markedly slowed nerve conduction velocities distinguish demyelinating forms on electrophysiological testing.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture and family history, supported by nerve conduction studies and electromyography. Genetic testing is increasingly the definitive route and is commercially available for the common causes. Nerve biopsy is now rarely needed.

Management

There is no cure and no approved disease-modifying therapy. Management is supportive: physical and occupational therapy, ankle–foot orthoses for foot drop, corrective orthopaedic surgery for severe deformity, and avoidance of neurotoxic drugs such as vincristine, which can cause severe worsening. High-dose ascorbic acid was trialled for CMT1A but showed no consistent benefit in large studies.
