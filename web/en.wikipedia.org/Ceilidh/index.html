Ceilidh

A cèilidh (Scottish Gaelic: [ˈkʰʲeːlɪ]) or céilí (Irish) is a traditional Scottish and Irish social gathering. In its most basic form, it simply means a social visit. In contemporary usage, it usually involves playing Gaelic folk music and dancing, either at a house party, or a larger concert at a social hall or other community gathering place.

History

Traditionally, a cèilidh was a gathering in a crofter's house where neighbours entertained one another with storytelling, poetry, songs, riddles and news, not necessarily dancing. With the suppression of Gaelic culture after the Jacobite risings and the disruptions of the Highland Clearances, the custom survived most strongly in rural communities and among the Scottish and Irish diaspora, where emigrant communities preserved it as a link to home.

Modern ceilidhs

The modern ceilidh centres on Scottish country dancing to a live band — typically fiddles, accordion, flute, drums and sometimes bagpipes — with a "caller" who explains each dance so that beginners can join in. Popular dances include the Gay Gordons, the Dashing White Sergeant, Strip the Willow and the Eightsome Reel. Ceilidhs are common at weddings, festivals, Highland games and Hogmanay (New Year) celebrations, and are popular social events in universities and across the Scottish and Irish diaspora.

Irish céilí

In Ireland, céilí dancing was promoted by the Gaelic League from the 1890s as part of the Gaelic revival, and the first céilí in London was held in 1897. Irish céilí dances are set dances performed in formations, regulated in the 20th century by An Coimisiún Le Rincí Gaelacha (the Irish Dancing Commission).
