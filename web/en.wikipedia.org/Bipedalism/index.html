Bipedalism is a form of terrestrial locomotion in which an organism moves by means of its two rear limbs. An animal that habitually walks on two legs is a biped. Humans are the only living obligate bipedal primates, and bipedal walking is one of the defining adaptations of the hominin lineage: it appeared millions of years before large brains or stone tools, reshaping the skeleton from the skull base to the foot.

Evolution in hominins

Fossils suggest early hominins combined climbing with upright walking: Sahelanthropus (~7–6 Ma) and Ardipithecus (4.4 Ma) show debated bipedal features, while Australopithecus was clearly bipedal on the ground. The 3.66-million-year-old Laetoli footprints in Tanzania, attributed to Australopithecus afarensis, preserve a human-like gait. Adaptations include the foramen magnum positioned under the skull, an S-curved spine, a short broad pelvis, angled femora (valgus knee), and an arched foot with a non-grasping big toe. Proposed advantages include energy-efficient walking over distance, freeing the hands for carrying and tool use, improved vigilance, and better heat dissipation in open habitats.

Bipedalism in other animals

Birds are bipedal descendants of theropod dinosaurs, which had adopted two-legged running over 200 million years ago. Kangaroos move bipedally by hopping; many rodents, bears and great apes stand or walk bipedally facultatively; some lizards, such as basilisks, run bipedally when fleeing. Flightless birds (ostriches, emus, penguins) show the range of bipedal specialisations, from cursorial running to upright waddling.

Biomechanics and costs

Human walking works like an inverted pendulum, exchanging kinetic and potential energy to save effort; running adds spring-like tendon recoil. Bipedalism also carries costs: back, knee and hip problems, difficult childbirth through a remodelled pelvis, and a slower sprint speed than most quadrupeds of similar size.
