Caldera

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A caldera (from Spanish caldera, meaning "cauldron") is a large cauldron-like hollow that forms shortly after the emptying of a magma chamber in a volcano. It is a type of volcanic crater, but much larger: calderas are typically more than 1 kilometre in diameter and can exceed 100 kilometres. When a volcano erupts a large volume of magma in a short time, the unsupported roof of the emptied magma chamber collapses, creating a depression at the surface.

Formation and types

Caldera-forming eruptions are among the largest volcanic events on Earth; the biggest eject more than 1,000 cubic kilometres of material and are rated VEI 8 ("supervolcanoes"), such as the eruptions that formed the Yellowstone Caldera (640,000 years ago) and Lake Toba (74,000 years ago). Geologists distinguish piston, trapdoor, piecemeal and Downsag caldera collapse styles. Many calderas fill with lakes (Crater Lake, Oregon, occupies the caldera of Mount Mazama) or host renewed volcanism from resurgent domes and younger cones within the depression.

Notable calderas

  • Santorini, Greece — formed c. 1600 BCE in the Minoan eruption.
  • Crater Lake, Oregon — formed c. 5700 BCE.
  • Yellowstone Caldera, Wyoming — 72 × 55 km, formed 640,000 years ago.
  • Lake Toba, Sumatra — the largest known Quaternary caldera lake.
  • La Garita Caldera, Colorado — source of one of the largest known explosive eruptions.
  • Camp Campi Flegrei (Phlegraean Fields), Italy — a restless caldera adjacent to Naples.

Calderas elsewhere

Calderas also occur on other planetary bodies — on Mars (e.g. the summit calderas of Olympus Mons), Venus, and Jupiter's moon Io.
