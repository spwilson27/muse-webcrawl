Implicit bias

Implicit bias
Field: Social psychology
Definition: Unconscious attitudes or stereotypes affecting judgement
Common measure: Implicit Association Test (IAT)

Implicit bias refers to attitudes or stereotypes that affect understanding, actions, and decisions in an unconscious manner. These biases, which include both favourable and unfavourable assessments, are activated involuntarily and without an individual's awareness or intentional control.

Measurement

The most widely used measure is the Implicit Association Test (IAT), introduced by Anthony Greenwald and colleagues in 1998. The IAT infers the strength of associations between concepts (such as racial groups) and evaluations (such as good or bad) from the speed with which people sort words and images. Millions of people have taken versions of the test online through Project Implicit.

Effects and debate

Research links implicit biases to behaviour in domains such as hiring, medical care, policing, and education, though the strength with which IAT scores predict individual discriminatory behaviour is debated. Meta-analyses have found small average predictive effects, and critics question the test's reliability and what exactly it measures. Interventions such as structured decision procedures, blind review, and perspective-taking are proposed to reduce the influence of bias.

Related ideas

Implicit bias is distinguished from explicit bias, which people consciously endorse and report. The concept sits within a wider study of implicit cognition, including implicit memory, attitudes, and self-esteem. See also Stereotype and In-group favoritism.
