Shell money

Shell money is a form of commodity money made from seashells, used historically in many parts of the world including Africa, Asia, the Pacific, and the Americas. Because shells are durable, portable, countable, and difficult to counterfeit, they served as media of exchange for thousands of years.

Forms

Cowrie shells (Monetaria moneta) were the most widespread shell money, circulating in China — where the shell inspired the radical for "value" in characters — India, and West Africa, where they remained in use into the 20th century. In North America, Indigenous peoples of the Northeast made wampum beads from quahog and whelk shells, used ceremonially and, in the colonial period, as currency. In the Pacific, elaborate forms included the stone-like shell valuables of Yap's region, New Guinea kina shells, and the string shell money of the Solomon Islands, some of which is still made and used for bride price and compensation payments.
