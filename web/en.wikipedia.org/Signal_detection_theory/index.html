Signal detection theory

Signal detection theory (often abbreviated SDT) is a means to measure the ability to differentiate between information-bearing patterns (called stimulus in living organisms, signal in machines) and random patterns that distract from the information (called noise, consisting of background stimuli and random activity of the detection machine and of the nervous system of the operator).

Basic concepts

In the basic yes/no detection task, an observer must decide on each trial whether a signal was present. Four outcomes are possible: a hit (signal present, observer says "yes"), a miss (signal present, observer says "no"), a false alarm (signal absent, observer says "yes"), and a correct rejection (signal absent, observer says "no"). The theory separates two independent aspects of performance: sensitivity, measured by d′ (d-prime), the distance between the signal and noise distributions; and response bias or criterion, the threshold the observer uses to say "yes".

Receiver operating characteristic

Plotting hit rate against false-alarm rate as the criterion varies produces the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, borrowed from radar engineering. The area under the ROC curve summarizes overall discriminability.

Applications

Signal detection theory is used in psychology and medicine (for example, evaluating diagnostic tests and radiology), in quality control, telecommunications, radar, and in research on memory and perception, where it helps separate genuine sensitivity from mere willingness to respond.
