Two-party system

TypePolitical system
Related conceptsMulti-party system, Dominant-party system
Key theoristMaurice Duverger

A two-party system is a political party system in which two major political parties consistently dominate the political landscape. At any point in time, one of the two parties typically holds a majority in the legislature and is usually referred to as the majority or governing party while the other is the minority or opposition party. Around the world, the term has different senses, but in most cases it describes a system in which all or nearly all elected officials belong to one of two major parties, and third parties rarely win any seats in the legislature.

Two-party systems are most commonly found in countries using first-past-the-post (plurality) electoral systems, a relationship formalized in Duverger's law, which holds that plurality voting tends to produce two-party systems while proportional representation tends to produce multi-party systems.

Examples

The United States is the most prominent modern example, where the Democratic Party and the Republican Party have dominated national politics since the mid-19th century. Other examples include Jamaica, Malta, and, in a looser sense, the United Kingdom, where the Conservative Party and the Labour Party are the two largest parties, though other parties regularly win seats in Parliament.

Causes

Political scientists attribute two-party systems to electoral rules, particularly single-member districts with plurality voting, which mechanically disadvantage smaller parties and psychologically discourage voters from "wasting" votes on candidates unlikely to win. Ballot access laws, campaign finance structures, and broad "big tent" party coalitions can reinforce the pattern.

Advantages and disadvantages

Supporters argue that two-party systems produce stable governments, clear accountability, and moderate, centrist policies. Critics argue that they restrict voter choice, entrench the status quo, encourage negative campaigning and polarization, and leave many viewpoints unrepresented. Some analysts also describe a "wasted vote" problem, in which votes for losing candidates have no effect on the outcome.
