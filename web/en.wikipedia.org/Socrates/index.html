Socrates (c. 470 – 399 BC) was a Greek philosopher from Athens credited as the founder of Western philosophy and among the first moral philosophers of the ethical tradition of thought. He wrote nothing himself; he is known through the accounts of his students, especially Plato and Xenophon, and the comic playwright Aristophanes — a source problem known as the Socratic problem.

Life

Socrates was the son of the stonemason Sophroniscus and the midwife Phaenarete. He served with distinction as a hoplite in the Peloponnesian War. In middle life he became a familiar figure in the Athenian agora, engaging fellow citizens in philosophical conversation and questioning reputed experts about virtue, justice, and piety. His association with figures later condemned as enemies of the democracy, such as Alcibiades and Critias, left him politically suspect after Athens' defeat by Sparta.

Trial and death

In 399 BC Socrates was tried and convicted on charges of impiety and corrupting the youth of Athens. As recounted in Plato's Apology, he refused to abandon his philosophical mission or to beg for mercy, and after conviction declined opportunities to escape. He was executed by drinking a cup of poison hemlock, surrounded by his friends — a scene narrated in the Phaedo.

Method and philosophy

The Socratic method proceeds by elenchus — cross-examination that exposes contradictions in an interlocutor's beliefs — typically ending in aporia, an admission of ignorance. Socrates professed that his wisdom consisted in knowing that he knew nothing. His ethical intellectualism held that virtue is knowledge and that no one does wrong willingly. His maxim that "the unexamined life is not worth living" remains a touchstone of philosophy.

Legacy

Through Plato, Socrates' student, and through schools founded by other followers — Cynics, Cyrenaics, Stoics — Socrates shaped the entire subsequent Western philosophical tradition. Philosophers before him are conventionally called the Pre-Socratics.
