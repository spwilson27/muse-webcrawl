U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton

The Supreme Court held that states may not impose term limits on members of Congress.
Citation514 U.S. 779 (1995)
DecidedMay 22, 1995
MajorityStevens, joined by Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer
DissentThomas, joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia

U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779 (1995), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States holding that states may not impose qualifications for prospective members of the United States Congress — specifically term limits — stricter than those specified in the United States Constitution. The decision invalidated congressional term-limit provisions adopted by Arkansas and, in effect, those of 22 other states.

Background

In 1992 Arkansas voters approved Amendment 73, which barred the names of long-serving congressional incumbents from the ballot. Representative Ray Thornton and the League of Women Voters challenged the provision, and U.S. Term Limits intervened to defend it. The Arkansas Supreme Court struck down the congressional term limits, and the case reached the US Supreme Court.

Decision

In a 5–4 opinion by Justice John Paul Stevens, the Court held that the qualifications listed in the Constitution — age, citizenship and residency — are exclusive, drawing on Powell v. McCormack (1969), and that the Tenth Amendment could not reserve to the states a power over federal elections that they had never possessed before the Constitution. Justice Kennedy concurred, emphasising the direct relationship between the people of the United States and their federal government. Justice Clarence Thomas's dissent argued that the Constitution is silent on term limits and that the states, or the people acting through them, retained the power to adopt them.

Significance

The ruling ended the early-1990s state-level term limits movement for Congress; term limits on federal legislators would require a constitutional amendment. States remain free to limit the terms of their own state officials.
