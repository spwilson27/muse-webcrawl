Medical ethics

[Image: The Hippocratic Oath manuscript]

Medical ethics is an applied branch of ethics which analyses the practice of clinical medicine and related scientific research. Medical ethics is based on a set of values that professionals can refer to in the case of any confusion or conflict. These values include the respect for autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence, and justice. Such tenets may allow doctors, care providers, and families to create a treatment plan and work towards the same common goal. It is important to note that these four values are not ranked in order of importance or relevance and that they all encompass values pertaining to medical ethics.

Principles

The "four principles" approach, articulated by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress, frames medical ethics through:

  • Autonomy — the patient has the right to refuse or choose their treatment, underpinning informed consent.
  • Beneficence — practitioners should act in the best interest of the patient.
  • Non-maleficence — "first, do no harm"; the harms of an intervention must be weighed against its benefits.
  • Justice — fairness in the distribution of scarce health resources and in treatment decisions.

History

Codes of medical conduct date from antiquity, most famously the Hippocratic Oath. Modern medical ethics was reshaped by reactions to abuses such as the Nazi medical experiments, codified in the Nuremberg Code (1947), the World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (1964) on research ethics, and the Declaration of Geneva. Landmark issues include informed consent, confidentiality, end-of-life care, abortion, and research on human subjects.
