Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898 (1997), was a United States Supreme Court decision holding that the federal government may not compel state or local officers to implement federal regulatory programs — a doctrine known as the anti-commandeering doctrine. The Court struck down interim provisions of the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act that required local chief law enforcement officers to conduct background checks on prospective handgun purchasers.

Background

The Brady Act of 1993 imposed a five-day waiting period on handgun purchases pending the creation of the national instant criminal background check system (NICS). In the interim, local officers were required to make "reasonable efforts" to verify that a proposed sale was lawful. Sheriffs Jay Printz of Montana and Richard Mack of Arizona sued, arguing the requirement violated the Tenth Amendment and principles of federalism.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision written by Justice Antonin Scalia, the Court invalidated the interim provisions. Building on New York v. United States (1992), which barred Congress from commandeering state legislatures, the Court held that Congress likewise cannot conscript state executive officers to administer federal law. Scalia's opinion rested on historical practice, the structure of the Constitution, and the argument that commandeering blurs political accountability, since voters cannot tell which level of government is responsible for a policy. Justice John Paul Stevens filed the principal dissent.

Significance

Printz became a cornerstone of modern federalism jurisprudence. The anti-commandeering rule was applied again in Murphy v. NCAA (2018) to strike down a federal ban on state-authorised sports betting, and the case is frequently cited in disputes over state and local cooperation with federal enforcement, including sanctuary city policies. The interim background-check requirement became moot when NICS launched in 1998.
