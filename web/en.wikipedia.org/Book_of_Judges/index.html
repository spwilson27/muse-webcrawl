Book of Judges

The Book of Judges (Hebrew: ספר שופטים, Sefer Shoftim) is the seventh book of the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament. It recounts the history of the Israelites between the death of Joshua and the establishment of the monarchy, a period traditionally dated to roughly the 12th–11th centuries BC, during which Israel was led by charismatic military leaders and arbitrators called "judges" (shoftim).

Contents

Judges falls into three parts: an introduction describing the incomplete conquest of Canaan; the main body, a series of stories about the judges; and an appendix (chapters 17–21) containing the stories of Micah's idol, the migration of the Danites, and the Levite's concubine and the ensuing civil war against the tribe of Benjamin.

The main narratives follow a recurring theological cycle: Israel does evil and worships other gods; God delivers them into the hands of enemies; the people cry out; and God raises up a judge who delivers them, after which the land has rest. The major judges are Othniel, Ehud, Deborah (with Barak, and Jael who kills the Canaanite general Sisera), Gideon, Jephthah, and Samson. Minor judges include Shamgar, Tola, Jair, Ibzan, Elon and Abdon. Deborah's victory song in chapter 5 is considered among the oldest texts in the Bible.

Notable stories

Gideon, called while threshing wheat in hiding, defeats the Midianites with just 300 men chosen at the water. Jephthah, victorious over the Ammonites, makes a rash vow and sacrifices his own daughter. Samson, a Nazirite of superhuman strength, fights the Philistines, is betrayed by Delilah, and dies pulling down the temple of Dagon on himself and his enemies. The book closes with the refrain that frames its theme of anarchy: "In those days there was no king in Israel; every man did what was right in his own eyes."

Composition

Scholars generally assign Judges to the Deuteronomistic History — the linked narrative from Deuteronomy through Kings — edited in the 7th–6th centuries BC, though it preserves much older traditions and poems. Archaeology's picture of early Israel as emerging largely from within Canaanite society has led historians to treat the book's conquest narratives cautiously, while the judges themselves may preserve genuine memories of local heroes.
