History of the Jews in Tunisia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of the Jews in Tunisia extends over nearly two millennia. Jewish presence in the region of ancient Carthage may date to the Punic era, and some traditions place Jewish settlement after the destruction of the First or Second Temple in Jerusalem. Under Roman, Vandal, Byzantine, and then Arab rule, Jewish communities existed throughout the territory. Kairouan became a world centre of Jewish scholarship in the 9th–11th centuries, and the island of Djerba—home to the El Ghriba Synagogue and a community claiming descent from Temple priests—remains a centre of Jewish life and pilgrimage. Tunisia's Jewish population peaked at over 100,000 around 1948; most subsequently emigrated to Israel and France, and around 1,000–1,500 Jews remain, mainly in Tunis and Djerba.

Antiquity and the Middle Ages

Ancient synagogues and inscriptions attest Jewish communities in Roman Africa; the community of ancient Carthage is mentioned by Josephus and later rabbinic sources. Persecution under the Vandals and Byzantines alternated with periods of calm. After the Arab conquest (7th–8th centuries), Jews lived as dhimmis under Muslim rule. The city of Kairouan, founded in 670, became one of the great centres of Jewish learning: its yeshiva corresponded with the Babylonian geonim, and figures such as Isaac Israeli (c. 855–955), physician and philosopher to the Fatimid court, Dunash ibn Tamim, and Rabbi Nissim ben Jacob made it famous. The Almohad invasions of the 12th century brought forced conversions and drove many Jews to flee or practise in secret; conditions later eased under the Hafsid dynasty. Refugees from Spain (after 1492) and Livornese ("Grana") Jews from Italy augmented the community, and a lasting division existed between the indigenous "Twansa" and the Europeanised Grana, with separate institutions in Tunis until the 20th century.

Ottoman era and French protectorate

Under Ottoman suzerainty and the Husainid beys, Jews faced discriminatory laws but also opportunities in trade and finance; the caïd (community leader) often served as the bey's treasurer. The 1857 Fundamental Pact and the 1861 constitution proclaimed legal equality, though implementation was uneven and marred by incidents such as the execution of Batto Sfez (1857), which prompted European intervention. The establishment of the French protectorate in 1881 brought Alliance Israélite Universelle schools, emancipation on the French model for some, and growing Europeanisation; unlike Algerian Jews, however, Tunisian Jews were not collectively granted French citizenship.

The twentieth century

Tunisian Jews were active in commerce, the professions, journalism, and politics; Albert Bessis became a minister in the 1950s. Under the Vichy regime's reach into Tunisia and the German occupation of November 1942–May 1943, Jews were subjected to forced labour, fines, and deportations of some leaders, though the community escaped the deportations to death camps visited on European Jewry. After 1948 and Tunisian independence (1956), amid Arab nationalism, the 1961 Bizerte crisis, and anti-Jewish riots in 1967, the great majority emigrated—roughly half to Israel and half to France. The 1985 Israeli raid on PLO headquarters in Tunis and periodic tensions during the intifadas prompted further departures and security concerns, including the 2002 al-Qaeda bombing of the Ghriba synagogue on Djerba, which killed 19 people.

Today

The remaining community, centred in Tunis and Djerba, maintains synagogues, schools, and kosher facilities; the annual Lag BaOmer pilgrimage to El Ghriba draws thousands of visitors from abroad. Prominent Tunisian Jews include the writer Albert Memmi and the singer and actresses of the Judeo-Tunisian musical tradition. The chief rabbinate continues to function, and relations between the small community and the Tunisian state are formally protected, though the population continues slowly to decline.
