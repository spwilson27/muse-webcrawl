The Yugoslav Partisans, formally the National Liberation Army and Partisan Detachments of Yugoslavia, were the communist-led resistance movement against the Axis occupation of Yugoslavia during the Second World War. Led by Josip Broz Tito, they are considered among the most effective resistance movements in occupied Europe and by 1945 had grown into a regular army of some 800,000 soldiers.

Formation and uprising

After the Axis invasion and partition of Yugoslavia in April 1941, the Communist Party of Yugoslavia organised an uprising in July 1941. Early liberated territory around Užice in Serbia was retaken by German offensives, and the Partisans withdrew into Bosnia, where they built a long-lasting base area around Bihać.

Major campaigns

The Partisans survived a series of large Axis offensives, including the Battle of the Neretva and the Battle of the Sutjeska in 1943, and Tito narrowly escaped capture in the airborne raid on Drvar in 1944. At the Tehran Conference the Allies recognised the Partisans as the principal Yugoslav resistance and shifted support from the royalist Chetniks. In 1944, cooperating with the Red Army, the Partisans liberated Belgrade, and in 1945 they cleared the remaining Axis forces, taking Zagreb and advancing into Trieste and Carinthia.

Political outcome

Alongside the military struggle, the Partisan political organisation AVNOJ laid the foundations of a new federal, socialist Yugoslavia. After the war Tito's communist government took power, and the Partisan victory became the founding myth of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, commemorated in monuments, films and holidays.
