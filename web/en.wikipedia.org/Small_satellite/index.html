Small satellite

A small satellite, also known as a smallsat, is a satellite of low mass and size, usually weighing less than 500 kilograms (1,100 lb). Such satellites are commonly classified by mass: minisatellites (100–500 kg), microsatellites (10–100 kg), nanosatellites (1–10 kg), picosatellites (0.1–1 kg) and femtosatellites (below 100 g). Miniaturized electronics, standardized form factors and cheaper launch opportunities have made small satellites the fastest-growing segment of the space industry since the 2010s.

CubeSats and standardization

The CubeSat standard, developed at Stanford and California Polytechnic State University around 1999–2003, defines satellites in units (1U = 10 cm cube) that fit a common deployer, enabling universities and companies to build spacecraft from commercial off-the-shelf components. CubeSats and other smallsats typically launch as secondary payloads ("rideshares") or in batches on dedicated small launch vehicles.

Applications

Small satellites are used for Earth observation, communications, technology demonstration, science and education. Commercial constellations — Planet's fleet of imaging CubeSats photographing the Earth daily, and large broadband constellations such as Starlink, whose flat-panel satellites are individually modest in mass — demonstrate how networks of small satellites can deliver capabilities once requiring a few large, expensive spacecraft. Swarms and formations of smallsats can provide high revisit rates and distributed measurements.

Limitations and concerns

Small size constrains power, aperture, propulsion and mission life, and many smallsats lack maneuvering capability. Their proliferation has intensified concerns about space debris and the long-term sustainability of low Earth orbit, prompting regulation on deorbiting and passivation. Small satellites are nevertheless credited with democratizing access to space, shortening development cycles from decades to months or a few years.
