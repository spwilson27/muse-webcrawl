Battle of the Scheldt

Date2 October – 8 November 1944
LocationScheldt estuary, northern Belgium and southwestern Netherlands
ResultAllied victory; port of Antwerp opened
BelligerentsCanada, United Kingdom, Poland and allies vs. Nazi Germany
CommandersLt-Gen. Guy Simonds (First Canadian Army) vs. Gen. Gustav-Adolf von Zangen

The Battle of the Scheldt was a series of operations in the autumn of 1944, undertaken principally by the First Canadian Army, to clear the banks of the Scheldt estuary and open the port of Antwerp to Allied shipping. Although Antwerp had been captured intact in September, its approaches remained in German hands, and until the estuary was cleared the port — desperately needed to relieve the Allies' supply crisis — was useless.

Fighting through flooded, mined polder country defined the battle.

Background

After the Normandy campaign, Allied armies raced across France and Belgium, outrunning their supply lines from Normandy. Antwerp, Europe's second-largest port, fell to the British 11th Armoured Division on 4 September, but German forces retained both banks of the 70-kilometre estuary. Field Marshal Montgomery prioritised Operation Market Garden in September, giving the Germans time to fortify the Scheldt region, including the island of Walcheren and the South Beveland peninsula, and to flood much of the low-lying ground.

The operations

The battle comprised four main operations. Canadian forces cleared the area north of Antwerp and cut off South Beveland (Operation Switchback centred on the Breskens pocket, where amphibious landings were made behind German lines across the flooded terrain), advanced along the South Beveland peninsula, and finally assaulted Walcheren island (Operation Infatuate), whose sea dykes were breached by RAF bombing so that the island was partly flooded before amphibious landings on 1 November. Fighting was bitter, often in waist-deep water, against determined defenders; the Black Watch of Canada was nearly destroyed in one attack along the Beveland causeway approach.

Aftermath

The estuary was declared clear on 8 November after minesweepers had swept the channel; the first convoy reached Antwerp on 28 November 1944. Allied casualties in the battle were about 12,900, roughly half of them Canadian; some 41,000 German prisoners were taken. Opening Antwerp transformed Allied logistics for the final campaigns into Germany.
