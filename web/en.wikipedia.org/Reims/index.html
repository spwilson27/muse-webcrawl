Reims is a city in the Grand Est region of northeastern France, about 130 km (80 miles) northeast of Paris. It is the largest city in the department of Marne, though not its prefecture (which is Châlons-en-Champagne). Reims is famous as the traditional coronation city of the kings of France and as a principal centre of Champagne production.

History

Founded by the Gauls and known to the Romans as Durocortorum, Reims was an important city of Roman Gaul. According to tradition, Clovis I, king of the Franks, was baptized there by Saint Remi around 496, beginning the association between Reims and the French monarchy. From 1027 onward, most French kings were crowned in Reims Cathedral, a tradition that lasted until Charles X in 1825. Joan of Arc famously escorted the Dauphin to his coronation there in 1429 during the Hundred Years' War.

Reims was heavily damaged during the First World War; its cathedral was shelled and burned in 1914, becoming a symbol of German barbarism in French propaganda, and was painstakingly restored afterward. On 7 May 1945, Germany signed its unconditional surrender at General Eisenhower's headquarters in Reims, an event commemorated by the Museum of the Surrender.

Landmarks

The city's Romanesque and Gothic monuments — Reims Cathedral, the adjacent Palace of Tau, and the Abbey of Saint-Rémi — were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1991. The cathedral, begun in 1211, is a masterpiece of High Gothic architecture, celebrated for its sculpted portals, including the famous Smiling Angel. Roman remains include the Porte de Mars, a large triumphal arch.

Champagne

Reims is home to many of the great Champagne houses — among them Veuve Clicquot, Taittinger, Pommery, Ruinart, and Mumm — whose cellars (crayères), dug into the chalk, are part of the UNESCO-listed Champagne hillsides, houses and cellars (2015).
