Baroclinic instability

In fluid dynamics, baroclinic instability is an instability that occurs in a rotating, stratified fluid in which surfaces of constant pressure and constant density do not coincide — a baroclinic state. It is the primary mechanism by which mid-latitude weather systems (extratropical cyclones) in the atmosphere, and mesoscale eddies in the ocean, extract energy from large-scale flows.

Mechanism

In a baroclinic atmosphere, temperature varies horizontally on pressure surfaces, which by the thermal wind relation implies that wind speed increases with height. The associated stored available potential energy can be released when small disturbances grow by arranging for warm air to rise and cold air to sink, flattening the tilted density surfaces. The theory was developed in the 1940s by Jule Charney (1947) and Eric Eady (1949), whose simplified models — the Charney and Eady models — derived growth rates and preferred wavelengths of a few thousand kilometres, matching observed cyclones.

Significance

Baroclinic instability sets the scale and growth rate of synoptic weather systems, drives storm tracks, and transports heat poleward. In oceanography it generates mesoscale eddies that play a similar role in mixing and transport.
