Anthem

An anthem is a musical composition of celebration, usually used as a symbol for a distinct group of people, such as the national anthem of a country or a school song. Originally the term referred to a form of liturgical music in the Anglican Church — a choral setting of a religious or moral text, sung during services as an alternative to a psalm.

History

The word derives from the Greek antiphōna via Old English antefn, originally meaning a chant sung in alternation by two choirs. In the Church of England the anthem became a distinct musical form during the Reformation; composers such as Thomas Tallis, William Byrd and later Henry Purcell and George Frideric Handel wrote celebrated anthems, Handel's Zadok the Priest (1727) remaining a fixture of British coronations.

National anthems

By extension, "anthem" became the standard term for a patriotic song officially adopted by a nation, typically played or sung at state occasions, sporting events and ceremonies. Among the best known are "La Marseillaise" (France), "The Star-Spangled Banner" (United States) and "Kimigayo" (Japan). See national anthem.

Other uses

The word is also used for songs strongly identified with a movement, subculture or event — for example a "gay anthem" or a rock anthem — and titles works such as Ayn Rand's 1938 novella Anthem.
