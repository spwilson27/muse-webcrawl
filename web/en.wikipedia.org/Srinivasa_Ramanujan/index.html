Srinivasa Ramanujan (22 December 1887 – 26 April 1920) was an Indian mathematician who, with almost no formal training in pure mathematics, made extraordinary contributions to mathematical analysis, number theory, infinite series, and continued fractions. He is regarded as one of the greatest mathematicians of all time, and his original and unconventional results have inspired a vast amount of further research.

Early life

Ramanujan was born in Erode, in present-day Tamil Nadu, to a Tamil Brahmin family. His father was a clerk in a cloth merchant's shop and his mother a housewife who sang at the local temple. A prodigy from childhood, he mastered advanced trigonometry by age twelve and, after encountering G. S. Carr's A Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure and Applied Mathematics, began his own research on Bernoulli numbers, infinite series and integrals. He won a scholarship to Government Arts College, Kumbakonam, but lost it because he neglected all subjects other than mathematics.

Contact with G. H. Hardy

In 1913 Ramanujan wrote to the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy, enclosing some 120 theorems he had discovered. Hardy, recognising the work of a genius, arranged for Ramanujan to travel to England. Ramanujan arrived in London in April 1914 and began work with Hardy and J. E. Littlewood at Trinity College. In 1916 he was awarded a Bachelor of Arts by research degree, and in 1918 he became one of the youngest Fellows of the Royal Society and the first Indian Fellow of Trinity College.

Mathematical work

Ramanujan compiled nearly 3,900 identities and equations, many of them collected in his famous notebooks. His results include the Ramanujan conjecture (later a key part of the proof of the Weil conjectures), highly original formulas for pi, partition function congruences, and the mock theta functions, whose significance was only fully appreciated near the end of the twentieth century. The Hardy–Ramanujan number 1729 — the smallest number expressible as a sum of two cubes in two different ways — is named after an anecdote from his final illness. Together with Hardy he devised the circle method for estimating the partition function.

Illness and death

Ramanujan's health, undermined by illness — later diagnosed as hepatic amoebiasis — and the restrictions of his strict vegetarian diet in wartime England, deteriorated badly. He returned to India in 1919 and died the following year, aged 32, leaving three notebooks and a sheaf of papers (the "lost notebook", rediscovered in 1976) whose contents mathematicians are still studying.

Legacy

The state of Tamil Nadu celebrates his birthday, 22 December, as State IT Day, and India observed 2012 as the National Mathematics Year in his honour. His life was dramatised in the 2015 film The Man Who Knew Infinity. The journal The Ramanujan Journal publishes work influenced by his research.
