Vacuum brake

The vacuum brake is a braking system once widely used on trains, in which braking effort is produced by a partial vacuum in a continuous pipe running the length of the train. It was a standard system on British railways and in many other countries before being largely replaced by the air brake.

Operation

An ejector or vacuum pump on the locomotive evacuates the train pipe to about 21 inches of mercury. Each vehicle carries brake cylinders whose pistons are held off by vacuum on both sides; admitting air to the pipe destroys the vacuum on one side and atmospheric pressure drives the piston, applying the brakes. If the train divides, vacuum is lost and the brakes apply automatically, making the system fail-safe.

Comparison with air brakes

Vacuum brakes are simpler and were adequate for moderate speeds and train weights, but the maximum pressure difference of less than one atmosphere limits braking force, and the system responds more slowly than compressed-air brakes. Modern practice favours air brakes, though vacuum brakes remain in heritage and some regional service.
