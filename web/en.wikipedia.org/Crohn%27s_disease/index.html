Crohn's disease

SpecialtyGastroenterology
SymptomsAbdominal pain, diarrhoea, fever, weight loss
ComplicationsBowel obstruction, fistulae, abscesses, colorectal cancer
Usual onset15–35 years

Crohn's disease is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) that may affect any segment of the gastrointestinal tract, most commonly the terminal ileum and colon. It causes transmural inflammation — inflammation extending through the entire thickness of the bowel wall — and can lead to strictures, fistulae, and abscesses. It is named after American gastroenterologist Burrill Bernard Crohn, who described the condition in 1932.

Signs and symptoms

Common symptoms include persistent diarrhoea, abdominal pain and cramping, blood in the stool, mouth sores, reduced appetite, and weight loss. Systemic features such as fever, fatigue, anaemia, and inflammation of the eyes, skin, and joints may also occur. The course of the disease typically alternates between flare-ups and periods of remission.

Cause

The exact cause is unknown. Crohn's disease appears to result from an inappropriate immune response to gut microbes in genetically susceptible individuals; variants in genes such as NOD2/CARD15 are associated with increased risk, and smoking doubles the risk of developing the disease.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on endoscopy with biopsy, imaging, and laboratory studies, and depends on excluding infections and ulcerative colitis. There is no cure; treatment aims to induce and maintain remission with anti-inflammatory drugs, immunosuppressants, and biologic therapies such as anti-TNF agents. Surgery is reserved for complications and refractory disease, since inflammation often recurs at the site of resection.

Epidemiology

Incidence is highest in North America and Europe, historically estimated at roughly 3 to 20 cases per 100,000 people per year, and rates are rising in newly industrialised countries.
