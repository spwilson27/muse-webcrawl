Muon

The muon (symbol μ) is an elementary particle similar to the electron, with an electric charge of −1 e and a spin of 1/2, but with a much greater mass — about 105.7 MeV/c², roughly 207 times that of the electron. It is classified as a lepton and is an unstable subatomic particle with a mean lifetime of 2.2 microseconds.

Muons were discovered in cosmic ray experiments by Carl D. Anderson and Seth Neddermeyer in 1936. Because its mass lay between those of the electron and the proton, it was initially mistaken for the meson predicted by Yukawa, prompting I. I. Rabi's famous remark, "Who ordered that?"

Decay and properties

A negative muon decays into an electron, an electron antineutrino and a muon neutrino; the positive muon (antimuon) decays into the corresponding antiparticles. Muons produced in the upper atmosphere by cosmic rays reach the ground in large numbers thanks to relativistic time dilation — an often-cited confirmation of special relativity. The muon's magnetic moment is measured with extraordinary precision, and small discrepancies with theory have long been a focus of particle physics research. Muons can also be captured by nuclei to form short-lived muonic atoms.
