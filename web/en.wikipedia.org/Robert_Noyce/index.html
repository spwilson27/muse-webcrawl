Robert Norton Noyce (December 12, 1927 – June 3, 1990), nicknamed "the Mayor of Silicon Valley", was an American physicist and entrepreneur who co-founded Fairchild Semiconductor in 1957 and Intel in 1968. He is credited, along with Jack Kilby, with the invention of the integrated circuit (IC) or microchip — his 1959 version, based on planar silicon technology, was the design that proved practical to manufacture, and it ignited the personal computer revolution and gave California's Silicon Valley its name.

Career

Noyce studied physics at Grinnell College and earned a PhD from MIT in 1953. After research at Philco and a stint at William Shockley's laboratory, he was one of the "traitorous eight" who left Shockley in 1957 to found Fairchild Semiconductor. There he developed the planar process and, in 1959, patented a silicon integrated circuit with aluminium interconnects. In 1968 he and Gordon Moore left to found Intel, where Noyce's informal, egalitarian management style helped define Silicon Valley's corporate culture; Intel launched the first commercial dynamic RAM and, in 1971, the first commercial microprocessor.

Honours

Noyce received the National Medal of Science (1979), the National Medal of Technology (1987) and the IEEE Medal of Honor (1978), and was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. He declined to live lavishly on his fortune and in his later years led Sematech, the industry consortium defending U.S. semiconductor manufacturing. He died of a heart attack in Austin, Texas, in 1990. The Noyce Foundation, established by his family, supports education, and Google honoured him with a "chip" doodle on what would have been his 84th birthday.
