Atmospheric optics

Atmospheric optics is the study of the optical characteristics of the atmosphere and how they produce the range of optical phenomena observed, including rainbows, halos, mirages, glories, and the colours of the sky. Many of these effects arise from the scattering, refraction, reflection and diffraction of light by air molecules, water droplets and ice crystals.

A primary rainbow, among the best-known atmospheric optical phenomena.

Colour of the sky

The blue colour of the daytime sky results from Rayleigh scattering, in which air molecules scatter short (blue) wavelengths of sunlight much more strongly than long (red) wavelengths. At sunrise and sunset, light passes through a longer path of air, most of the blue is scattered out, and the Sun appears red.

Rainbows and halos

A rainbow is produced by refraction, internal reflection and dispersion of sunlight in raindrops, forming a circular arc centred on the antisolar point. Halos are rings and arcs around the Sun or Moon produced by refraction or reflection in hexagonal ice crystals in cirrus clouds; the most common is the 22° halo.

Mirages and other phenomena

A mirage occurs when layers of air at strongly different temperatures refract light so that displaced or inverted images appear; inferior mirages are familiar on hot roads, while superior mirages and the complex Fata Morgana occur over cold surfaces. Other phenomena treated in atmospheric optics include the glory, the corona, iridescent clouds, crepuscular rays, the green flash, and the gegenschein.

History

Explanations of the rainbow were developed by Theodoric of Freiberg and Kamāl al-Dīn al-Fārisī in the 14th century, and René Descartes gave the first quantitative account of the rainbow's angle in 1637. The classic modern reference is Marcel Minnaert's The Nature of Light and Colour in the Open Air (1940).
