Saxon Shore

The Saxon Shore (Latin: litus Saxonicum) was a military command of the Late Roman Empire, consisting of a series of fortifications on both sides of the English Channel. It was established in the late 3rd century and was led by the "Count of the Saxon Shore" (Comes Litoris Saxonici). In the late 4th century, his functions were limited to Britain, while the fortifications in Gaul were established as separate commands. Several Saxon Shore forts survive in east and south-east England, among the best-preserved Roman military monuments in the country.

The forts

The principal British forts, listed in the late-4th-century Notitia Dignitatum, ran from the Wash to the Solent: Brancaster (Branodunum) and Burgh Castle (Gariannonum) in Norfolk, Bradwell-on-Sea (Othona) in Essex, Reculver (Regulbium), Richborough (Rutupiae), Dover (Dubris), and Lympne (Lemannis) in Kent, and Pevensey (Anderitum) and Portchester (Portus Adurni) on the south coast. Built mainly between about AD 270 and 290, the later forts display the characteristic features of late Roman military architecture: thick stone walls with projecting bastions for artillery, single gated entrances, and sites positioned on estuaries and harbours.

Purpose and interpretation

The name may mean the shore attacked by Saxons, or the shore settled by Saxons. Traditionally the forts were read as a coordinated defensive system built against Germanic sea-raiders—Saxons, Franks, and others—who increasingly raided Roman Britain and Gaul in the 3rd and 4th centuries; the command may have been organized under the usurper Carausius, who also campaigned against Frankish pirates. Alternative interpretations see the chain as protecting shipping and the shipment of British grain and goods to the Rhine armies, or as fortified commercial ports. The command survived in Britain until the Roman withdrawal in the early 5th century.

Survival

Portchester Castle retains the most complete circuit of Roman walls in northern Europe; Pevensey, where William the Conqueror landed in 1066, was refortified in the Middle Ages; Richborough was also the site of the Claudian invasion base of AD 43. Several forts were later occupied by Saxon settlements and Norman castles, and Dover's Roman lighthouse still stands within the castle precinct.
