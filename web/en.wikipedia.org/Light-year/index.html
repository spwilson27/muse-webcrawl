DefinitionDistance light travels in vacuum in one Julian year

A light-year is a unit of length used to express astronomical distances, equal to the distance that light travels in a vacuum in one Julian year (365.25 days). Because the speed of light is exactly 299,792,458 metres per second, one light-year is exactly 9,460,730,472,580.8 kilometres — roughly 9.46 trillion kilometres or 5.88 trillion miles. Despite its name, the light-year is a measure of distance, not time.

Usage

The light-year is most often used when expressing distances to stars and other distances on a galactic scale, especially in popular science. Professional astronomers generally prefer the parsec (about 3.26 light-years) for such distances. Distances between solar-system objects are usually given in astronomical units, while intergalactic distances use multiples such as kiloparsecs and megaparsecs.

Examples

  • Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to the Sun, is about 4.246 light-years away.
  • The Milky Way galaxy is roughly 100,000 light-years across.
  • The Andromeda Galaxy is about 2.5 million light-years away.
  • The observable universe has a radius of about 46.5 billion light-years.

History

The unit arose after the first successful measurement of the distance to a star other than the Sun, by Friedrich Bessel in 1838, and appeared in popular German astronomy writing in 1851 before spreading into English-language use. Smaller related units such as the light-second (about 300,000 km) and light-minute are also used; the Moon is roughly 1.3 light-seconds from Earth.
