Roman Britain was the territory of Great Britain under Roman rule from the Claudian invasion of AD 43 until the end of Roman administration around AD 410 — roughly modern England and Wales, with shifting frontiers in what is now Scotland.

Conquest

Julius Caesar had raided Britain in 55 and 54 BC, but permanent conquest began under Claudius in AD 43, when four legions under Aulus Plautius landed in Kent. Resistance led by Caratacus ended by AD 51. The Iceni revolt under Boudica (AD 60/61) burned Colchester, London and St Albans before its defeat. Agricola pushed into Scotland, winning at Mons Graupius (AD 83/84), but Rome never held the far north; Hadrian's Wall (begun AD 122) and the short-lived Antonine Wall (AD 142) marked the frontier.

Society and economy

Romano-British society combined Roman towns, villas, roads, baths and law with native British culture. Londinium became the largest city, with perhaps 60,000 inhabitants. Roads such as Watling Street and Ermine Street, ports, mines (lead, tin, gold at Dolaucothi), pottery and grain exports tied Britain into the empire's economy. Latin was the language of administration; Christianity spread in the 3rd–4th centuries, producing martyrs such as St Alban and, from Britain, the emperor Constantine was proclaimed at York in 306.

End of Roman rule

Usurpers repeatedly drained the garrison — Magnus Maximus (383) and Constantine III (407) took armies to the continent. Around 409–410 the Britons expelled Roman officials and, in the traditional account, the Emperor Honorius told the cities to look to their own defence. Roman coinage and mass-produced goods vanish from the archaeological record within a generation, and Anglo-Saxon settlement reshaped lowland Britain over the following two centuries.
