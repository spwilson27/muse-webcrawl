Visible spectrum

The visible spectrum is the portion of the electromagnetic spectrum that is visible to the human eye. Electromagnetic radiation in this range of wavelengths is called visible light. A typical human eye responds to wavelengths from about 380 to about 750 nanometres, corresponding to frequencies of roughly 790 down to 400 terahertz. In the spectrum, these wavelengths are perceived as the colours red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet.

History

In the 1660s Isaac Newton used a prism to disperse sunlight into a spectrum and showed that the colours were intrinsic to the light, not added by the glass; he divided the spectrum into seven colours by analogy with the musical scale. In 1800 William Herschel discovered infrared radiation just beyond the red end, and in 1801 Johann Wilhelm Ritter found ultraviolet beyond the violet, showing that the visible band is part of a much larger continuum.

Colour perception

The retina contains three types of cone cells sensitive to short (blue), medium (green) and long (red) wavelengths. The brain compares their signals to produce colour perception, a scheme exploited by RGB displays. The exact boundaries of visibility vary between individuals and with light intensity; sensitivity peaks near 555 nm (green) under daylight conditions. Many animals see different ranges: bees and many birds are sensitive to ultraviolet, while some snakes detect infrared via specialised organs rather than the eye.

Atmospheric window

Earth's atmosphere transmits visible light relatively well — one reason, it is thought, that eyes evolved sensitivity in this band. Sunlight reaching the surface peaks in the visible range, and water also absorbs least strongly in the blue-green part of the spectrum.
