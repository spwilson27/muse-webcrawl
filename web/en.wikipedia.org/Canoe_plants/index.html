Canoe plants

Canoe plants is the term for the useful plants that Polynesian voyagers carried across the Pacific Ocean in their voyaging canoes and introduced to the islands they settled, including Hawaiʻi, New Zealand (Aotearoa), and Rapa Nui. The plants formed the agricultural foundation of Polynesian societies and transformed island ecosystems.

Background

Polynesian settlers voyaged vast ocean distances in double-hulled canoes, deliberately stocking them with planting material, animals, and supplies. Because many remote islands lacked edible native plants, these transported cultivars were essential to survival. About two dozen plant species are recognized as canoe plants in Hawaiʻi.

Principal species

The most important canoe plants include taro (kalo), the central staple of Hawaiian culture; coconut (niu); banana (maiʻa); sugarcane (kō); breadfruit (ʻulu); sweet potato (ʻuala), whose South American origin testifies to pre-Columbian contact between Polynesia and the Americas; yam; ʻawa (kava); ti (kī); paper mulberry (wauke), used for barkcloth; and kukui (candlenut). Non-food plants such as bamboo and bottle gourd also travelled as containers and tools.

Impact

Canoe plants reshaped island landscapes, supporting terraced irrigation systems and large populations. Some species naturalized aggressively, while others, like Hawaiian sandalwood, were later over-exploited. Today canoe plants are symbols of Hawaiian and Polynesian cultural identity, and traditional varieties are being preserved by indigenous farmers and seed banks.
