Big Bang

The Big Bang is the prevailing cosmological model explaining the origin and evolution of the universe. It describes how the universe expanded from an initial state of extremely high density and temperature approximately 13.8 billion years ago, and continues to expand today. The model is supported by comprehensive observations including the cosmic microwave background, the abundance of light elements, and the redshift of distant galaxies.

History of the model

In the 1920s, Alexander Friedmann and Georges Lemaître independently derived expanding-universe solutions to Einstein's equations of general relativity; Lemaître proposed that the universe began from a "primeval atom". In 1929 Edwin Hubble reported that distant galaxies recede at speeds proportional to their distance, evidence of cosmic expansion. The name "Big Bang" was coined, somewhat dismissively, by Fred Hoyle in a 1949 radio broadcast; Hoyle favoured the rival steady-state theory.

Timeline

After an extremely brief period of exponential expansion known as cosmic inflation, the hot, dense universe cooled enough for fundamental particles to form, then protons and neutrons, and within the first minutes light atomic nuclei (hydrogen, helium, lithium) formed in Big Bang nucleosynthesis. After about 380,000 years, atoms formed and the universe became transparent, releasing the radiation now seen as the cosmic microwave background. Over billions of years, gravity gathered matter into stars, galaxies, and the large-scale structure of the cosmos. Since the late 1990s, observations of distant supernovae have shown that the expansion is accelerating, attributed to dark energy.

Evidence

  • The cosmic microwave background radiation, discovered in 1965 by Penzias and Wilson, matches the predicted relic heat of the hot early universe.
  • The observed abundances of hydrogen, helium, and lithium match nucleosynthesis predictions.
  • The Hubble–Lemaître law relating galactic redshift to distance confirms ongoing expansion.
