Metric system

The metric system is a decimal system of measurement first adopted in France in 1795, built on base units such as the metre and kilogram and scaling by powers of ten. Its modern form is the International System of Units (SI), the world's most widely used measurement system, employed officially by nearly every country; notable holdouts for everyday use include the United States, Liberia, and Myanmar.

History

Revolutionary France defined the metre as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the Equator, and the kilogram from a cubic decimetre of water. The Metre Convention of 1875 created the BIPM and international prototypes. Successive systems (MKS, MKSA) culminated in the SI in 1960. In 2019, all SI base units were redefined in terms of fixed physical constants — the speed of light, the Planck constant, the elementary charge, the Boltzmann and Avogadro constants, the caesium hyperfine frequency, and luminous efficacy — retiring the last artefact standard, the International Prototype of the Kilogram.

Features

The system's advantages are decimal coherence (derived units are products of base units without conversion factors), a single unit per quantity, and scaling by standard prefixes. Implementation varies: many countries metricated in the 19th–20th centuries, while the UK retains miles on roads and pints for beer, and aviation worldwide still uses feet for altitude.
