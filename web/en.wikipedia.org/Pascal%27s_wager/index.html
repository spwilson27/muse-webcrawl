Pascal's wager

Pascal's wager is a philosophical argument presented by the seventeenth-century French philosopher Blaise Pascal in his posthumously published Pensées. It holds that human beings bet with their lives on whether God exists, and that a rational person should live as though God exists: the potential gain (eternal happiness) vastly outweighs the finite loss if God does not exist, while disbelief risks infinite loss.

The argument

Pascal framed the choice as unavoidable—"you must wager"—and compared possible outcomes: believing and being right yields infinite reward; believing and being wrong costs little; not believing and being wrong risks infinite punishment; not believing and being right gains little. On expected-value reasoning, belief dominates.

Criticisms

Objections include the "many gods" problem (the wager does not specify which god to believe in), doubts that belief can be adopted at will, questions about whether a deity would reward calculated belief, and disputes over assigning infinite utilities.

Influence

The wager is an early landmark in decision theory and the philosophy of religion, and it continues to be debated by philosophers and theologians.
