Receptor (biochemistry)

In biochemistry and pharmacology, a receptor is a protein molecule that receives chemical signals from outside a cell. When such chemical signals bind to a receptor, they cause some form of cellular or tissue response, for example a change in the electrical activity of a cell. In this sense, a receptor is a protein molecule that recognises and responds to endogenous chemical signals.

Overview

The term receptor was popularised by Paul Ehrlich and John Newport Langley around the turn of the 20th century. Receptors can be broadly divided into two classes depending on their location. Intracellular receptors are located inside the cell and are activated by hydrophobic ligands that cross the cell membrane, such as steroid hormones. Cell surface receptors are embedded in the plasma membrane and are activated by extracellular ligands that cannot cross the membrane.

Cell surface receptors

Cell surface receptors are usually divided into three general classes: ion channel-linked receptors (ligand-gated ion channels), G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and enzyme-linked receptors such as receptor tyrosine kinases. GPCRs form the largest family of cell surface receptors in humans, with roughly 800 members, and are the targets of a large fraction of modern medicinal drugs.

Ligand binding

A molecule that binds to a receptor is called a ligand. A ligand that activates a receptor is an agonist, while one that binds without activating it and blocks agonist binding is an antagonist. Binding is usually reversible and follows the law of mass action; the strength of binding is described by the dissociation constant, Kd. Dose–response curves relate ligand concentration to the magnitude of the cellular response.

Signal transduction

Once activated, receptors trigger intracellular signalling cascades. GPCRs activate heterotrimeric G proteins, which modulate enzymes such as adenylyl cyclase and phospholipase C, producing second messengers including cyclic AMP, IP3 and diacylglycerol. Receptor tyrosine kinases autophosphorylate and recruit adaptor proteins that activate pathways such as the MAPK/ERK cascade.

Regulation

Receptor activity is tightly regulated. Prolonged stimulation often leads to desensitisation, in which the response diminishes despite continued ligand presence, and to downregulation, a decrease in receptor number through internalisation and degradation. Conversely, chronic blockade can cause upregulation and supersensitivity.
