Exon

An exon is any part of a gene that remains in the final mature RNA product after introns have been removed by RNA splicing. The term, coined by Walter Gilbert in 1978, derives from "expressed region." Exons include both the protein-coding sequences and the untranslated regions (UTRs) at the ends of messenger RNA.

Splicing and alternative splicing

In eukaryotes, genes are transcribed into pre-mRNA containing alternating exons and introns. The spliceosome removes the introns and ligates the exons. Through alternative splicing, exons can be included or skipped in different combinations, so a single gene can produce multiple distinct proteins — a major source of proteomic diversity in complex organisms. The human genome contains roughly 180,000 exons across its protein-coding genes.

Exon shuffling

Gilbert proposed that exons often correspond to functional protein domains, and that recombination within introns can shuffle exons between genes, assembling new proteins from pre-existing modules. Evidence for this "exon shuffling" is seen in modular proteins such as those of the blood-clotting cascade, whose domains recur in many combinations.
