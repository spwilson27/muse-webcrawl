Sediment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sediment deposited in layers along a river.

Sediment is a naturally occurring material that is broken down by processes of weathering and erosion, and is subsequently transported by the action of wind, water, or ice or by gravity, and eventually deposited. Sediments are most often transported by water (fluvial processes), but also by wind (aeolian processes) and glaciers. Sand dunes, river deltas, beach sands, and the deposits on the ocean floor are all examples of sediment accumulations.

Classification

Sediment can be classified by grain size, from clay and silt through sand and gravel to boulders, as described by the Wentworth scale. Clastic sediments are composed of fragments of pre-existing rock; chemical sediments precipitate from solution, as in evaporites and some limestones; and biogenic sediments consist of the remains of organisms, such as shell debris, coral, and diatom ooze. Composition and texture reveal the sediment's source, transport history, and depositional environment.

Transport and deposition

Moving fluid can entrain grains by traction (rolling and sliding), saltation (bouncing), and suspension. When the energy of the transporting medium falls, grains settle out — coarsest first — a process called deposition. Sediment transport shapes landscapes, builds floodplains and deltas, and, over geological time, fills sedimentary basins. Excess sediment or sediment starved by dams can destabilize river channels and coastlines.

Lithification

Buried sediments are compacted and cemented into sedimentary rock through lithification: sand becomes sandstone, mud becomes shale, and carbonate sediments become limestone. Sedimentary rocks preserve most of the fossils and the record of Earth's surface history, and they host major reservoirs of water, oil, and gas.
