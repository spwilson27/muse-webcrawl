The Suzuki reaction (also called the Suzuki–Miyaura coupling) is an organic reaction in which an organoboron compound (usually a boronic acid) is cross-coupled with an organic halide or triflate in the presence of a palladium(0) catalyst and a base. It is one of the most widely used methods for forming carbon–carbon bonds, particularly for making biaryl compounds.

History

The reaction was first published in 1979 by Akira Suzuki and Norio Miyaura at Hokkaido University. Suzuki shared the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Richard F. Heck and Ei-ichi Negishi "for palladium-catalyzed cross couplings in organic synthesis."

Mechanism

The catalytic cycle begins with oxidative addition of the organic halide to the Pd(0) catalyst, giving a Pd(II) intermediate. Reaction with base generates a reactive boronate from the boronic acid, and transmetalation transfers the organic group from boron to palladium. Reductive elimination then releases the coupled product and regenerates the Pd(0) catalyst. A base such as sodium carbonate, potassium phosphate, or cesium fluoride is essential, both to form the boronate and to neutralise the acid byproduct.

Advantages and scope

Boronic acids are attractive coupling partners because they are stable to air and moisture, tolerant of many functional groups, commercially available in great variety, and produce relatively non-toxic inorganic byproducts. The reaction is used on laboratory and industrial scales, including in the manufacture of pharmaceuticals such as the antihypertensive drug losartan, and in the synthesis of liquid crystals and advanced materials. Variants couple alkyl partners, though historically aryl and vinyl substrates have predominated.
