Autobiography

[Image placeholder: a handwritten life narrative]

An autobiography, sometimes informally called an auto-bio, is a self-written account of one's own life. The word, composed of the Greek autos (self), bios (life) and graphein (to write), was used sparingly in the late eighteenth century and became common in English in the early nineteenth century.

Nature of the genre

Unlike a biography, an autobiography is written by its subject. Unlike a diary or journal, it is usually composed retrospectively, imposing a narrative shape on a whole life or a substantial part of it. Autobiographical writing ranges from intimate memoirs written for private circulation to the widely read life stories of political leaders, artists and celebrities.

Early examples

Augustine of Hippo's Confessions (c. 400 AD) is often regarded as the first Western autobiography, blending personal narrative with theological reflection. In the fifteenth century Margery Kempe dictated what is considered the first autobiography in English. Jean-Jacques Rousseau's Confessions (1782) set a new standard of psychological candour, and Benjamin Franklin's unfinished Autobiography became a model of the self-made life story.

Memoir and related forms

A memoir is usually more focused than an autobiography, covering a particular period or theme rather than an entire life. Related forms include the apology, the spiritual autobiography, autofiction, and the personal essay. Scholars debate how far any autobiography can be factually reliable, since memory is selective and self-presentation shapes every account.

Modern autobiography

The twentieth and twenty-first centuries saw an expansion of the form: slave narratives, Holocaust testimony, political memoirs and celebrity autobiographies (often written with ghostwriters) all became major publishing categories. Autobiography is now also a recognised field of literary scholarship, studied for what it reveals about identity, memory and culture.
