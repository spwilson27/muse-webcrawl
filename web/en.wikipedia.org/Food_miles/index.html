Food miles

Food miles
ConceptDistance food travels from production to consumer
CoinedEarly 1990s, associated with Tim Lang and Sustain
Related conceptsCarbon footprint, local food, life-cycle assessment

Food miles is the distance food is transported from the time of its production until it reaches the consumer. Popularised in the early 1990s — notably in reports by the British organisation Sustain and the work of professor Tim Lang — the term became a shorthand for the environmental and social costs of long, globalised food supply chains, and a rallying concept for the local food movement.

Rationale

Advocates argue that long-distance transport increases fossil fuel use and greenhouse gas emissions (especially for air-freighted perishables), road congestion, packaging, and dependence on centralised distribution, while disconnecting consumers from producers and seasons. Measuring food miles was an early attempt to make these hidden costs visible to shoppers and policymakers.

Criticism

Life-cycle assessments complicate the picture: transport is often a modest share of food's total footprint — around a tenth in influential studies — while production methods dominate. Heated greenhouses near consumers can emit more than field-grown produce shipped from sunnier climates; a frequently cited comparison found lamb shipped from New Zealand could have lower overall emissions than some locally produced equivalents. Shipping by sea is far more efficient per tonne-kilometre than road or air, so distance alone is a poor proxy. Critics also warn that "buy local" campaigns can disadvantage exporters in developing countries for whom food exports are a vital income.

Use today

Food miles remains a popular, intuitive concept, but researchers and retailers increasingly favour full carbon footprinting and life-cycle analysis, sometimes combined with concerns about resilience, freshness, and regional economies that go beyond emissions alone. Some retailers label air-freighted products, and "locavore" eating and short supply chains (farmers' markets, box schemes) draw on the food-miles idea in broader form.
