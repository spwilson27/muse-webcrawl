Muscle cell

Also calledMyocyte
SystemMuscular system
FunctionContraction and force generation
TypesSkeletal, cardiac, smooth

A muscle cell, or myocyte, is a cell specialised for contraction. Muscle cells contain protein filaments of actin and myosin that slide past one another to shorten the cell and generate force. Animals use three types of muscle cell: skeletal muscle cells, which form the voluntary muscles attached to the skeleton; cardiac muscle cells, which make up the heart wall; and smooth muscle cells, which line organs such as the gut and blood vessels.

Skeletal muscle fibres are long, multinucleated cells with a striated appearance.

Types

Skeletal muscle cells, also called muscle fibres, are long cylindrical cells formed by the fusion of many precursor myoblasts, making them multinucleate; their actin and myosin are arranged in repeating units called sarcomeres, giving them a banded or striated appearance under the microscope. Cardiac muscle cells are also striated but are branched, usually single-nucleated, and joined end-to-end by intercalated discs that let the heart contract as a coordinated unit; they have intrinsic rhythmicity. Smooth muscle cells are spindle-shaped, lack striations, and produce slow, sustained, involuntary contractions.

Structure and contraction

Contraction is triggered when calcium ions flood the cell interior, exposing binding sites on actin so that myosin heads can attach, pull, and detach in a cycle powered by ATP — the sliding filament model. In skeletal muscle, calcium is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum when an action potential arrives from a motor neuron at the neuromuscular junction, releasing acetylcholine. Skeletal fibres are further classified as slow-twitch (type I, oxidative, fatigue-resistant) or fast-twitch (type II, glycolytic, powerful but easily fatigued).

Development and repair

Muscle cells develop from mesodermal stem cells called myoblasts. In adult skeletal muscle, satellite cells residing beside fibres can be activated by injury or exercise to repair damage and contribute to hypertrophy, though the heart and smooth muscle have more limited regenerative capacity.
