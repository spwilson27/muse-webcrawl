Ballistic missile

A ballistic missile at launch. [Image placeholder]

A ballistic missile is a type of missile that follows a ballistic trajectory for most of its flight: it is powered and guided during a relatively short initial "boost" phase, after which its warhead(s) follow an unpowered arc determined by gravity and momentum, re-entering the atmosphere toward the target. Ballistic missiles can carry conventional, chemical, biological or nuclear warheads and are typically launched from land-based silos or mobile launchers, submarines, ships or aircraft.

Flight phases

  1. Boost phase — the rocket motor burns (seconds to a few minutes), lifting the missile out of the atmosphere; multi-stage rockets discard empty stages.
  2. Midcourse phase — the warhead bus releases one or more re-entry vehicles, which coast through space along the ballistic arc, often accompanied by decoys and penetration aids.
  3. Terminal phase — the re-entry vehicles plunge back into the atmosphere at hypersonic speed toward their targets.

Classification by range

  • Tactical ballistic missile — under about 300 km
  • Short-range ballistic missile (SRBM) — 300 to 1,000 km
  • Medium-range ballistic missile (MRBM) — 1,000 to 3,500 km
  • Intermediate-range ballistic missile (IRBM) — 3,500 to 5,500 km
  • Intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) — over 5,500 km; modern examples reach beyond 10,000 km
  • Submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM) — classified by basing rather than range

History

The first ballistic missile was Germany's V-2 rocket, developed under Wernher von Braun and used against London and Antwerp in 1944–45. After the Second World War, captured V-2 technology seeded both the American and Soviet programmes. The USSR fielded the first ICBM, the R-7 Semyorka, in 1957 — a derivative of which launched Sputnik. During the Cold War, ballistic missile arsenals became central to nuclear deterrence strategy, and anti-ballistic missile defence systems and arms-control treaties (such as the ABM Treaty and INF Treaty) were developed in response.

Guidance and accuracy

Guidance is provided during boost by inertial navigation systems, later supplemented by stellar or GPS updates. Accuracy is measured by circular error probable (CEP), which for modern ICBMs may be on the order of a few hundred metres or less. Advanced missiles carry multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles (MIRVs).
