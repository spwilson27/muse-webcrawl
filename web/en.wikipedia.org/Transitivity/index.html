Transitivity

Transitivity is a term used in several fields to describe a property in which a relation or process carries across connected elements.

Mathematics and logic

In mathematics, a binary relation R is transitive if, whenever an element a is related to b, and b is related to c, then a is also related to c. Equality and inequality (for example "is greater than") are transitive relations, while "is a friend of" is not necessarily transitive. Transitive relations underlie equivalence relations and partial orders.

Grammar

In linguistics, transitivity concerns whether a verb takes a direct object: transitive verbs (such as "kick" in "she kicked the ball") require one, while intransitive verbs do not. Some grammars analyse transitivity as a broader property of clauses, involving participants and the effectiveness of an action.

Other uses

In economics, preferences are called transitive if preferring A to B and B to C implies preferring A to C, an assumption central to rational choice theory. Group actions in mathematics are also called transitive when any element can be carried to any other.
