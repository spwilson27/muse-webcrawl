Ancient Greek religion comprises the beliefs, rituals and mythology of the ancient Greek world. It had no scripture, creed or professional priesthood in the modern sense: religious practice was woven into the life of the city-state (polis), the household and the calendar of festivals, and centred on honouring the gods — above all the twelve Olympians headed by Zeus — through prayer, sacrifice and votive offerings in exchange for divine favour.

Beliefs

The Greek gods were anthropomorphic, powerful and immortal, but not omnipotent or wholly benevolent: they quarrelled, deceived and intervened in human affairs, and even they were subject to fate (moira). Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BC) gave the standard account of the gods' genealogy, from Chaos and Gaia through the Titans to the Olympians. Belief also encompassed heroes — semi-divine mortals such as Heracles and Achilles, worshipped at local shrines — and countless local nymphs, river gods and daimones. The afterlife was generally shadowy: the dead went to Hades, with little expectation of reward or punishment except for the exceptionally famous or wicked, though mystery cults promised initiates a better lot.

Practices

The central act of worship was sacrifice: animals (or, for poorer worshippers, cakes, fruit or libations of wine) were offered at an outdoor altar, the god's portions burned and the meat shared in a communal feast. Sanctuaries ranged from simple altars and sacred groves to monumental temple complexes; the temple housed the cult image and treasury rather than a congregation. Cities maintained festival calendars — Athens' Panathenaea, with its procession and peplos for Athena, is the best known — and the great Panhellenic sanctuaries hosted the Olympic, Pythian, Nemean and Isthmian games. Oracles, most famously Apollo's at Delphi, were consulted on public and private decisions, and the Mysteries at Eleusis, promising initiates insight into death and rebirth, drew participants from across the Greek and Roman worlds.

Development and decline

Greek religion grew from Bronze Age and Near Eastern influences, absorbed local cults as cities formed, and was given Panhellenic shape by the Homeric and Hesiodic poems. Philosophers from Xenophanes onward criticised anthropomorphic mythology, and Hellenistic rulers added ruler cults and imported deities such as Isis and Serapis. Under the Roman Empire the traditional cults coexisted with Christianity for centuries, until Christian emperors suppressed sacrifice and the games in the 4th century AD and closed the last temples.
