Indirect realism

Indirect realism (also called representative realism or epistemological dualism) is the philosophical view that our sensory experience is not of the external world itself, but of an internal representation of it — variously described as sense data, ideas or percepts — which is caused by, and usually (on the realist assumption) corresponds to, external objects. It contrasts with direct realism (naïve realism), which holds that the objects of perception are the external objects themselves, and with idealism, which denies that experience corresponds to a mind-independent material world.

History

Versions of the view appear in Descartes and were developed by John Locke, who distinguished primary qualities (such as shape and motion) that resemble properties of objects from secondary qualities (colour, taste, sound) that exist only as powers to produce ideas. In the 20th century the position was reformulated in terms of "sense-data" by Bertrand Russell, G. E. Moore, H. H. Price and A. J. Ayer, partly in response to the argument from illusion: if a straight stick looks bent in water, what is directly seen cannot simply be the stick as it is.

Criticism

Critics, including J. L. Austin and Gilbert Ryle, objected that sense data are theoretical fictions and that indirect realism interposes a "veil of perception" between mind and world, inviting scepticism about how the external world could ever be known. Defenders reply that the causal and scientific facts about perception — time lags, perceptual constancy failures, dreams and hallucinations — make some mediating representation unavoidable, and that representation need not amount to a veil.
