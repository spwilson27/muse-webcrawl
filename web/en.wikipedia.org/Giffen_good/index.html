Giffen good

A Giffen good has an upward-sloping demand curve, defying the law of demand.

In microeconomics, a Giffen good is a product that people consume more of as the price rises and less of as the price falls, violating the basic law of demand. For a Giffen good, the income effect of a price change must outweigh the substitution effect.

Conditions

Three conditions are necessary: the good must be an inferior good; it must constitute a substantial share of the consumer's budget; and there must be a lack of close substitute goods. The classic candidates are staple foods among very poor consumers — if the price of bread rises, a family may no longer afford meat and be forced to buy still more bread.

History and evidence

The concept is named after the Scottish economist Sir Robert Giffen, to whom Alfred Marshall attributed the idea in his Principles of Economics (1895). Marshall's example — wheat bread in the diet of the poorest labouring class — has never been convincingly verified empirically. Modern research, including a widely cited study of rice consumption among poor households in China's Hunan province, has found some of the best field evidence for Giffen behaviour, though the phenomenon remains rare.

Distinction from Veblen goods

Giffen goods are often confused with Veblen goods, for which demand rises with price because high price confers status. The mechanisms differ: Giffen behaviour is driven by poverty and income effects, not prestige.
