Thalidomide

Thalidomide is a drug first marketed in the late 1950s as a sedative and treatment for morning sickness in pregnancy. It was withdrawn in the early 1960s after it was found to cause severe birth defects. It later returned to medical use under strict controls for leprosy complications and multiple myeloma.

Thalidomide
Trade names: Contergan, Thalomid
Class: Immunomodulatory imide drug
Formula: C₁₃H₁₀N₂O₄
Status: Strictly controlled prescription

History

Developed by Chemie Grünenthal in West Germany and sold from 1957, thalidomide was promoted as safe. By 1961 physicians such as William McBride and Widukind Lenz linked it to phocomelia — malformed limbs — and other defects in thousands of babies worldwide. In the United States, FDA reviewer Frances Kelsey withheld approval, limiting exposure there. The disaster transformed drug regulation, requiring proof of safety and efficacy and teratogenicity testing.

Mechanism

Thalidomide is chiral and interconverts in the body, modulates tumour necrosis factor-alpha and binds cereblon, affecting protein degradation. Its teratogenicity is linked to anti-angiogenic effects and cereblon binding in development.

Later uses

Under programmes such as STEPS / REMS with pregnancy prevention, thalidomide and analogues lenalidomide and pomalidomide treat erythema nodosum leprosum and multiple myeloma.
