Australian residential architectural styles

Australian residential architectural styles have evolved significantly over time, from the early days of the colony, with adaptations for the Australian climate and landscape, through the flowering of early twentieth-century movements, to the post-war suburban expansion and contemporary designs. Distinctive adaptations include the wide verandah, used for shade and outdoor living.

Early and Victorian eras

Colonial Georgian cottages of the early 1800s were followed by Victorian styles — Regency, Filigree terraces with cast-iron lacework, Italianate villas and Gothic Revival houses — and by the distinctive Queenslander: a timber house raised on stumps with broad verandahs, developed for the subtropical north. The Federation era (c. 1890–1915) produced the Federation Bungalow and Queen Anne styles in red brick with ornate timber fretwork.

Twentieth century

Interwar styles included the Californian Bungalow, Spanish Mission, Old English and Art Deco. After the Second World War, austerity and mass suburbanisation brought the triple-fronted brick-veneer house, the fibrous-cement ("fibro") cottage, and project homes. Modernist architects such as Harry Seidler introduced International Style houses, while the Melbourne and Sydney regional schools responded to local landscapes with bush-integrated designs.

Contemporary

Recent decades have seen large project-home estates, the "McMansion" phenomenon, medium-density infill, and renewed interest in passive solar design and sustainability suited to Australia's varied climates.
