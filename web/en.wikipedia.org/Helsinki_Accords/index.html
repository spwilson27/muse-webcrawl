The Helsinki Accords, also known as the Helsinki Final Act, were the concluding document of the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe, signed on 1 August 1975 in Helsinki, Finland, by 35 participating states. The accords sought to improve relations between the Communist bloc and the West during the Cold War détente.

Contents

The Final Act was organised into "baskets". The first basket covered security in Europe, including ten principles guiding relations between states — among them sovereign equality, refraining from the threat or use of force, the inviolability of frontiers, and, crucially, respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms. The second basket addressed economic, scientific, and environmental cooperation; the third basket dealt with humanitarian issues such as family reunification, travel, and the freer movement of information.

Significance

Although not a legally binding treaty, the accords had major political consequences. The Soviet Union welcomed the recognition of post-war borders, but the human-rights provisions gave dissident movements in Eastern Europe — such as Moscow Helsinki Group, Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia and Solidarity in Poland — a recognised standard by which to hold their governments to account. Historians widely credit this "Helsinki process" with helping to undermine Communist rule in Europe. The CSCE later became the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) in 1995.
