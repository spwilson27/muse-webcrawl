Statistical graphics

Statistical graphics, also known as statistical graphical techniques, are graphics used in the field of statistics for data visualization. Well-designed statistical graphics present complex data with clarity, precision, and efficiency, allowing patterns, trends, and outliers to be seen at a glance; poorly designed graphics can distort or obscure the data.

History

Scottish engineer and economist William Playfair pioneered many standard chart forms in his Commercial and Political Atlas (1786) and Statistical Breviary (1801), introducing the line chart, bar chart, pie chart, and circle graph. In the 1850s Florence Nightingale used her "coxcomb" (polar area) diagram to persuade the British government to improve sanitary conditions in military hospitals. Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 map of Napoleon's disastrous 1812 Russian campaign is often cited — notably by Edward Tufte — as perhaps the best statistical graphic ever drawn.

Types

Common statistical graphics include scatter plots, histograms, bar charts, box plots, line graphs, pie charts, heat maps, and choropleth maps. Exploratory data analysis, as developed by John Tukey, relies heavily on graphics such as the stem-and-leaf plot and box plot to reveal the structure of data before formal modelling.

Design principles

Edward Tufte's work, beginning with The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983), codified principles such as maximizing the data-ink ratio, avoiding "chartjunk," and showing data variation rather than design variation. Jacques Bertin's Semiology of Graphics (1967) provided an earlier systematic theory of visual variables such as position, size, shape, and colour.
