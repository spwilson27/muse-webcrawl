Persistent organic pollutant

Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) are organic compounds that resist environmental degradation through chemical, biological and photolytic processes. Because they persist for long periods, bioaccumulate in fatty tissue, and can be transported long distances, they have become global contaminants found even in remote regions such as the Arctic. Many POPs are or were used as pesticides or industrial chemicals, and several are unintentional by-products of combustion and industrial processes.

Properties and effects

POPs are typically halogenated, lipophilic compounds with low water solubility. They accumulate up food chains, reaching high concentrations in top predators and in human tissues, where exposure has been linked to cancers, reproductive and developmental disorders, immune suppression and endocrine disruption.

Stockholm Convention

The Stockholm Convention, adopted in 2001 and in force since 2004, requires parties to eliminate or restrict listed POPs. The original twelve substances — the "dirty dozen" — included the pesticides aldrin, chlordane, DDT, dieldrin, endrin, heptachlor, hexachlorobenzene, mirex and toxaphene, the industrial chemicals polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), and the by-products dioxins and furans. Additional substances, such as PFOS, lindane and endosulfan, have since been added as science and monitoring identified further candidates.

Sources

Sources include obsolete pesticide stockpiles, electrical equipment containing PCBs, waste incineration and open burning, and contaminated industrial sites. Long-range atmospheric transport — the "grasshopper effect" of repeated volatilisation and deposition — carries POPs toward colder latitudes, where they condense and accumulate.
