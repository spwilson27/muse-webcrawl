Geology of the Moon

The lunar surface divides into dark basaltic maria and bright anorthositic highlands.

The geology of the Moon (sometimes called selenology) is the study of the Moon's structure, composition and history. It differs markedly from Earth's geology: the Moon lacks a significant atmosphere, hydrosphere and plate tectonics, so its surface preserves a record of impacts spanning billions of years. Knowledge comes from telescopic observation, orbiting spacecraft, and samples returned by the Apollo and Luna missions and by robotic landers.

Surface features

Two terrain types dominate. The bright lunar highlands are heavily cratered and composed largely of anorthosite, a rock rich in plagioclase feldspar thought to have crystallised from an early magma ocean. The dark maria are vast plains of basaltic lava that flooded giant impact basins mainly between about 3.9 and 3.0 billion years ago. A blanket of fragmented rock and dust, the regolith, covers nearly the entire surface.

Internal structure

Seismic data from Apollo instruments indicate a crust averaging roughly 50 km thick, a deep mantle, and a small, partly molten iron-rich core a few hundred kilometres in radius. The Moon's low average density (3.34 g/cm³) reflects its depletion in iron and volatile elements compared with Earth.

Formation

The leading explanation is the giant-impact hypothesis: a Mars-sized body struck the early Earth about 4.5 billion years ago, and the ejected material accreted into the Moon. Isotopic similarities between terrestrial and lunar rocks support this model.
