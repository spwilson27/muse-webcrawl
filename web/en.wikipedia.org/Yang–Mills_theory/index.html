Yang–Mills theory

FieldQuantum field theory, gauge theory
Proposed1954, by Chen Ning Yang and Robert Mills
Based onNon-abelian Lie groups (e.g. SU(2), SU(3))
ApplicationsQuantum chromodynamics, electroweak theory

Yang–Mills theory is a gauge theory based on a non-abelian Lie group, generalizing James Clerk Maxwell's electromagnetism — which is based on the abelian group U(1) — to groups such as SU(2) and SU(3). Proposed by physicists Chen Ning Yang and Robert Mills in 1954, it provides the mathematical framework underlying the Standard Model of particle physics: quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong interaction, is a Yang–Mills theory with gauge group SU(3), and the electroweak interaction is built on SU(2) × U(1).

Theory

In Yang–Mills theory, the field strength tensor Fμν includes terms quadratic in the gauge potential, reflecting the fact that the force-carrying gauge bosons themselves carry the charge of the interaction and therefore interact with one another — gluons, unlike photons, interact among themselves. This self-interaction underlies asymptotic freedom, discovered in 1973, whereby the strong coupling weakens at short distances, and is believed to cause confinement, the permanent binding of quarks inside hadrons.

Mathematics and open problems

Yang–Mills theory has deeply influenced geometry and topology: instantons and Donaldson's work on four-manifolds derive from it. The Clay Mathematics Institute lists "Yang–Mills existence and mass gap" as one of the Millennium Prize Problems: to prove rigorously that a quantum Yang–Mills theory exists and that its spectrum has a mass gap (a positive minimum energy above the vacuum).

Classical theory

The source-free Yang–Mills equations are nonlinear generalizations of Maxwell's equations. Their solutions include monopoles and instantons central to modern mathematical physics.
