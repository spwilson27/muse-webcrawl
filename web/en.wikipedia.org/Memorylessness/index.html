Memorylessness

In probability and statistics, memorylessness is a property of certain probability distributions in which the conditional probability of an event occurring in the future, given that it has not yet occurred, is independent of how much time has already elapsed. A distribution is memoryless if, for a random variable X representing waiting time, P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t) for all s, t ≥ 0.

Memoryless distributions

The only continuous probability distributions that are memoryless are the exponential distributions; among discrete distributions, only the geometric distributions have this property. This uniqueness is why the exponential distribution is central to the theory of Poisson processes and queuing theory: the time until the next event does not depend on how long one has already waited.

Markov property

Memorylessness is closely related to the Markov property of stochastic processes: a Markov process is one whose future depends only on its present state, not on its history. The exponential and geometric distributions' lack of memory makes them the natural building blocks of Markov models.

Interpretation

A common intuition is the waiting time for a bus that arrives as a Poisson process: having waited ten minutes does not make the bus "due"; the remaining wait has the same distribution as at the start. By contrast, lifetimes of physical components usually are not memoryless, because wear makes failure more likely with age (hazard rates that increase over time).
