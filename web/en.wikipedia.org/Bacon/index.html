Bacon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bacon is a type of salt-cured pork made from various cuts, typically the belly or less fatty parts of the back. It is eaten on its own, as a side dish, or used as a flavouring ingredient in dishes. The meat is cured with salt, nitrates or nitrites, and often smoked, giving bacon its characteristic pink colour, savoury flavour, and long shelf life.

Types and cuts

Different countries favour different cuts. In the United States, "streaky" bacon from the pork belly is most common; in the United Kingdom and Ireland, back bacon — a leaner cut combining the loin and belly — is the standard for breakfasts. Canadian "peameal" bacon is a wet-cured boneless loin rolled in cornmeal, while Italian pancetta is an unsmoked belly bacon seasoned with herbs and spices. Turkey, chicken, and vegetarian bacon substitutes are produced for consumers who avoid pork.

Curing and smoking

Traditional curing rubs the meat with salt and curing salts, then hangs or smokes it; modern industrial production typically injects or immerses the meat in brine and may use liquid smoke. Sodium nitrite inhibits botulism and fixes the pink colour, though "uncured" products cured with celery-derived nitrates are also marketed. After curing, bacon is usually sliced into rashers and sold raw, to be cooked by frying, grilling, or baking.

Uses and nutrition

Bacon is a component of the full English breakfast, the BLT and club sandwiches, and dishes such as spaghetti alla carbonara (traditionally with guanciale) and bacon-wrapped foods. It is high in fat and sodium, and processed red meat including bacon was classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer in 2015 as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) on the basis of its association with colorectal cancer. Despite this, bacon remains culturally iconic, inspiring festivals, novelty products, and a wide range of bacon-flavoured goods.
