Iridium is a chemical element with the symbol Ir and atomic number 77. A very hard, silvery-white transition metal of the platinum group, iridium is considered the second-densest naturally occurring metal (after osmium), with a density of 22.56 g/cm³, and it is the most corrosion-resistant metal known, even at temperatures as high as 2,000 °C.

Discovery and properties

Iridium was discovered in 1803 among insoluble impurities in natural platinum by the English chemist Smithson Tennant, who named it after Iris, the Greek goddess of the rainbow, because of the varied colours of its salts. The metal is extremely refractory and brittle, making it difficult to machine; it resists attack by acids, including aqua regia, and by molten metals.

Occurrence and uses

Iridium is one of the rarest elements in Earth's crust — a scarcity that makes its enrichment in the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary clay layer, where iridium is far more abundant than in ordinary crust, a key piece of evidence for the asteroid impact that caused the end-Cretaceous mass extinction 66 million years ago. Industrially, iridium is used in spark plug electrodes, crucibles for crystal growth, catalysts, electrical contacts and, alloyed with osmium, in fountain-pen nib tips; the international prototype kilogram was made of platinum–iridium alloy. Radioactive iridium-192 is used in industrial radiography and brachytherapy.
