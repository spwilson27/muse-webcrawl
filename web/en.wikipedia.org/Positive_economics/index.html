Positive economics

Positive economics is the branch of economics that concerns the description, quantification, and explanation of economic phenomena. It focuses on facts and cause-and-effect relationships — on what is — rather than on value judgments about what ought to be, which are the domain of normative economics.

Definition

Positive economic statements are statements of fact that can, in principle, be tested against evidence: for example, "an increase in the minimum wage increases unemployment among teenagers" is a positive statement whether or not it is true, because it can be checked empirically. By contrast, "the minimum wage ought to be raised" is a normative statement expressing a value judgment.

History

The distinction was developed by John Neville Keynes in The Scope and Method of Political Economy (1891), which separated a "positive science" of economics from a "normative or regulative science" and an "art" of policy. Lionel Robbins's Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science (1932) reinforced the division. Milton Friedman's influential 1953 essay "The Methodology of Positive Economics" argued that a theory should be judged by its predictive accuracy rather than by the realism of its assumptions.

Criticism

Critics argue that the separation between positive and normative economics cannot be maintained perfectly: the choice of questions, concepts, and measurements in economic research may itself embed values, and apparently factual claims can carry implicit normative weight. Philosophers of economics such as Daniel Hausman have examined the entanglement of facts and values in economic methodology.
