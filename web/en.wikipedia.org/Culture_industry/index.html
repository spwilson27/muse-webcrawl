Culture industry

The term culture industry (German: Kulturindustrie) was coined by the critical theorists Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer of the Frankfurt School. It refers to the industrial production and mass distribution of cultural goods — films, radio programs, magazines, popular music — under capitalism, which Adorno and Horkheimer argued standardizes culture and pacifies audiences.

Dialectic of Enlightenment

The concept was introduced in the chapter "The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception" of Dialectic of Enlightenment, written during the authors' exile in the United States and published in 1947. Having witnessed both Nazi propaganda and Hollywood entertainment, they argued that mass culture under monopoly capitalism produces pseudo-individuality: products appear varied while following identical formulas.

Critique

For Adorno, amusement under late capitalism prolongs work: entertainment reconciles people to the existing order and erodes autonomous art. Later cultural studies scholars, such as Stuart Hall, criticized the thesis as overly pessimistic and elitist, emphasizing that audiences actively interpret and resist media messages. The concept nevertheless remains influential in media studies and discussions of streaming platforms, blockbuster franchises, and algorithmic recommendation.
