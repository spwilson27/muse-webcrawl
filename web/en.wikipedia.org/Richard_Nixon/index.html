Richard Milhous Nixon (9 January 1913 – 22 April 1994) was the 37th president of the United States, serving from 1969 until his resignation in 1974 — the only U.S. president to resign the office. A Republican, he had previously served as Dwight D. Eisenhower's vice president and as a congressman and senator from California.

Early political career

Born in Yorba Linda, California, Nixon practised law, served in the U.S. Navy during the Second World War, and was elected to the House of Representatives in 1946. He gained national prominence through the Alger Hiss case and won a Senate seat in 1950. As vice president (1953–1961) he survived a campaign-fund scandal with the televised "Checkers speech". Nixon narrowly lost the 1960 presidential election to John F. Kennedy and the 1962 California governorship, but staged a comeback to win the presidency in 1968.

Presidency

Domestically, Nixon's administration created the Environmental Protection Agency, signed the Clean Air Act and OSHA, ended the military draft and implemented the "Southern strategy". In foreign policy he pursued détente with the Soviet Union (including the SALT I treaty), opened relations with China with his historic 1972 visit, and negotiated the Paris Peace Accords (1973) ending direct U.S. involvement in the Vietnam War, even as he had expanded the war into Cambodia and Laos.

Watergate and resignation

The break-in at the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate complex in June 1972, and the White House cover-up that followed, unravelled Nixon's second term. The Senate Watergate Committee hearings, the "Saturday Night Massacre" and the Supreme Court's order in United States v. Nixon to surrender tape recordings destroyed his support. Facing certain impeachment, Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974. His successor, Gerald Ford, pardoned him the following month.

Later life and legacy

Nixon spent his later years writing memoirs and books on foreign policy and gradually re-emerged as an elder statesman, consulted by subsequent presidents. He died in 1994. His legacy remains contested: credited with major foreign-policy achievements and significant domestic reforms, but indelibly associated with Watergate and the expansion of presidential scandal denoted by the "-gate" suffix.
