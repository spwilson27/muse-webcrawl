Andromeda Galaxy

Designations: Messier 31, M31, NGC 224

Constellation: Andromeda

Type: Spiral galaxy (SA(s)b)

Distance: 2.537 million light-years

Diameter: ~220,000 light-years

Stars: ~1 trillion

The Andromeda Galaxy is a barred spiral galaxy approximately 2.5 million light-years from Earth and the nearest major galaxy to the Milky Way. Visible to the naked eye as a faint smudge in the constellation Andromeda, it was catalogued by Charles Messier as M31. Persian astronomer Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi described it in 964 CE as a "Little Cloud".

History of observation

Its nature was debated for centuries. In the 1920s Edwin Hubble identified Cepheid variable stars in Andromeda, proving it lay far beyond the Milky Way and settling the "Great Debate" over whether spiral nebulae were island universes. Vesto Slipher had earlier measured its blueshift: Andromeda approaches the Milky Way at roughly 110 km/s.

Structure

Andromeda is the largest member of the Local Group, spanning about 220,000 light-years with an estimated one trillion stars, roughly double the Milky Way's mass in stars. It hosts a double nucleus, prominent dust lanes, satellite galaxies including M32 and M110, and a supermassive black hole of about 100 million solar masses.

Future collision

In roughly 4.5 billion years Andromeda and the Milky Way are expected to merge, forming a giant elliptical galaxy sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda". Because of the vast distances between stars, few stellar collisions are expected during the merger.
