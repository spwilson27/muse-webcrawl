Mausoleum

EtymologyFrom the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, tomb of Mausolus
FunctionAbove-ground tomb containing burial chambers or crypts

A mausoleum is an external free-standing building constructed as a monument enclosing the interment space or burial chamber of a deceased person or people. A mausoleum may be considered a type of tomb, or a tomb may be considered within a mausoleum. Many mausoleums contain crypts with niches for caskets stacked above the ground, while others are small private family structures in cemeteries.

Etymology and origin

The word derives from the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus in present-day Bodrum, Turkey — the vast tomb built around 350 BC for Mausolus, a satrap of the Persian Empire, and his wife-sister Artemisia II of Caria. Counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, it was so famous that "mausoleum" became the generic word for grand tombs of all kinds.

History

Monumental tombs predate the term: the Egyptian pyramids and the Roman mausolea of Augustus and Hadrian (now Castel Sant'Angelo) are early examples. Islamic architecture produced celebrated mausolea such as the Taj Mahal at Agra, built by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan for his wife Mumtaz Mahal. In the 19th century, garden cemeteries in Europe and North America popularized smaller family mausolea. The 20th century saw politically charged mausolea for revolutionary leaders, including Lenin's Mausoleum in Moscow, the mausolea of Mao Zedong in Beijing and of Ho Chi Minh in Hanoi, and the Kumsusan Palace of the Sun in Pyongyang.

Modern use

Community mausolea offer above-ground entombment as an alternative to earth burial, partly because they require less land and are often cheaper than burial plots with monuments. In some jurisdictions, private mausolea on family land remain a marker of wealth.
