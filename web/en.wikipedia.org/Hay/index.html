Hay

Hay is grass, legumes, or other herbaceous plants that have been cut and dried to be stored for use as animal fodder, either grazed by livestock directly from the field or fed to them, particularly for large grazing animals raised as livestock, such as cattle, horses, goats, and sheep. However, it is also fed to smaller domesticated animals such as rabbits and guinea pigs. Pigs may also be fed hay, though they digest it far less efficiently than ruminants.

Composition and making

Good hay is made from leafy forage crops cut before they are fully mature, including grasses such as timothy, ryegrass, orchard grass and brome, and legumes such as alfalfa (lucerne) and clover. Quality hay is judged by its leafiness, green color, sweet smell, and freedom from weeds, dust and mold. Plants intended for hay are cut and left to dry in the field (curing), raked or tedded to aid drying, and then collected — traditionally into haystacks or haycocks, and since the mechanization of agriculture, into bales produced by balers: small rectangular bales, or the large round and rectangular bales now typical.

Hay that is baled too wet can mold and can heat by microbial action and spontaneous combustion, a known cause of barn fires. Hay with a moisture content below roughly 15–20% is generally considered safe to store. Wet-harvested forage is instead commonly preserved as silage or haylage.

Use

Hay provides the bulk of winter feed for livestock in temperate climates where grass does not grow year-round, and supplements pasture during drought. Horses typically consume hay as the mainstay of their diet, and their hay is selected with particular care, as moldy or dusty hay can cause respiratory disease (heaves) and colic. The nutritional value of hay varies with plant species, stage of growth at cutting, and storage; legume hays such as alfalfa are richer in protein and calcium than grass hays.

The phrase "make hay while the sun shines" reflects the dependence of haymaking on dry weather. Historically, hay meadows were among the most valuable agricultural land, and haymaking was a central labor of the summer calendar.
