David I of Scotland

David I (c. 1084 – 24 May 1153), known in Gaelic as Dabíd mac Maíl Choluim, was King of Scots from 1124 until his death. The youngest son of Malcolm III and Saint Margaret of Scotland, he spent much of his youth at the Norman-influenced court of Henry I of England, whose ward and brother-in-law he became.

Before his accession David ruled southern Scotland as Prince of the Cumbrians and held the great English honour of Huntingdon through his marriage to Maud, Countess of Huntingdon.

Reign

David's reign transformed Scotland. He introduced feudal landholding, granting estates to Anglo-Norman knights; founded royal burghs such as Edinburgh, Perth, and Berwick; reformed coinage; and reorganized the church, founding dioceses and monasteries of the new orders — Melrose, Holyrood, and Dryburgh among them. These changes, sometimes called the "Davidian Revolution," drew Scotland closer to the mainstream of European civilization, though they also provoked resistance in Moray and Galloway.

The Anarchy

Bound by oath to support his niece, the Empress Matilda, David invaded northern England during the civil war against King Stephen. His army was defeated at the Battle of the Standard in 1138, but he nonetheless retained control of much of the north, including Cumberland and Northumberland, for the rest of his reign. He died at Carlisle in 1153 and was succeeded by his grandson Malcolm IV.
