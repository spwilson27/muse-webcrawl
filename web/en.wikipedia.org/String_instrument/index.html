String instrument

String instruments — also called stringed instruments or chordophones — are musical instruments that produce sound from vibrating strings when the performer plays or sounds them in some manner. In the Hornbostel–Sachs classification, chordophones form one of the main families of instruments, divided into lutes, harps, lyres, and zithers.

Playing techniques

Musicians play string instruments by plucking (pizzicato, fingerstyle, plectrum), bowing (with rosined horsehair), striking (as in the hammered dulcimer and piano), or occasionally by wind and sympathetic resonance (the aeolian harp). Pitch is determined by string length, tension, and mass, and players shorten strings with fingers or frets to change notes.

Major families

The Western orchestra's string section comprises violin, viola, cello, and double bass. Other widely played instruments include the guitar, banjo, mandolin, harp, ukulele, lute, sitar, koto, erhu, and piano — the last classed as a chordophone because its sound comes from struck strings. Electric variants amplify the vibration with pickups.

History

The earliest stringed instruments were lyres and harps of ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt; bowed instruments emerged in Central Asia around the first millennium CE and spread along trade routes, evolving into the European violin family perfected by Cremonese makers such as Antonio Stradivari in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.
