Lubusz Land

Lubusz Land (Polish: Ziemia lubuska; German: Land Lebus) is a historical region in western Poland and eastern Germany, on both sides of the middle Oder river. Its name derives from the medieval town of Lebus (Lubusz), once the seat of a bishopric. Today the greater part of the region lies in the Polish Lubusz Voivodeship, while a smaller western strip around the German town of Lebus lies in Brandenburg.

History

The territory was part of the early Polish state of the Piast dynasty and guarded the western approaches to Greater Poland. Around 1124–1125, Duke Bolesław III Wrymouth established the Diocese of Lebus, subordinate to the Archdiocese of Gniezno, to support the Christianisation of the area.

In the mid-13th century, under the pressure of the Margraves of Brandenburg, the region passed to the Ascanian margraves and was incorporated into the Neumark ("New March") of Brandenburg. The bishopric of Lebus survived, later moving its seat to Göritz and then Fürstenwalde.

After the Second World War, the Potsdam Agreement placed the lands east of the Oder–Neisse line under Polish administration, and the German population was expelled. The region was repopulated with Poles, including many displaced from Poland's former eastern territories.

Geography

The Lubusz Land is a lowland of lake districts and forests cut by the Oder and its tributaries, including the Warta's confluence near Kostrzyn. Principal towns include Gorzów Wielkopolski, Zielona Góra (just south of the historical core), Słubice and Frankfurt (Oder) across the river.
