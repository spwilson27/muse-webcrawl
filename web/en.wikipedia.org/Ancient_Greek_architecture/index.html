Ancient Greek architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ancient Greek architecture is the architecture produced by the Greek-speaking peoples of the ancient world, from around 900 BC until roughly the 1st century AD, with the earliest remaining works dating from about 600 BC. It is best known for its temples, built in stone in the Doric, Ionic and Corinthian orders, but it also encompassed theatres, stoas, gymnasia, bouleuteria, treasuries and fortifications.

Characteristics

Greek architecture is distinguished by its highly formalised structures, both in plan and elevation, governed by proportion, symmetry and optical refinements such as entasis and the slight curvature of horizontal surfaces. Buildings were typically constructed of limestone or marble using post-and-lintel (trabeated) construction. Sculpture was integral to temple design, filling pediments and metopes with mythological scenes.

The orders

  • Doric — the sturdiest and plainest order, with simple cushion capitals; exemplified by the Parthenon and the Temple of Hephaestus.
  • Ionic — slimmer and more decorative, with voluted capitals, originating in Ionia; seen on the Erechtheion and the Temple of Athena Nike.
  • Corinthian — the most ornate, with capitals carved as acanthus leaves, favoured in the Hellenistic and Roman periods.

Building types

Beyond temples, the Greeks developed the stoa (covered colonnade), the open-air theatre with its circular orchestra (as at Epidaurus), the odeon for music, the stadium, the gymnasium and palaestra for athletics and education, and civic structures such as the bouleuterion and prytaneion. Domestic architecture remained modest by comparison, organised around a central courtyard.

Influence

Greek forms were adopted and transformed by the Romans, revived in the Renaissance, and codified again in the Neoclassical and Greek Revival movements of the 18th and 19th centuries, shaping public architecture across Europe and North America. Roman architect Vitruvius's treatise De architectura preserved much Greek theory for later ages.
