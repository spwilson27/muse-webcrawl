Irish traditional music (also known as Irish trad, Irish folk music or simply trad) is a genre of folk music that developed in Ireland and is practised today in Ireland, in the Irish diaspora and worldwide. It encompasses dance music — reels, jigs, hornpipes, polkas and slides — slow airs, and a singing tradition that includes both lively songs and the unaccompanied, highly ornamented sean-nós ("old style") singing.

Instruments and style

The core melodic instruments are the fiddle, wooden flute, tin whistle, uilleann pipes (Ireland's bellows-blown bagpipes), button accordion and concertina, with accompaniment from guitar, bouzouki, piano and the bodhrán frame drum. Tunes are typically played in unison at sessions, learned by ear, and ornamented with rolls, crans, cuts and triplets. Regional fiddle styles — Donegal's driving bowing, Clare's lyricism, Sligo's virtuosity (exported to America by Michael Coleman in the 1920s) — remain influential.

History and revival

The tradition is rooted in centuries of dance and song, documented in collections from the eighteenth century onward (Edward Bunting transcribed harpers at the 1792 Belfast Harp Festival; Captain Francis O'Neill's Chicago-published collections preserved thousands of tunes). Emigration carried the music to America, where 78 rpm recordings fed back into Ireland. The founding of Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann in 1951, the Fleadh Cheoil competitions, Seán Ó Riada's Ceoltóirí Chualann in the 1960s, and groups such as The Chieftains, Planxty and The Bothy Band drove a revival that made Irish traditional music a global phenomenon, later amplified by shows like Riverdance (1994).
