Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki

On 6 and 9 August 1945, the United States detonated two atomic bombs over the Japanese cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, respectively. The bombings killed between 150,000 and 246,000 people, most of whom were civilians, and remain the only use of nuclear weapons in an armed conflict. Japan surrendered to the Allies on 15 August, six days after the bombing of Nagasaki, and formally signed the instrument of surrender on 2 September 1945, ending the Second World War.

Background

The Manhattan Project had developed two weapon designs by mid-1945. The Potsdam Declaration of 26 July 1945 called for Japan's unconditional surrender; its rejection led President Harry S. Truman to authorise the bombs' use. Hiroshima, a major army depot and embarkation port largely untouched by conventional bombing, was the first target; Kokura was the primary target for the second mission, but cloud and smoke diverted the B-29 Bockscar to Nagasaki.

The bombings

At 08:15 on 6 August, the B-29 Enola Gay dropped the 15-kiloton uranium bomb "Little Boy" over Hiroshima, destroying about 12 square kilometres of the city and killing roughly 70,000–80,000 people immediately, with comparable numbers dying later of injuries and radiation sickness. On 9 August, the 21-kiloton plutonium bomb "Fat Man" exploded over Nagasaki's Urakami Valley, killing roughly 40,000–75,000. Survivors, known as hibakusha, suffered burns, radiation illness, and elevated cancer rates, and faced social stigma.

Japanese surrender and debate

On 9 August the Soviet Union also declared war on Japan and invaded Manchuria. Emperor Hirohito intervened to accept the Potsdam terms; his surrender broadcast followed on 15 August. Historians continue to debate the bombings' necessity and ethics — whether they shortened the war and avoided a costly invasion, whether Soviet entry was the decisive factor, and whether alternatives such as a demonstration or modified surrender terms were feasible — as well as the morality of targeting cities.

Legacy

The bombings inaugurated the nuclear age, shaped the ensuing Cold War arms race, and made Hiroshima and Nagasaki global symbols of nuclear disarmament. The Hiroshima Peace Memorial (Genbaku Dome) is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
