Upright piano

An upright piano, also called a vertical piano, is a piano in which the strings and soundboard run vertically, with the hammers moving horizontally to strike them. This design makes the instrument much more compact than a grand piano, whose strings lie horizontally, and uprights are the most common type of piano in homes, schools, churches and practice rooms.

Design

In an upright, the frame, strings, dampers and action are arranged in a tall cabinet, typically 110–135 cm (43–53 in) high. When a key is pressed, the action lifts a hammer toward the strings; because the hammers move horizontally rather than upward, they rely on springs and tapes to return to rest, which gives most upright actions a slightly less responsive repetition than a grand action. The pedals usually comprise the damper (sustain) pedal, a soft pedal that shifts or shortens hammer travel, and often a practice (mute) pedal that interposes a strip of felt.

Types

Upright pianos are classified by height: spinet pianos (under about 97 cm) use an indirect "drop action"; console pianos (about 100–110 cm) have a direct action above the keys; studio pianos (about 110–115 cm) are common in institutions; and professional or upright grand models (115 cm and taller) have longer strings and larger soundboards, giving a tone approaching that of a small grand.

History

Vertical pianos appeared in the early 19th century, building on earlier upright harpsichord and piano designs. Robert Wornum's small cottage pianoforte of the 1820s and his tape-check action of 1842 established the basic form of the modern upright. By the late 19th century uprights had become standard domestic instruments in Europe and North America, and manufacturers such as Steinway & Sons, Yamaha and Kawai developed them further in the 20th century. Their sound is influenced by their shorter bass strings, which produce greater inharmonicity than the longer strings of a grand.
