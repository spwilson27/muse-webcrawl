Redistricting

Redistricting is the process of drawing electoral district boundaries. In the United States, redistricting occurs following each decennial census, as required by the Constitution, to reallocate seats in the House of Representatives among the states (reapportionment) and to redraw congressional and state legislative district lines so that districts contain roughly equal populations.

Process

In most US states, the state legislature draws the maps, subject to the governor's veto. Some states use independent or bipartisan redistricting commissions—notably California, Arizona, Michigan, and Colorado—intended to reduce partisan manipulation. Criteria typically include equal population (mandated by Supreme Court rulings such as Wesberry v. Sanders and Reynolds v. Sims), contiguity, compactness, respect for political subdivisions and communities of interest, and compliance with the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Gerrymandering

Redistricting can be manipulated to favour a party or group, a practice called gerrymandering, named after Governor Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts and an 1812 district shaped like a salamander. Common techniques include "packing" opposing voters into a few districts and "cracking" them across many districts to dilute their influence. Landmark litigation includes Shaw v. Reno (1993) on racial gerrymandering and Rucho v. Common Cause (2019), in which the Supreme Court held that partisan gerrymandering claims present nonjusticiable political questions in federal court, leaving remedies to Congress and state courts.

Around the world

Many other democracies assign boundary drawing to independent bodies—such as the UK Boundary Commissions, Elections Canada's boundary commissions, and Australia's electoral redistribution process—with the goal of insulating the process from partisan control.
