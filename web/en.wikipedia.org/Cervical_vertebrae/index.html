Cervical vertebrae

In tetrapods, cervical vertebrae (singular: vertebra) are the vertebrae of the neck, immediately below the skull. Trunk vertebrae (divided into thoracic and lumbar vertebrae in mammals) lie caudal (toward the tail) of cervical vertebrae. In sauropsid species, the cervical vertebrae bear cervical ribs. In lizards and saurischian dinosaurs, the cervical ribs are large; in birds, they are small and completely fused to the vertebrae. The cervical vertebrae of mammals are the only vertebrae that have a hole in the transverse processes, the transverse foramen; these foramina are for the passage of the vertebral artery, vein and sympathetic plexus.

Structure

A typical cervical vertebra (C3–C6) has a small, wide vertebral body; a large, roughly triangular vertebral foramen; bifid (split) spinous processes, except C7; and transverse processes pierced by transverse foramina that transmit the vertebral artery and vein. The uncovertebral joints (of Luschka) on the upper surface of the bodies are common sites of degenerative change causing neck pain and nerve compression.

Atlas and axis

The first cervical vertebra, the atlas (C1), lacks a body and spinous process; it is a ring with two lateral masses bearing concave facets that cradle the occipital condyles of the skull, permitting the nodding "yes" movement. Beneath it the axis (C2) bears the odontoid process (dens), a peg around which the atlas rotates to produce the shaking "no" movement. Fractures of the dens and of the atlas (Jefferson fracture) and of the axis pars interarticularis (hangman's fracture) are classic injuries.

C7: vertebra prominens

The seventh cervical vertebra has a long, non-bifid spinous process that is easily felt at the base of the neck — the vertebra prominens — a key surface-anatomy landmark. Its transverse foramina are small or absent and usually transmit only accessory veins.

Number across species

Almost all mammals have exactly seven cervical vertebrae, regardless of neck length — giraffes and mice alike; exceptions are manatees (six), two-toed sloths (five to seven) and three-toed sloths (eight or nine). Birds have many more, typically 13–25, contributing to their flexible necks.

Clinical significance

The neck is vulnerable to whiplash injury in vehicle collisions. Cervical spondylosis, disc herniation and spinal stenosis can compress the cord (cervical myelopathy) or nerve roots (radiculopathy). The vertebral artery's course through the transverse foramina makes it susceptible to injury in fractures and, rarely, to dissection.
