Narcissism is a personality trait characterised by an inflated sense of self-importance, a deep need for excessive attention and admiration, and a lack of empathy for others. The term originates from Greek mythology, in which the young Narcissus fell in love with his own image reflected in a pool of water. In everyday speech the word is used loosely for vanity and self-absorption; in psychology it describes a dimensional trait present to some degree in most people, and in extreme, rigid, and impairing form, the diagnosis of narcissistic personality disorder.

History of the concept

The concept was introduced into psychology by Havelock Ellis and Paul Näcke in the 1890s and developed by Sigmund Freud in his 1914 essay "On Narcissism", which distinguished healthy primary narcissism of infancy from pathological secondary narcissism. Heinz Kohut's self psychology and Otto Kernberg's object-relations account later offered influential, differing models of pathological narcissism.

Forms and assessment

Researchers commonly distinguish grandiose narcissism — extraverted, dominant, exhibitionistic — from vulnerable narcissism — hypersensitive, defensive, and resentful beneath a fragile self-image. The most widely used research measure is the Narcissistic Personality Inventory (NPI), developed by Raskin and Terry (1988). The DSM-5 lists narcissistic personality disorder among the Cluster B personality disorders, with an estimated prevalence of up to about 6% in community samples.

Culture

Christopher Lasch's The Culture of Narcissism (1979) popularised the idea that modern societies encourage narcissistic traits. Debates continue over whether narcissism is rising across generations and how it relates to social media use, self-esteem, and leadership.
