Catacombs

TypeUnderground burial galleries
Famous examplesCatacombs of Rome; Catacombs of Paris
OriginsLate antiquity (2nd–5th centuries AD, Rome)

Catacombs are man-made underground passageways used for religious practice, most notably burial. Any chamber used as a burial place is a catacomb, although the word is most closely associated with the Roman Empire's network of subterranean cemeteries. The term derives from the Latin catacumbae, the name of a particular district along Rome's Appian Way.

Catacombs of Rome

The catacombs of Rome are extensive networks of galleries cut into the soft volcanic tuff outside the ancient city walls, used by early Christians, Jews, and pagans from roughly the second to the fifth century AD. Bodies were placed in niches (loculi) sealed with stone or terracotta slabs, or in small chambers (cubicula). The best known are the catacombs of San Callisto (Callixtus), San Sebastiano, and Domitilla. Their walls preserve some of the earliest Christian art, including images of the Good Shepherd, Jonah, and the fish symbol.

Other catacombs

The Catacombs of Paris, in former limestone quarries under the city, became an ossuary in the late eighteenth century when the remains of some six million people were transferred there from overflowing cemeteries. Other notable catacombs include those of Naples (San Gennaro), Syracuse, Malta (St. Paul's), Alexandria (Kom El Shoqafa), and the catacombs beneath Vienna and Lima.

Use and significance

Catacombs served not only for burial but for commemoration of martyrs and the celebration of funerary rites. Popular imagination long pictured them as hiding places during persecutions, though historians note their locations were well known to the authorities. Today they are major archaeological sites and places of pilgrimage and tourism.
