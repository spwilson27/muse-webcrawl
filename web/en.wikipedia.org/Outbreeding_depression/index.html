Outbreeding depression

Outbreeding depression is a reduction in the biological fitness of offspring produced by mating between individuals from genetically distinct populations of the same species. It is the counterpart of inbreeding depression and of heterosis (hybrid vigour), which often occurs in the first generation.

Mechanisms

Two main mechanisms cause outbreeding depression. First, populations adapt to their local environments, so hybrids may be poorly matched to either parental habitat. Second, populations evolve co-adapted gene complexes; recombination in second and later hybrid generations breaks these up, producing unfit combinations — a form of hybrid breakdown related to Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities. In some cases the first generation (F1) is vigorous, but the F2 generation shows reduced survival or fertility.

Examples and significance

Classic cases come from conservation biology: the Tatra mountain ibex population collapsed after introductions of animals from other regions shifted the breeding season so calves were born in winter. Similar effects have been documented in fish, plants and invertebrates. Outbreeding depression is a key concern in genetic rescue, reintroduction programmes and the management of hybridising crops and wild relatives.
