Carbon fixation

Overview of the Calvin cycle, the main carbon fixation pathway (placeholder)

Carbon fixation is the process by which living organisms convert inorganic carbon, primarily atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2), into organic compounds. It is the first step of carbon assimilation and the basis of nearly all primary production on Earth. By far the most important pathway is photosynthesis in plants, algae, and cyanobacteria, in which the enzyme RuBisCO incorporates CO2 into ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate within the Calvin cycle. Global gross carbon fixation by photosynthesis is estimated at roughly 120 billion tonnes of carbon per year on land and a comparable amount in the oceans.

Pathways

Six autotrophic carbon fixation pathways are known in nature:

  • Calvin–Benson–Bassham (CBB) cycle — used by plants, algae, cyanobacteria, and many proteobacteria; includes the C4 and CAM variants that concentrate CO2 around RuBisCO to reduce photorespiration.
  • Reductive tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycle — a reversed Krebs cycle found in some bacteria such as Chlorobium.
  • Wood–Ljungdahl (reductive acetyl-CoA) pathway — used by acetogens and methanogens; thought to be among the most ancient pathways.
  • 3-Hydroxypropionate bicycle — in Chloroflexus and relatives.
  • 3-Hydroxypropionate/4-hydroxybutyrate cycle — in aerobic archaea.
  • Dicarboxylate/4-hydroxybutyrate cycle — in anaerobic archaea.

Significance

Carbon fixation is the entry point of carbon into the biosphere: heterotrophic organisms, including animals and humans, ultimately depend on fixed carbon for food. Fixed carbon stored in biomass, soils, and sediments also drives the long-term carbon cycle and influences atmospheric CO2 and climate. Artificial carbon fixation — engineered enzymes and synthetic pathways for capturing CO2 — is an active field of research.
