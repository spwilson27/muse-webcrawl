Acasta Gneiss

LocationSlave Craton, Northwest Territories, Canada
AgeUp to ~4.03 billion years (Hadean–Archean)
TypeTonalitic gneiss
Named forAcasta River

The Acasta Gneiss is a tonalite gneiss geological unit in the Slave craton of the Northwest Territories, Canada. Its outcrop lies on an island about 300 kilometres (190 mi) north of Yellowknife. The rock was first described in the 1980s and was dated in 1999 at up to 4.03 billion years old, making certain of its components the oldest known intact rock formations on Earth.

Age and significance

Radiometric dating (U–Pb dating of zircon crystals, notably by Samuel Bowring and colleagues) showed that the original igneous protolith crystallised about 4.03 billion years ago, in the Hadean eon, only about 500 million years after Earth formed. Later studies identified inherited zircon cores as old as roughly 4.2 billion years. The Acasta Gneiss thus preserves evidence of Earth's earliest crust, formed after the Moon-forming impact and the cooling of the magma ocean.

Geology

The unit consists mainly of tonalitic and granitic gneisses that have been strongly metamorphosed and deformed multiple times. It forms part of the Acasta Gneiss Complex, an area of about 13,000 square kilometres of ancient basement rocks. Claims of still older material elsewhere — such as the Nuvvuagittuq greenstone belt in Quebec, dated by some methods to 4.28 billion years — remain debated, since that material may not constitute intact rock in the same sense.
