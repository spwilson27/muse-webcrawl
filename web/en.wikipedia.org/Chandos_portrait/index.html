Chandos portrait


The Chandos portrait, artist unknown, c. 1600–1610. National Portrait Gallery, London.

The Chandos portrait is the most famous of the portraits that are believed to depict William Shakespeare (1564–1616). Painted between 1600 and 1610, it may have served as the basis for the engraved portrait of Shakespeare used in the First Folio in 1623. It is named after James Brydges, 1st Duke of Chandos, who owned the picture in the early 18th century. The portrait was given to the National Portrait Gallery in London on the gallery's foundation in 1856, and it remains the gallery's best-known image of the playwright.

Description

The portrait is a head-and-shoulders oil painting on canvas, showing a man in his forties with a beard, a loose white collar and a gold earring in his left ear. The sitter's dark, informal dress and unbuttoned collar contrast with the more formal court portraits of the period, and have often been read as suiting a man of the theatre.

Provenance

The picture's early history is uncertain. Tradition associates it with the actor Richard Burbage, Shakespeare's leading man, and with Joseph Taylor, another actor, who is said to have possessed it before it passed to the playwright and theatre manager William Davenant. It was later owned by Thomas Betterton, the leading actor of the Restoration, and after his death in 1710 was acquired by the Duke of Chandos.

Authenticity debate

Whether the portrait actually depicts Shakespeare has been debated for centuries. No portrait of Shakespeare painted from life is documented beyond doubt, and the Chandos portrait's chain of ownership contains gaps. In 2006 the National Portrait Gallery's exhibition Searching for Shakespeare examined the picture alongside other candidates such as the Flower portrait and the Cobbe portrait, concluding that the Chandos remains one of the most plausible likenesses, though not a certain one.
