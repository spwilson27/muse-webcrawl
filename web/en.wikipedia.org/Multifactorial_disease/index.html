Multifactorial disease

Multifactorial disease
CausesMultiple genetic variants plus environmental factors
ExamplesType 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, schizophrenia, neural tube defects

A multifactorial disease (also called a complex disease) is a condition caused by the combined effects of many genetic variants, each of small effect, interacting with environmental and lifestyle factors. Unlike single-gene (Mendelian) disorders, multifactorial diseases do not follow simple inheritance patterns, although they tend to cluster in families because relatives share both genes and environments.

Examples include type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease, hypertension, asthma, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, Alzheimer's disease, cleft lip and palate, and neural tube defects. The liability-threshold model explains such conditions: a continuously distributed underlying liability (genetic plus environmental) produces disease only when it exceeds a threshold.

Genetics

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified hundreds or thousands of loci contributing to individual complex diseases, each typically raising risk by only a few percent. Polygenic risk scores aggregate these small effects to estimate an individual's overall predisposition, though environmental modification remains central to prevention.

Prevention and management

Because environment is a major contributor, multifactorial diseases are often preventable or delayable by lifestyle measures — diet, exercise, avoiding smoking — and by screening people at elevated familial risk. Folate supplementation before pregnancy, for instance, greatly reduces the incidence of neural tube defects.
