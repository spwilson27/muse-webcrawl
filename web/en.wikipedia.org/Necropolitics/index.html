Necropolitics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Necropolitics is the use of social and political power to dictate how some people may live and how others must die. The term was coined by the Cameroonian historian and political theorist Achille Mbembe in his 2003 essay "Necropolitics" (published in Public Culture) and expanded in his book Necropolitics (2016; English translation 2019).

Concept

Building on Michel Foucault's notion of biopower—the management of life—Mbembe argued that biopolitics is insufficient to explain political formations in which the sovereign power to kill is central. Necropolitics examines how states and other actors create "death-worlds": conditions such as slavery, colonial occupation, apartheid, refugee camps and besieged territories in which populations are kept in a status of "living death".

Applications and debates

The concept has been applied to the transatlantic slave plantation, colonial warfare, the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, drone warfare, border regimes, and public-health policies that expose racialized populations to premature death. Scholars have connected necropolitics to work by Giorgio Agamben (Homo Sacer), Judith Butler's analyses of precarity and grievability, and Jasbir Puar's "debility". Critics debate its scope and its differences from biopolitics.

Reception

"Necropolitics" is among the most cited essays in contemporary political theory and postcolonial studies, and the concept is now used across anthropology, geography, sociology, and critical theory.
