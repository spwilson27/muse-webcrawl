Escheat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Escheat is a common law doctrine that operates as a default rule of property law: when a person dies without a will and without heirs, or when property is otherwise ownerless, ownership reverts to the state. The term derives from the Old French escheoir, "to fall to", reflecting feudal law, under which land held by a tenant who died without heirs — or who committed a felony — fell back to the lord of the fee, and ultimately to the Crown.

Historical feudal escheat

In medieval England, all land was held, directly or indirectly, of the Crown. If a tenant-in-chief died without an heir, or was attainted for treason, the land escheated to the lord. Escheat for felony (other than treason) went to the immediate lord, while escheat for treason went to the Crown. A royal official, the escheator, supervised inquiries ("inquisitions post mortem") into the lands of deceased tenants.

Modern law

With the decline of feudal tenure, escheat came to mean the passing of ownerless property to the state as bona vacantia ("vacant goods"). In modern practice:

  • Intestacy without heirs: the estate of a person dying intestate with no surviving relatives passes to the state or, in the United Kingdom, to the Crown or the Duchies of Lancaster and Cornwall.
  • Unclaimed property: in the United States, statutes in every state require banks, insurers, and companies to transfer long-dormant accounts, uncashed cheques, and abandoned safe-deposit contents to state custody — a process also called escheat, although owners or their heirs may usually reclaim the property at any time.
  • Dissolved corporations: assets of dissolved companies with no owner may escheat to the state.

Escheat should be distinguished from forfeiture and eminent domain: the state takes not by confiscation but because the property has no private owner.
