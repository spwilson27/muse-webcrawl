Benzene is an organic chemical compound with the molecular formula C6H6. Its molecule is composed of six carbon atoms joined in a planar hexagonal ring with one hydrogen atom attached to each carbon. Because it contains only carbon and hydrogen atoms, benzene is a hydrocarbon, and specifically the parent aromatic compound. Benzene is a colourless, highly flammable liquid with a sweet smell, present in crude oil and produced in large quantities by the petrochemical industry as a precursor to polymers, resins, and pharmaceuticals.

History

Michael Faraday first isolated benzene in 1825 from an oily residue of London illuminating gas. In 1865 Friedrich August Kekulé proposed the ring structure with alternating single and double bonds; the story that the idea came to him in a dream of a snake seizing its own tail (the ouroboros) is famous, whether or not literally true. The modern picture, confirmed by X-ray crystallography, is of a regular hexagon in which all C–C bonds are equal (139 pm) and the six π electrons are delocalised over the ring — the origin of benzene's aromatic stability and of its characteristic substitution rather than addition chemistry.

Production and uses

Benzene is produced mainly by catalytic reforming of naphtha and by steam cracking, with toluene hydrodealkylation as a supplementary route. About 60% of benzene output goes to ethylbenzene (for styrene and polystyrene), with much of the rest going to cumene (for phenol and acetone), cyclohexane (for nylon), and nitrobenzene (for aniline). Because of its toxicity, benzene's former uses as a solvent and as a gasoline additive have been curtailed, though it remains a natural component of crude oil and tobacco smoke and is present in gasoline at regulated low levels.

Health effects

Benzene is a known human carcinogen (IARC Group 1), associated with leukaemia and other blood disorders following chronic exposure; it also depresses the central nervous system in acute exposure. Occupational exposure limits have been progressively tightened (e.g. OSHA's permissible exposure limit of 1 ppm as an 8-hour average), and benzene in consumer products and ambient air is regulated in most countries.

Derivatives and aromaticity

Substituted benzenes include toluene, the xylenes, phenol, aniline, and chlorobenzene; fused-ring analogues include naphthalene and anthracene. Benzene is the archetype of aromaticity as formalised by Hückel's rule (4n+2 π electrons), and the benzene ring is the most common structural motif in organic chemistry and in approved drug molecules.
