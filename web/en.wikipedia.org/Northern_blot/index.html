The northern blot, or RNA blot, is a technique used in molecular biology research to study gene expression by detecting RNA (or isolated mRNA) in a sample. Developed in 1977 by James Alwine, David Kemp, and George Stark at Stanford University, its name is a pun on the earlier Southern blot for DNA, which was named after its inventor Edwin Southern.

Procedure

Total RNA or purified mRNA is separated by size using gel electrophoresis on a denaturing agarose gel, so that the single-stranded RNA molecules remain unfolded. The RNA is then transferred ("blotted") onto a nylon or nitrocellulose membrane and immobilised, often by UV crosslinking or baking. The membrane is incubated with a labelled probe — a DNA, RNA, or oligonucleotide sequence complementary to the transcript of interest — labelled radioactively or with a chemiluminescent tag. After washing away unbound probe, the signal is detected by autoradiography or imaging.

Applications

Northern blotting reveals both the abundance and the size of a transcript, allowing researchers to compare expression of a gene across tissues, developmental stages, or experimental treatments, and to detect alternative splicing products. Its disadvantages are its low throughput, relatively low sensitivity, and the use of hazardous radioactivity, so it has largely been supplemented by RT-PCR, microarrays, and RNA sequencing — though it remains a standard for validating transcript size.

Reverse northern blot

In a reverse northern blot, labelled RNA from a sample is used as the probe against known DNA sequences immobilised on a membrane — an arrangement related to the dot blot and early microarray formats.
