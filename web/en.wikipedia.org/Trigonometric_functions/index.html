In mathematics, the trigonometric functions (also called circular functions, angle functions or goniometric functions) are real functions which relate an angle of a right-angled triangle to ratios of two side lengths. They are widely used in all sciences related to geometry, such as navigation, solid mechanics, celestial mechanics, geodesy, and many others. They are among the simplest periodic functions, and as such are also widely used for studying periodic phenomena through Fourier analysis.

Definitions

In a right triangle with acute angle θ, sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse, cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse, and tan θ = opposite/adjacent. Extending beyond acute angles, the unit-circle definition takes a point at angle θ on the circle x² + y² = 1: its coordinates are (cos θ, sin θ). The remaining functions are reciprocals: secant (1/cos), cosecant (1/sin), and cotangent (1/tan). In calculus the angle is measured in radians, and the functions can be defined by power series or as solutions of differential equations, permitting extension to complex arguments via Euler's formula, eix = cos x + i sin x.

Identities and inverses

The functions obey a rich web of identities: the Pythagorean identities (sin²x + cos²x = 1), angle-sum and double-angle formulas, product-to-sum formulas, and the laws of sines and cosines for general triangles. Their (restricted) inverses — arcsine, arccosine, arctangent — return angles from ratios and are essential in solving triangles and in integration.

History

Trigonometry developed in Hellenistic astronomy (Hipparchus's chord tables, Ptolemy's Almagest) and in India, where the sine (from Sanskrit through Arabic jība, misread as jayb, "fold", and translated to Latin sinus) replaced the chord. Medieval Islamic mathematicians systematized all six functions; early modern Europe turned them from triangle ratios into analytic functions of a variable, a transformation completed by Euler in the 18th century.

Applications

Beyond surveying and navigation, trigonometric functions model periodic phenomena — waves, sound, alternating current, seasons — and underlie Fourier series and transforms, which decompose arbitrary signals into sinusoidal components.
