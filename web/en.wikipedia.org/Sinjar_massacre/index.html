The Sinjar massacre was the killing and abduction of thousands of Yazidis in the Sinjar district of northern Iraq in August 2014 by the Islamic State (IS). Beginning on 3 August 2014, IS fighters overran Sinjar town and surrounding villages after Kurdish Peshmerga forces withdrew. Men who refused to convert to Islam were executed, while women and girls were abducted and subjected to sexual slavery and boys were forcibly recruited — crimes that the United Nations later determined constituted genocide against the Yazidi people.

The siege of Mount Sinjar

Tens of thousands of Yazidis fled to the barren Mount Sinjar, where they were trapped without water, food, or shelter in extreme heat. Images of the stranded civilians prompted international action: the United States and allies airdropped supplies and began airstrikes against IS positions on 7–8 August 2014, while Kurdish forces from Syria (the YPG and PKK) opened a corridor from the mountain to Syria, evacuating many thousands.

Scale and aftermath

Estimates suggest between 2,000 and 5,000 Yazidis were killed and around 6,000–7,000 abducted, many of whom remained missing years later. Mass graves have been exhumed for evidence and identification. Sinjar town was retaken in November 2015, but the district remained devastated and contested, and much of its Yazidi population remains displaced. In 2023 the German parliament formally recognized the crimes as genocide, joining the UN, the United States, and other bodies; Yazidi survivor Nadia Murad received the 2018 Nobel Peace Prize for her advocacy.
