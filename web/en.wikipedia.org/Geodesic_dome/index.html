Geodesic dome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A geodesic dome is a spherical or partially spherical shell structure (lattice-shell) based on a geodesic polyhedron composed of a network of triangles. The triangles create a self-bracing framework that gives structural strength while using a minimum of material. The geodesic form efficiently distributes stress: the more complex the network of triangles, the greater the dome's strength for a given weight.

History

The first geodesic dome was designed by Walther Bauersfeld for a planetarium at the Carl Zeiss works in Jena, Germany, in 1926. The form was independently developed and popularised from the late 1940s by American engineer and designer R. Buckminster Fuller, who coined the term "geodesic" (from field experiments with Kenneth Snelson at Black Mountain College) and received US patents in 1954. Fuller's domes were promoted as efficient, lightweight, rapidly assembled shelters.

Famous examples

Notable geodesic domes include the United States Pavilion at Expo 67 in Montreal (now the Montreal Biosphere), designed by Fuller; Spaceship Earth at Epcot in Florida, a complete sphere 50 metres in diameter; the Eden Project biomes in Cornwall, England; and hundreds of DEW Line and other radomes built for radar installations. Geodesic domes have also been used for homes, greenhouses, emergency shelters and climbing frames.

Properties

Because a sphere encloses the greatest volume for a given surface area, domes are thermally efficient and have favourable aerodynamics. Their subdivided triangular geometry can be generated by subdividing the faces of an icosahedron (frequency 1v, 2v, 3v, and so on) and projecting onto a sphere. Disadvantages include awkward interior space near the shell, difficulty sealing the many joints against weather, and building-code complexities for residential use.
