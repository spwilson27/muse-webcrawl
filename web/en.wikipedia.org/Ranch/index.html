Ranch

PurposeRaising grazing livestock (cattle, sheep, horses)
RegionsWestern United States and Canada, Mexico, South America, Australia, South Africa
WorkersRanch hands, cowboys, wranglers, gauchos, stockmen

A ranch (from Spanish rancho/Mexican Spanish) is an area of land, including structures, given over primarily to ranching: the practice of raising grazing livestock such as cattle and sheep. The term is used most often in Mexico, the western United States and Canada, though similar operations exist elsewhere — estancias in much of South America and stations in Australia.

Overview

Ranches generally consist of large areas of privately owned or leased grazing land, ranch buildings including a ranch house, barns, corrals and bunkhouses, and herds managed by ranch hands, many working on horseback. Because ranch land is often arid or semi-arid, stocking rates are low and holdings can be enormous: the King Ranch in Texas covers some 825,000 acres, and Anna Creek Station in South Australia is the world's largest working cattle property.

History

Ranching spread through the Americas with Spanish colonization, which introduced cattle and horses to Mexico and the Southwest. After the American Civil War, the great cattle drives moved Texas longhorns north to railheads in Kansas, creating the cowboy culture of the open range. Barbed wire, homesteading and the disastrous winter of 1886–87 ended the open-range era, and ranching settled into fenced operations that persist today.

Variants

Dude ranches (guest ranches) take paying visitors who participate in ranch life; working ranches may also raise horses, bison, elk or ostriches, or combine livestock with farming and hunting leases.
