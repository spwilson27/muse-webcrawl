Pororoca

The Pororoca (Portuguese pronunciation: [poɾoˈɾɔkɐ]) is a tidal bore, with waves up to 4 m (13 ft) high that travel as much as 13 km (8.1 mi) inland upstream on the Amazon River and neighboring rivers in Brazil. Its name comes from the indigenous Tupi language, where it translates into "great roar" — the sound of the approaching bore can be heard up to half an hour before it arrives.

Formation

The Pororoca occurs at the mouth of the Amazon where river water meets the Atlantic Ocean, most strongly during the spring tides around the equinoxes in February and March. When the incoming tide is funnelled into the shallow, narrowing river mouth, it forms a single breaking wave — a tidal bore — that surges upstream against the current. The phenomenon is most famous on the Araguari River in the state of Amapá, where waves travel great distances inland through channels in the floodplain.

Surfing

Since 1999, surfers have competed in an annual championship held in São Domingos do Capim on the Guamá River, attempting to ride the Pororoca for as long as possible. Brazilian surfer Picuruta Salazar holds a notable record for riding the bore for more than 12 kilometres over about 37 minutes in 2003. Riding the Pororoca is hazardous because the water carries debris, including entire trees, and the rivers are home to piranhas and the bull shark.
