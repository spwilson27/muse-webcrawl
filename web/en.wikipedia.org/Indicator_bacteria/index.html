Indicator bacteria

Indicator bacteria are types of bacteria used in water quality testing to detect and estimate the level of faecal contamination of water. They are not themselves usually pathogenic; rather, their presence indicates that faecal material — and therefore potentially disease-causing pathogens such as Salmonella, Campylobacter, norovirus or Cryptosporidium — may be present. Testing directly for every possible pathogen would be slow and impractical, so monitoring concentrates on a few organisms that are abundant in faeces and easy to culture.

Common indicators

  • Total coliforms — a broad group of Gram-negative, rod-shaped bacteria found in soil, vegetation and faeces; used mainly as an indicator of treatment efficacy and distribution-system integrity in drinking water.
  • Faecal (thermotolerant) coliforms — the subset that grows at 44.5 °C, more closely associated with warm-blooded animal faeces.
  • Escherichia coli (E. coli) — the preferred indicator of recent faecal contamination of drinking water; the World Health Organization and most national standards require that no E. coli be detected in a 100 mL sample of treated drinking water.
  • Intestinal enterococci (Enterococcus spp.) — survive longer in seawater and are the standard indicator for marine bathing waters.
  • Clostridium perfringens — a spore-former whose spores persist long after other indicators die off, indicating older or intermittent contamination.

Criteria for an ideal indicator

An ideal indicator organism should be present whenever pathogens are present and in greater numbers; absent from uncontaminated water; unable to multiply in the environment; at least as resistant to disinfection as the pathogens it signals; harmless to humans; and detectable by simple, cheap, standardised methods such as membrane filtration, defined-substrate tests (e.g. Colilert) or multiple-tube fermentation. No real organism meets all criteria, which is why several indicators are used in combination.

Limitations

Indicator counts correlate imperfectly with health risk. Some pathogens — notably viruses and protozoan cysts such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium — are more resistant to chlorination than bacterial indicators, so water can pass bacterial tests while still transmitting them (as in the 1993 Milwaukee cryptosporidiosis outbreak). Indicators also cannot identify the source of contamination (human versus animal) without additional microbial source-tracking methods, and counts can regrow in warm distribution systems or sediments. Modern practice increasingly supplements culture-based indicator tests with molecular methods such as qPCR for specific pathogens or markers.
