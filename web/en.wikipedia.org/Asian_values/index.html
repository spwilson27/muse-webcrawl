Asian values is a political ideology that emerged in the 1990s, promoted especially by Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew and Malaysia's Mahathir Mohamad, holding that East and Southeast Asian societies possess distinctive communal values — respect for authority, strong families, thrift, hard work, emphasis on consensus and community over individual rights — that justify governance models different from Western liberal democracy.

The debate

Advocates credited these values, rooted partly in Confucian tradition, with the rapid economic growth of the "Asian tiger" economies, and argued that human rights priorities should reflect local culture. The 1993 Bangkok Declaration by Asian governments stressed non-interference and the cultural specificity of rights ahead of the Vienna World Conference on Human Rights.

Criticism

Critics, including economist Amartya Sen and South Korean dissident (later president) Kim Dae-jung, argued that there is no single set of "Asian values", that Asian traditions contain strong democratic and tolerant strands, and that the doctrine was used to legitimise authoritarian rule. The 1997 Asian financial crisis weakened the claim that such governance was economically superior, and the debate subsided, though related arguments continue to recur.
