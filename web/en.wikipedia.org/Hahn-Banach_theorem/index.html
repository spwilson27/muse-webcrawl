Hahn–Banach theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hahn–Banach theorem is a central result in functional analysis. It states that a bounded linear functional defined on a subspace of a vector space can be extended to the whole space while preserving its bound, provided the functional is dominated by a sublinear function. The theorem is named after Hans Hahn and Stefan Banach, who proved it independently in the late 1920s.

Statement

Let V be a vector space over the real or complex numbers, and let p : V → ℝ be a sublinear function (subadditive and positively homogeneous). If f is a linear functional defined on a subspace U of V satisfying f(x) ≤ p(x) for all x in U, then there exists a linear extension F of f to all of V that is still dominated by p everywhere.

Important consequences

  • Norm-preserving extension: a continuous linear functional on a subspace of a normed space extends to the whole space with the same norm.
  • Abundance of duals: for any non-zero vector x in a normed space there is a continuous linear functional f with f(x) = ‖x‖ and ‖f‖ = 1; hence the dual space separates points.
  • Separation theorems: disjoint non-empty convex sets, one of them open, can be separated by a hyperplane — a result underpinning convex optimisation and mathematical economics.
  • Riesz representation and Banach limits: the theorem underlies constructions such as Banach limits and invariant means.

Proof and logical strength

The standard proof extends the functional one dimension at a time and then invokes Zorn's lemma to pass to a maximal extension; the theorem is therefore non-constructive. Over ZF set theory, the Hahn–Banach theorem is strictly weaker than the axiom of choice but is not provable without some choice-like principle; it follows from the Boolean prime ideal theorem.

History

Precursors appeared in work of Eduard Helly and Frigyes Riesz. Hahn published his version in 1927 and Banach gave the general form, for functionals dominated by a seminorm, in 1929 and in his 1932 monograph Théorie des opérations linéaires.
