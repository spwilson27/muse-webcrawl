Gilded Age America combined dazzling wealth with deep inequality.

The Gilded Age is the period in United States history from the 1870s to about 1900, following the Reconstruction era and preceding the Progressive Era. The name, taken from Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner's 1873 novel The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today, evokes an era of glittering surface prosperity concealing serious social problems — "gilded" (thinly coated in gold) rather than golden. It was a time of extraordinary economic growth, industrialization and immigration, alongside political corruption, corporate monopolies and stark inequality.

Economic transformation

Railroads expanded from about 35,000 miles in 1865 to some 200,000 by 1900, stitching together a national market. Steel, oil, telegraphy and meatpacking became giant industries organized in corporations and trusts. Industrialists such as Andrew Carnegie (steel), John D. Rockefeller (Standard Oil) and Cornelius Vanderbilt (railroads) amassed vast fortunes and were celebrated as "captains of industry" or condemned as "robber barons." Gross national product grew rapidly and the United States became the world's leading industrial economy by the 1890s.

Labour and immigration

Between 1870 and 1900 roughly 12 million immigrants arrived, many from southern and eastern Europe, supplying labour for factories, mines and mills. Working conditions were dangerous and hours long; workers organized in unions such as the Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor, and the era saw major strikes and violence — the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, the Haymarket affair (1886), the Homestead Strike (1892) and the Pullman Strike (1894) — usually defeated with the help of courts and troops.

Politics

National politics was marked by closely contested elections, high voter turnout and patronage-driven parties. Every president from Rutherford B. Hayes to William McKinley served in this period; corruption scandals and machine politics flourished, though reforms such as the Pendleton Civil Service Act (1883), the Interstate Commerce Act (1887) and the Sherman Antitrust Act (1890) began federal regulation. Agrarian discontent over deflation and railroad rates fuelled the Populist movement of the 1890s. The Panic of 1893 triggered a severe depression. The election of 1896 and the Spanish–American War of 1898 are often taken as closing the era, which gave way to Progressive reform in the early 1900s.

Culture and society

The age produced skyscrapers, department stores, vaudeville, and the beginnings of mass consumer culture, as well as conspicuous consumption by the new rich in mansions on New York's Fifth Avenue and in Newport, Rhode Island. In the South, Jim Crow laws entrenched racial segregation even as the West was settled and Native American peoples were confined to reservations.
