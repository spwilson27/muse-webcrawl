Transparency and translucency

From left: an opaque material, a translucent material (light passes but is scattered), and a transparent material.

In the field of optics, transparency (also called pellucidity or diaphaneity) is the physical property of allowing light to pass through a material without appreciable scattering. A transparent material, such as window glass or clear water, lets enough light through that objects can be seen distinctly on the other side. Translucency (also called translucence or translucidity) allows light to pass through but diffuses it so that objects on the opposite side are not clearly visible; examples include frosted glass and tracing paper. The opposite property is opacity.

Mechanisms

Whether a material is transparent depends on the wavelength of the light and on the material's electronic structure. In many insulators, photons in the visible range lack the energy to excite electrons across the band gap, so they pass through unabsorbed; scattering is minimal when the material is homogeneous at scales comparable to the wavelength. Translucency arises when internal structures — grain boundaries, tiny crystals, fibres, or suspended particles — scatter light while still transmitting a large fraction of it.

On a macroscopic scale, transmission is described by the Beer–Lambert law, which relates absorption to the thickness of material the light travels through. Reflection at surfaces, governed by the Fresnel equations, also limits total transmission.

Applications

Transparent materials are essential for windows, lenses, optical fibres, display screens and protective visors. Translucent materials are used for privacy glazing, lampshades, daylighting panels, and packaging. Many living tissues are translucent, and numerous animals — jellyfish, glass frogs, and certain butterflies — exploit transparency for camouflage.
