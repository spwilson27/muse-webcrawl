Bicycle law

Bicycle law is the parts of law that apply to the riding of bicycles. Although bicycle law is a relatively narrow speciality within the law, it does form part of the body of the transport law governing road use, and intersects with insurance, liability, property, and criminal law. Bicycle law varies substantially between jurisdictions.

Position of cyclists in traffic law

In most jurisdictions, a person riding a bicycle on a public road is granted the rights and is subject to the duties of the driver of a vehicle. Cyclists are generally required to ride in the same direction as traffic, obey traffic signals and signs, yield to pedestrians, and signal turns. At the same time, many jurisdictions impose cycle-specific rules, such as requirements for lights and reflectors at night, bell or horn requirements, and in some places mandatory helmet laws — universal in Australia and New Zealand, and common for children elsewhere.

Common specific issues

Bicycle law frequently addresses: whether cyclists may ride two abreast; when a cyclist may "take the lane" rather than ride near the kerb; minimum passing distance laws requiring motorists to leave (commonly) 1 to 1.5 metres when overtaking; sidewalk (pavement) riding rules; access to bicycle lanes and whether their use is mandatory; e-bike classifications; and liability in "dooring" collisions. The Vienna Convention on Road Traffic (1968) provides an international framework under which bicycles are treated as vehicles in signatory states.

History

Bicycle-specific legislation dates to the 19th-century cycling boom, when local ordinances regulated speed, lamps, and bells, and cycling organizations successfully campaigned for the legal status of the bicycle as a vehicle with road rights — groundwork that shaped modern motor vehicle codes.
