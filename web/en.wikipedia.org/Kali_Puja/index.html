Kali Puja

Kali Puja
Observed byHindus, especially in West Bengal, Assam, Odisha, Tripura, Bihar, and Bangladesh
TypeHindu festival
SignificanceWorship of goddess Kali; victory of good over evil
DateAmavasya (new moon) of Kartik month — coincides with Diwali

Kali Puja, also known as Shyama Puja or Mahanisha Puja, is a Hindu festival originating from the Indian subcontinent, dedicated to the goddess Kali. It is celebrated on the new moon day (Dipannita Amavasya) of the Hindu month of Kartik, coinciding with the Diwali festival.

Celebration

Devotees worship Kali, the fierce form of the goddess Parvati/Adi Shakti, as the destroyer of evil forces and the divine mother who liberates her children from fear and bondage. Worship is typically performed at night, in temples, cremation grounds, and community pandals — temporary decorated structures housing clay idols of Kali, often shown standing on Shiva, wearing a garland of skulls, accompanied by images of Shiva, Ramakrishna, and Bamakhepa in Bengali tradition.

Homes are lit with earthen lamps (diyas) and candles, fireworks are set off, and offerings of red hibiscus flowers, sweets, rice, and lentils are made. In many households Lakshmi Puja (as part of Diwali) and Kali Puja are observed on the same night.

History

The festival gained popularity in Bengal in the 18th century, promoted by Raja Krishnachandra of Nadia and by zamindari patronage, and grew out of older Tantric traditions of Kali worship. Saint Ramakrishna's devotion at Dakshineswar Kali Temple in the 19th century further spread the festival's popularity.

Regional observance

Kali Puja is a major public festival in West Bengal — especially Kolkata — as well as in Assam, Tripura, Odisha, parts of Bihar and Jharkhand, and among Hindu communities in Bangladesh and the diaspora. In Nepal it is observed during Tihar.
