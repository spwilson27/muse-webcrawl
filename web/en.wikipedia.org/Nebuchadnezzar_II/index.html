Nebuchadnezzar II (Babylonian cuneiform: Nabû-kudurri-uṣur, "Nabu, watch over my heir"; c. 642 – 562 BC) was the second king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, ruling from the death of his father Nabopolassar in 605 BC until his own death in 562 BC. Historically known as Nebuchadnezzar the Great, he is regarded as the empire's greatest king.

Reign and campaigns

As crown prince he defeated the Egyptians and Assyrians at the Battle of Carchemish (605 BC). As king he campaigned repeatedly in the Levant (the "Hatti-land"), subjugating the Kingdom of Judah: Jerusalem was first taken in 597 BC, and after a further rebellion was besieged, captured and destroyed in 587/586 BC, with its temple burned and much of its population deported to Babylonia — the Babylonian captivity of the Hebrew Bible. He also besieged Tyre for thirteen years and invaded Egypt.

Building works

Nebuchadnezzar transformed Babylon into one of the wonders of the ancient world: he rebuilt its temples and ziggurat (Etemenanki), raised massive double walls, and constructed the Ishtar Gate and Processional Way with their glazed blue bricks and reliefs of animals. Tradition credits him with the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, said to have been built for his Median queen Amytis, though their existence and location remain debated among scholars.

In the Bible and later tradition

Nebuchadnezzar is a major figure in the Hebrew Bible (Books of Kings, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and Daniel), where he appears both as God's instrument of judgment and as a proud king humbled by madness — in Daniel, driven to live like a beast for "seven times". Verdi's opera Nabucco (1842) retells his story.
