Mudflow

A mudflow, also known as a mudslide or debris flow, is a form of mass wasting involving rapid surging flow of debris that has become partially or fully liquefied by the addition of significant amounts of water to the source material. Mudflows containing more than 50% silt- and clay-sized particles are sometimes called lahars when they occur on volcano slopes.

Aftermath of a mudflow

Causes

Mudflows occur when slopes saturated by heavy rainfall, rapid snowmelt, or volcanic eruption lose stability and collapse. They can move at speeds of up to tens of kilometres per hour, carrying boulders, trees and buildings. Vegetation loss from wildfire greatly increases mudflow risk on burned slopes.

Notable events

The 1985 Armero tragedy in Colombia, where a lahar from Nevado del Ruiz buried the town of Armero killing more than 23,000 people, is among the deadliest volcanic mudflows in history. The 2014 Oso landslide in Washington state and recurring flows in Southern California's burn areas are prominent debris-flow disasters.
