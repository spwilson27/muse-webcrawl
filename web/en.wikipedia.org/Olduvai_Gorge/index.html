Olduvai Gorge, in the Great Rift Valley of northern Tanzania, is one of the most important paleoanthropological sites in the world. The steep-sided ravine, about 48 kilometres (30 mi) long and 90 metres deep, exposes a sequence of lake, stream, and volcanic deposits spanning roughly the last two million years, preserving abundant fossils and stone tools that document the early stages of human evolution.

Research history

The German entomologist Wilhelm Kattwinkel visited the site in 1911, and Hans Reck led early geological work in 1913. Louis and Mary Leakey began excavations in 1931 and returned for decades. In 1959 Mary Leakey discovered the robust australopithecine skull OH 5, nicknamed "Zinj" (Paranthropus boisei), which transformed the site's fame. In 1960–61 the Leakeys and their son Jonathan found remains attributed to Homo habilis, the presumed maker of the Oldowan stone tools for which the gorge gave its name.

Stratigraphy and dating

The gorge's deposits are divided into Beds I through IV and younger formations, laid down around an ancient lake basin and interleaved with volcanic ash layers. Because the ashes could be dated by potassium-argon methods — one of the first successful applications of radiometric dating to early human fossils — Olduvai provided a crucial timescale for human evolution: Bed I was shown to be about 1.8 million years old, far older than most scientists had expected.

Findings

Fossils from the gorge include Paranthropus boisei, Homo habilis, Homo erectus, and a rich fauna of extinct animals. Stone tool industries progress from Oldowan in Bed I to Developed Oldowan and Acheulean handaxes in the upper beds. The nearby Laetoli site, where Mary Leakey's team found 3.6-million-year-old hominin footprints, complements the gorge's record.

Museum

The Olduvai Gorge Museum, founded by Mary Leakey and opened in 1970, exhibits fossils and tools at the site and was renovated and expanded in the 2010s.
