Clever Hans

Clever Hans (German: der Kluge Hans) was a horse that was claimed to have performed arithmetic and other intellectual feats during the early 1900s in Germany. Owned by Wilhelm von Osten, a retired schoolteacher, Hans appeared to answer questions by tapping his hoof the correct number of times, correctly solving problems involving addition, fractions, reading, and telling time. An investigation in 1907 by the psychologist Oskar Pfungst showed that the horse was not calculating but was instead responding to involuntary cues in the body language of his questioners. The case gave its name to the Clever Hans effect, a classic problem in experimental psychology and animal cognition research.

The performances

Von Osten, convinced that animals possessed intelligence comparable to humans, trained his Orlov Trotter Hans for several years using rewards of bread and carrots. By 1904 the horse was famous: large crowds gathered to watch him paw out answers, and he was usually correct. Skeptics suspected fraud, but von Osten charged no admission and appeared entirely sincere.

Investigation

In 1904 a commission of experts, including the psychologist Carl Stumpf, found no evidence of trickery. Pfungst, working in Stumpf's laboratory, then conducted controlled tests in 1907. He found that Hans answered correctly only when the questioner knew the answer and was visible to the horse. When questioners wore blinders blocking their view, or did not know the answer, Hans's accuracy collapsed. Pfungst showed that as Hans approached the correct number of taps, questioners involuntarily straightened up or relaxed — minute movements Hans had learned to read.

Legacy

The Clever Hans effect became a foundational cautionary tale for experimental design, motivating double-blind testing and controls for experimenter expectancy effects. The phrase is still used whenever an animal, or an experiment, appears to succeed by reading subtle cues rather than by the ability being tested.
