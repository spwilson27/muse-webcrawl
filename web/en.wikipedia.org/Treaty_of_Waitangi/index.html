Treaty of Waitangi

The Treaty of Waitangi (Māori: Te Tiriti o Waitangi), sometimes referred to simply as Te Tiriti, is a treaty of cession signed on 6 February 1840 by representatives of the British Crown and various Māori chiefs from the North Island of New Zealand. It is widely considered to be the founding document of New Zealand.

Background

By the late 1830s, growing British settlement, lawlessness among traders, and French interest in New Zealand pushed the British government to intervene. Captain William Hobson was sent with instructions to obtain a cession of sovereignty by treaty. The English text was drafted in days and translated into Māori overnight by missionary Henry Williams and his son Edward.

Content and translation differences

The treaty has a preamble and three articles. In the English text, Māori cede sovereignty to the Crown (Article I), are guaranteed possession of their lands and treasures (Article II), and receive the rights of British subjects (Article III). In the Māori text — the version most chiefs signed — the Crown receives kāwanatanga (governance) rather than full sovereignty, and chiefs retain tino rangatiratanga (full authority) over their lands and taonga (treasures); these differences have been debated ever since.

Aftermath and modern status

The treaty was subsequently breached through land confiscations and purchasing practices, leading to the New Zealand Wars and major Māori land loss. From the 1970s, Māori protest and the establishment of the Waitangi Tribunal (1975) brought the treaty back to the centre of public life; many statutes now require regard to the "principles of the Treaty", and the Crown has negotiated settlements and apologies for historical breaches. Waitangi Day, 6 February, is New Zealand's national day.
