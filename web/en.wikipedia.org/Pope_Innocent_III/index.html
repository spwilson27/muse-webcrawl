Pope Innocent III (Latin: Innocentius III; 1160 or 1161 – 16 July 1216), born Lotario dei Conti di Segni, was head of the Catholic Church from 8 January 1198 until his death. He is widely regarded as the most powerful and influential of the medieval popes: he asserted the doctrine of papal supremacy — the plenitudo potestatis ("fullness of power") — claimed authority over all Christian rulers in matters of sin, and made the papacy the arbiter of Europe's politics.

Papal monarchy

Elected at about 37, Innocent intervened decisively in the disputed German imperial election, supporting first Otto IV and then Frederick II. He placed France under interdict to force Philip Augustus to take back his repudiated wife, and in 1213 received England as a papal fief from King John, after an interdict of five years; he subsequently annulled Magna Carta (1215) as an unjust infringement of his rights as John's feudal overlord. Aragon, Portugal, Poland, Hungary and other kingdoms acknowledged forms of papal overlordship during his reign.

Crusades and reform

Innocent launched the Fourth Crusade (1202–04), which horrified him by diverting to sack Christian Zara and then Constantinople, despite his prohibitions and excommunications. He proclaimed the Albigensian Crusade against the Cathars of southern France (1209) and the Fifth Crusade shortly before his death. He gave verbal approval to Francis of Assisi's new mendicant order in 1209/10 and supported Dominic's Order of Preachers. His crowning legislative achievement was the Fourth Lateran Council (1215), the largest council of the Middle Ages, which defined transubstantiation, mandated annual confession and communion for all Christians, required distinguishing dress for Jews and Muslims, reformed clerical discipline, and regulated episcopal elections.

Legacy

Innocent professionalised the papal administration, expanded the use of decretals that shaped canon law (later systematised under Gregory IX, his successor's successor and a member of his own family), and authored the influential meditation De miseria conditionis humanae. Dante placed his memory ambiguously — praising his approval of Francis while damning several of his successors — but historians generally rank his pontificate as the apex of the medieval papacy.
