Groundwater pollution (also called groundwater contamination) occurs when pollutants are released to the ground and make their way into an aquifer, rendering the water unsafe for drinking and other uses. Groundwater supplies drinking water for billions of people, and because aquifers are hidden, slow-moving and difficult to clean, contamination may go undetected for years and persist for decades or longer.

Causes

Sources are divided into point sources, such as leaking underground fuel storage tanks, landfills, industrial spills, septic systems and mine workings, and non-point sources, notably agricultural fertilisers and pesticides leached over wide areas. Contaminants include nitrate from fertiliser and sewage; chlorinated solvents from industry; petroleum hydrocarbons; heavy metals; naturally occurring arsenic and fluoride mobilised by pumping; pathogens from sanitation; and persistent synthetic chemicals such as PFAS. Saline intrusion from over-pumping coastal aquifers is another major form of degradation.

Transport and fate

Pollutants move with groundwater flow by advection, spread by dispersion and diffusion, and may be retarded by sorption or transformed by chemical and microbial processes. Dense non-aqueous phase liquids (DNAPLs) such as chlorinated solvents sink below the water table and dissolve slowly, creating long-lived plumes.

Prevention and remediation

Prevention relies on land-use zoning around wellheads, lining of landfills, double-walled tanks, and regulation of waste disposal. Remediation techniques include pump-and-treat, in-situ bioremediation, chemical oxidation, permeable reactive barriers, and monitored natural attenuation. Laws such as the U.S. Safe Drinking Water Act and the EU Water Framework Directive set standards for groundwater protection.
