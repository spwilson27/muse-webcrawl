Industry 4.0

Industry 4.0, also known as the Fourth Industrial Revolution, is the ongoing automation and digitization of manufacturing and industry through technologies such as the Internet of Things (IoT), cyber-physical systems, cloud computing, artificial intelligence, and big data analytics. The term was introduced by the German government as part of its High-Tech Strategy, presented at the Hannover Fair in 2011, and has since become a global framework for industrial modernization.

Concept and technologies

Industry 4.0 envisions "smart factories" in which machines, products, and systems communicate continuously, enabling decentralized decision-making, real-time monitoring, and mass customization. A product's "digital twin" — a live virtual replica — accompanies it through design, production, and service. Key enabling technologies include industrial IoT sensors, collaborative robots, additive manufacturing (3D printing), augmented reality for maintenance, autonomous logistics, and AI-driven predictive maintenance and quality control.

Relation to earlier revolutions

The framework numbers industrial revolutions as: mechanization with water and steam power (Industry 1.0); mass production using electricity and assembly lines (Industry 2.0); and computerization and programmable automation from the 1970s (Industry 3.0). Industry 4.0 builds on the third by networking systems within and across firms, supply chains, and national borders.

Adoption and criticism

Governments worldwide launched analogous programs — "Made in China 2025", Japan's "Society 5.0", and various smart-manufacturing initiatives in the United States and Europe. Critics question the concept's hype, cybersecurity risks from connected machinery, workforce displacement and reskilling needs, and the dependence of smaller suppliers on dominant platform providers. Researchers have also debated the term's usefulness, proposing alternatives such as Industry 5.0, which emphasizes human-centric and sustainable production.
