Bill of attainder

A bill of attainder (also known as an act of attainder) is an act of a legislature declaring a person, or a group of people, guilty of some crime and punishing them, often without a judicial trial. Because such bills bypass the courts, they are prohibited by the United States Constitution.

English origins

Bills of attainder were used frequently in England between the 14th and 18th centuries, especially during political turmoil. A person attainted suffered "corruption of blood," forfeiting property and civil rights, and attainder was often accompanied by execution. Notable victims included figures condemned during the Wars of the Roses and the reign of Henry VIII.

United States

Article I, Section 9 of the U.S. Constitution forbids the federal government from passing bills of attainder, and Section 10 imposes the same prohibition on the states. The Supreme Court has struck down laws that legislatively impose punishment on identifiable persons, as in United States v. Lovett (1946).

Related concepts

A bill of pains and penalties was a similar legislative act imposing penalties short of death. The attainder prohibition is closely related to the constitutional ban on ex post facto laws.
