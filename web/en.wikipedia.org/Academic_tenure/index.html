Academic tenure

Tenure is a category of academic appointment that, in the United States and Canada, exists as an indefinite academic appointment that can be terminated only for cause or under extraordinary circumstances, such as financial exigency or program discontinuation. Tenure is a means of defending academic freedom, which, in the view of its advocates, is necessary for universities to fulfil their function of producing and disseminating knowledge.

In North America, tenure typically follows a probationary period — the "tenure track" — of roughly six to seven years as an assistant professor, after which the faculty member is reviewed for promotion to associate professor with tenure. Faculty who are denied tenure are usually given a final year of employment. Full professors are almost always tenured.

History

The modern American tenure system was shaped by the American Association of University Professors (AAUP), whose 1940 Statement of Principles on Academic Freedom and Tenure, written jointly with the Association of American Colleges, established the standard framework of a probationary period of no more than seven years followed by permanent tenure, terminable only for adequate cause after a hearing.

Arguments for and against

Supporters argue tenure protects scholars who pursue controversial or unfashionable research, allows long-term research programs, and attracts talented people to academic careers despite lower salaries than industry. Critics argue it can shelter unproductive faculty, reduce accountability, and make institutions inflexible. Many countries outside North America use permanent civil-service-style appointments rather than tenure, while the United Kingdom abolished statutory tenure for new appointments in 1988.
