Moa are an extinct group of flightless birds formerly endemic to New Zealand. They comprised nine species in six genera, ranging from the turkey-sized upland moa to the giant moa (Dinornis), whose females stood up to 3.6 metres tall with neck outstretched and weighed as much as 230 kilograms — among the tallest birds ever known. Uniquely among birds, moa had completely lost their wings, lacking even vestigial wing bones.

Biology and ecology

Moa were herbivorous browsers and grazers, grinding tough vegetation with stones held in a muscular gizzard; preserved gizzard contents and coprolites show diets of twigs, leaves and seeds. They exhibited pronounced reversed sexual dimorphism, females being up to 150 per cent taller and 280 per cent heavier than males — a difference so extreme that the sexes of Dinornis were long classified as separate species until DNA analysis united them. Their main natural predator was the enormous Haast's eagle.

DNA studies place moa as the sister group of the flighted tinamous of South America, implying that their ancestors flew to New Zealand after it separated from Gondwana, and that flightlessness evolved on the islands.

Extinction

Polynesian settlers — the ancestors of the Māori — arrived in New Zealand around 1300 and hunted moa intensively for meat, bone and eggs, while introduced Pacific rats ate eggs and chicks. The birds' slow reproduction could not sustain the pressure. All species were extinct by about 1445, within roughly two centuries of human arrival; the Haast's eagle, dependent on moa prey, died out soon after. Moa hunting is one of the clearest documented cases of human-driven megafaunal extinction.

Rediscovery and study

Moa bones were described scientifically from 1839, when Richard Owen recognised a fragment as belonging to a giant bird, and spectacular finds of skeletons, mummified remains, feathers and eggs followed in swamps and caves. Moa remain prominent in Māori tradition and New Zealand culture, and occasional unsubstantiated sightings keep them a fixture of cryptozoological speculation.
