Nominative–accusative language

A nominative–accusative language is one that treats the subject of an intransitive verb and the agent of a transitive verb in the same way grammatically (the nominative case), while treating the object of a transitive verb distinctly (the accusative case). This alignment is the most common worldwide and characterises most Indo-European languages, English included (visible in pronouns: he runs / he sees him).

Alignment

Linguists describe alignment with three roles: S (intransitive subject), A (transitive agent) and O (transitive patient). Nominative–accusative systems group S with A against O. The main alternative, ergative–absolutive alignment, groups S with O. Marking may be carried by case affixes, agreement, or both.

Distribution

Nominative–accusative alignment predominates in Europe, Africa and much of Asia and the Americas. Some languages mix systems — for example, split ergativity conditioned by tense or animacy — but the nominative–accusative pattern remains the statistical norm among the world's languages.
