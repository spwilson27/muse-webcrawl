A spa town is a resort town developed around mineral or thermal springs, whose waters are reputed to have health-giving properties. Visitors travel to spa towns to bathe in and drink the waters — "taking the cure" — and the towns typically grew elegant ensembles of bathhouses, pump rooms, hotels, promenades, parks and assembly rooms to serve them.

History

Bathing in mineral springs dates to antiquity; the Romans built baths across their empire, including Aquae Sulis (Bath) in Britain. The modern spa town takes its very name from Spa in the Ardennes of Belgium, fashionable from the sixteenth century. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, spa towns flourished across Europe — Bath, Tunbridge Wells, Cheltenham and Harrogate in England; Baden-Baden, Wiesbaden and Bad Kissingen in Germany; Karlovy Vary and Mariánské Lázně in Bohemia; Vichy in France — as centres of medicine, leisure and polite society. Railway travel widened their clientele, and grand hotels and casinos followed. In North America, places such as Saratoga Springs and Hot Springs, Arkansas, developed similarly.

The cure

The traditional regimen combined bathing in thermal pools with drinking prescribed quantities of mineral water, rest, diet and exercise, supervised by spa physicians. Waters rich in sulphur, iron, salts or radioactivity (a once-fashionable attribute) were held to treat ailments from rheumatism to gout and skin disease. Modern evidence for many claims is limited, but balneotherapy remains embedded in the health systems of several European countries.

Heritage

Eleven towns in seven countries were inscribed together on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2021 as the "Great Spa Towns of Europe", recognising their shared urban form and cultural role.
