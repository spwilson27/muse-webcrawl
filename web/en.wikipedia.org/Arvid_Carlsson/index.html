Arvid Carlsson ( January 25, 1923 – June 29, 2018) was a Swedish neuropharmacologist best known for his work with the neurotransmitter dopamine and its effects in Parkinson's disease. He shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Eric Kandel and Paul Greengard "for their discoveries concerning signal transduction in the nervous system".

Research

In the 1950s, working at Lund University and later the University of Gothenburg, Carlsson demonstrated that dopamine is a neurotransmitter in its own right, concentrated in brain regions controlling movement, overturning the view that it was merely a precursor of noradrenaline. He showed that animals depleted of dopamine with reserpine could be restored by administering L-DOPA, laying the foundation for modern Parkinson's therapy.

Later career

Carlsson's work also influenced the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia and the development of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors; he helped develop zimelidine, one of the first SSRIs. He remained active in research and public debate in Sweden into his nineties.
