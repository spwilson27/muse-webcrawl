Hurdy-gurdy

Hurdy-gurdy
Other names: Wheel fiddle, vielle à roue
Classification: String instrument (chordophone)
Origin: Europe, by the 10th–11th century
Sound production: Rosined wheel bowed by a crank

The hurdy-gurdy is a stringed instrument that produces sound by a hand-cranked, rosined wheel rubbing against the strings. The wheel functions much like a violin bow, and single notes and harmonies are played by pressing keys that touch tangents to the melody strings, shortening them to change pitch. Most forms also have drone strings that sound continuously.

History

The instrument probably developed in Europe by the 10th or 11th century; the large church organistrum required two players. By the Renaissance smaller solo forms spread across Europe. In 18th-century France the vielle à roue enjoyed aristocratic fashion, with compositions by court musicians, while elsewhere it became associated with folk music, street musicians and beggars — a stigma reflected in names such as the German Bauernleier (“peasant lyre”).

Construction

A typical hurdy-gurdy has two or three melody (chanterelle) strings passing through a keybox, plus drone strings (bourdons) and sometimes trompette strings resting on a movable bridge that buzzes rhythmically when the crank speed varies — a technique called coup de poignet, giving rhythmic accompaniment.

Modern use

The instrument survives in the folk traditions of France, Hungary (tekerőlant), Spain, Poland, Ukraine (lira) and Sweden's related nyckelharpa tradition. It has been revived in early music, folk-rock and film scores, and is played by contemporary musicians across Europe.
