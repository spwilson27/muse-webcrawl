To Kill a Mockingbird

To Kill a Mockingbird is a novel by the American author Harper Lee, published in 1960. It became immediately successful, winning the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction in 1961, and has become a classic of modern American literature. The plot and characters are loosely based on Lee's observations of her family and neighbours in Monroeville, Alabama, and an event that occurred there in 1936, when she was ten years old.

Plot

The novel is narrated by Jean Louise "Scout" Finch, who lives with her older brother Jeremy "Jem" and their widowed father, Atticus Finch, a lawyer, in the fictional town of Maycomb, Alabama, during the Great Depression. Scout and Jem befriend a boy named Dill and become fascinated by their reclusive neighbour, Arthur "Boo" Radley. When Atticus defends Tom Robinson, a Black man falsely accused of raping a white woman, Mayella Ewell, the children witness the town's racial prejudice and the trial's unjust outcome. Robinson is convicted and later killed while attempting to escape prison. In the climax, Bob Ewell attacks Scout and Jem, and Boo Radley rescues them, killing Ewell; the sheriff agrees to report that Ewell fell on his own knife.

Themes

The novel explores racial injustice in the American South, moral conscience, courage, compassion and the loss of innocence. Atticus Finch has become an iconic figure of integrity in the legal profession, and the book is widely taught in schools in the United States, though it has also been challenged for its language and racial themes.

Adaptations and legacy

The 1962 film adaptation, directed by Robert Mulligan and starring Gregory Peck as Atticus Finch, won three Academy Awards. A stage adaptation by Aaron Sorkin premiered on Broadway in 2018. Lee's earlier draft of the novel was published in 2015 as Go Set a Watchman. The novel has sold more than 40 million copies and has been translated into dozens of languages.
