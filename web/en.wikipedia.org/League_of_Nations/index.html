The League of Nations (French: Société des Nations) was the first worldwide intergovernmental organisation whose principal mission was to maintain world peace. It was founded on 10 January 1920 by the Paris Peace Conference that ended the First World War. The Covenant of the League was part of the Treaty of Versailles.

Origins

The idea of a community of nations was championed by, among others, US President Woodrow Wilson, whose Fourteen Points called for a "general association of nations." The League's principal organs were an Assembly of all member states, a Council of great powers, and a permanent Secretariat based in Geneva.

Successes and failures

The League achieved modest successes in the 1920s, settling disputes such as the Åland Islands question and overseeing mandates and plebiscites. It also made lasting contributions through agencies concerned with health, refugees, and labour (the International Labour Organization). However, it lacked an armed force and depended on member states for enforcement. Its failure to respond effectively to the Japanese invasion of Manchuria (1931), the Italian invasion of Abyssinia (1935), and the outbreak of the Second World War destroyed its credibility.

Dissolution and legacy

The League formally dissolved itself on 20 April 1946, transferring its assets and many of its functions to the newly created United Nations. Its experiment in collective security shaped the design of its successor.
