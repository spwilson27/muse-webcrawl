Corporate raid

In business, a corporate raid is the process of buying a large stake in a publicly traded corporation and then using the shareholder voting rights to require the company to undertake novel measures designed to increase the share value, generally in opposition to the desires and practices of the corporation's current management. The measures might include replacing top executives, downsizing operations, or liquidating the company.

History

Corporate raiding became prominent in the United States in the 1970s and 1980s, when figures such as T. Boone Pickens, Carl Icahn, Ronald Perelman and Kirk Kerkorian accumulated stakes in undervalued companies. Raiders often profited through "greenmail", in which the target company bought back the raider's shares at a premium to end the threat. The era produced famous contests such as the battle for RJR Nabisco, chronicled in Barbarians at the Gate.

Defences

Target companies developed defences including poison pills (shareholder rights plans), staggered boards, golden parachutes for executives, and seeking a friendly "white knight" acquirer. Many jurisdictions and stock exchanges later restricted greenmail and other raiding tactics.

Modern practice

The modern descendant of the corporate raider is the activist investor, who typically seeks board representation and strategic change rather than outright takeover or liquidation, though the line between the two remains debated.
