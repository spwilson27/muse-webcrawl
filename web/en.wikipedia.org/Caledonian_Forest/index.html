Caledonian Forest

The Caledonian Forest is the name given to the ancient temperate rainforest that once covered much of Scotland. Named after Caledonia, the Roman name for the region, the forest is famous for its distinctive ecosystem dominated by old-growth Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris). Today only fragments survive — about 1% of the original area — in 35 main remnants scattered across the Scottish Highlands.

History

The forest colonised Scotland after the last ice age, reaching its maximum extent around 5,000 years ago. From the Neolithic era onward it was gradually cleared by human action — felling for timber and fuel, and grazing that prevented regeneration — a decline accelerated by climatic cooling and the spread of blanket bog. By the 18th and 19th centuries the remaining pinewoods were heavily exploited for timber, including during both World Wars.

Ecology

The Caledonian pinewoods support a unique assemblage of wildlife, including the Scottish crossbill (Britain's only endemic bird species), capercaillie, red squirrel, pine marten, crested tit, wildcat, and characteristic plants such as twinflower, creeping lady's-tresses and one-flowered wintergreen. Dead and dying "granny pines" and fallen timber are important habitat features.

Conservation

Remnants survive at places such as Glen Affric, Rothiemurchus, Abernethy Forest and the Black Wood of Rannoch. Conservation bodies including Trees for Life, the RSPB and Forestry and Land Scotland work to protect and restore the forest by fencing out deer, removing non-native trees and planting native pines to reconnect fragments.
