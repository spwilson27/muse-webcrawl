Low-frequency oscillation

A low-frequency oscillation (LFO) is an electronic signal, usually below 20 Hz, which creates a rhythmic, pulsing modulation of another signal rather than being heard directly as a pitched tone. LFOs are a fundamental tool of electronic music synthesis and audio effects, and the term also refers in other fields to slow periodic variations, such as oscillations in electric power systems.

In music synthesis

On a synthesizer, an LFO can be routed to modulate parameters such as the pitch of an oscillator (producing vibrato), the cutoff frequency of a filter (producing wah-like sweeps), or amplitude (producing tremolo). LFO waveforms typically include sine, triangle, sawtooth, and square waves; some instruments allow the LFO rate itself to be modulated or synced to tempo. The technique was standard on modular and analogue synthesizers of the 1960s–1970s and remains central to sound design and electronic dance music, where slowly evolving modulations shape pads, basses, and rhythmic effects.

In power systems

In electrical engineering, low-frequency oscillations are poorly damped power oscillations, typically between about 0.1 and 2 Hz, that can arise between generators or regions of an interconnected power grid. Inter-area oscillations can limit power transfer capacity and, if uncontrolled, threaten system stability, so grid operators monitor them with phasor measurement units and counter them with power system stabilisers.
