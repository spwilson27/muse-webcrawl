Entr'acte

Entr'acte (pronounced [ɑ̃.tʁakt]), French for "between the acts", means an interval between two parts of a stage production, or music performed during such an interval. In the theatre of the 18th and 19th centuries, and in opera and ballet, composers supplied entr'acte music to fill scene changes and intermissions, keeping the audience entertained while scenery was moved.

In theatre and opera

Orchestral entr'actes became substantial compositions in their own right: the entr'actes of Schubert's Rosamunde, Mendelssohn's music for A Midsummer Night's Dream, and Bizet's for Carmen are frequently played in concert. In French grand opera, an entr'acte might also include a ballet divertissement. In musical theatre the term survives for the overture to the second act, which reintroduces the show's themes after the interval.

Entr'acte (1924 film)

Entr'acte is also the title of a famous 1924 French Dadaist short film directed by René Clair and written by Francis Picabia, made to be shown during the intermission of the ballet Relâche at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées. With a score by Erik Satie and appearances by Picabia, Marcel Duchamp and Man Ray playing chess on a rooftop, the film strings together surreal, comic episodes — a slow-motion funeral procession that accelerates into a chase — and is considered a landmark of avant-garde cinema.
