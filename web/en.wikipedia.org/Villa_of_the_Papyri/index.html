The Villa of the Papyri (Italian: Villa dei Papiri) was a luxurious private residence in the ancient Roman town of Herculaneum, buried in the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 CE. It is named after its unique library of more than 1,800 carbonised papyrus scrolls — the only library to survive intact from the ancient Greco-Roman world.

Excavation and collection

The villa was discovered by well-diggers in 1750 and explored through tunnels under the Bourbon kings of Naples. Excavations recovered an extraordinary collection of bronze and marble statues, busts of Greek philosophers and statesmen, frescoes, and mosaics, now largely in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples. The villa, stretching some 250 metres along the ancient shoreline, included gardens, pools, and a large peristyle; it is commonly attributed to Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, father-in-law of Julius Caesar and patron of the Epicurean philosopher Philodemus.

The papyri

The scrolls, carbonised by the eruption's heat, are extremely fragile. Early attempts to unroll them destroyed many; modern methods — careful mechanical unrolling and, since the 2010s, X-ray phase-contrast tomography and "virtual unwrapping" — have recovered texts, mostly Epicurean philosophical works by Philodemus of Gadara. In 2023–24, machine-learning analysis of CT scans in the Vesuvius Challenge read substantial passages from a scroll without opening it.

Legacy

The J. Paul Getty Museum's Getty Villa in California is modelled on the Villa of the Papyri's plan. Much of the Herculaneum villa remains unexcavated underground.
