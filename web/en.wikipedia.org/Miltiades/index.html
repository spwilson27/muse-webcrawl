Miltiades the Younger (c. 550 – 489 BC) was an Athenian general and statesman, best remembered for commanding the Athenian and Plataean forces that defeated the Persians at the Battle of Marathon in 490 BC, during the first Persian invasion of Greece. Before that, he had ruled as tyrant of the Thracian Chersonese, a family possession, and had campaigned with Darius I of Persia before joining the Ionian Revolt's aftermath and returning to Athens.

Early life and the Chersonese

Miltiades belonged to the wealthy Philaid family, rivals of the Peisistratids; his father Cimon Coalemos was a famed Olympic chariot winner, and his uncle Miltiades the Elder had founded an Athenian colony in the Thracian Chersonese (the Gallipoli peninsula), which controlled the Hellespont grain route. Sent out by the Peisistratids around 516 BC, the younger Miltiades succeeded to the tyranny there. In 513 BC he accompanied Darius on his Scythian expedition and — according to Herodotus — proposed (unsuccessfully) that the Ionians destroy the Danube bridge and strand the Persian king. After the failed Ionian Revolt he fled Persian retaliation, losing one son captured, and reached Athens in 493 BC, where he survived a trial for tyranny in the Chersonese.

Marathon

Elected one of the ten strategoi for 490 BC, Miltiades persuaded the polemarch Callimachus to give battle rather than wait behind Athens' walls as a Persian expedition under Datis and Artaphernes landed at Marathon. In the ensuing battle the Athenians, with 1,000 Plataeans, charged the larger Persian force at a run, strengthened their wings at the expense of the centre, and enveloped the Persian troops that broke through — killing some 6,400 Persians for 192 Athenian dead, according to Herodotus. Marathon became the founding victory of Athenian democratic confidence.

Fall and death

Given a fleet of 70 ships in 489 BC, Miltiades failed in an expedition against Paros — possibly a private vendetta — and returned wounded. His enemy Xanthippus prosecuted him for deceiving the people; he was fined 50 talents and, unable to pay, died in prison of his wound (gangrene, according to later accounts). His son Cimon paid the fine and went on to lead Athens. Modern historians debate how much of the Marathon plan was truly Miltiades's, but the ancient tradition unanimously credits him as the architect of the victory.
