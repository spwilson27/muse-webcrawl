A prairie is a type of temperate grassland ecosystem found principally in the interior of North America, characterised by flat or gently rolling terrain, moderate rainfall, and vegetation dominated by grasses, herbs and shrubs rather than trees. The word comes from the French prairie, meaning meadow.

Types

North American prairies are commonly divided into tallgrass prairie in the wetter east, mixed-grass prairie in the central plains, and shortgrass prairie in the drier west near the Rocky Mountain rain shadow. In Canada, the Prairie Provinces of Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba contain extensive prairie and parkland regions.

Ecology

Prairies are maintained by a combination of climate, grazing and fire. Historically, vast herds of American bison grazed the plains, and periodic fires—both natural and set by Indigenous peoples—suppressed woody growth. Prairie soils are among the deepest and richest in the world, formed over millennia from the decomposition of dense grass root systems.

Agriculture and loss

Because of its fertile soil, most of the tallgrass prairie was converted to farmland after European settlement, making it one of the most endangered ecosystems in North America; less than a few percent of the original tallgrass prairie remains. Surviving remnants are protected in reserves such as the Tallgrass Prairie Preserve in Oklahoma and Konza Prairie in Kansas.
