Entoptic phenomenon

An entoptic phenomenon is a visual effect whose source is within the eye itself rather than in the external scene. Because the structures causing the effect lie in the optical path or retina, the perceptions can reveal features of the viewer's own visual system.

Examples

Common examples include floaters—shadows of fibers or cells in the vitreous humor; the blue field entoptic phenomenon, in which white blood cells in retinal capillaries appear as bright dots against a blue field; and Haidinger's brush, a faint bow-tie pattern linked to polarization sensitivity.

Perception and testing

Entoptic images can be stabilized or made visible by special lighting, eye movements, or uniform backgrounds. Clinicians and vision scientists use some effects to assess retinal circulation and macular pigment, while artists and psychologists have long used them to study perception.

Distinction from illusions

Unlike many optical illusions, entoptic phenomena have a direct anatomical source inside the eye. They are distinct from phosphenes produced by pressure or electrical stimulation and from hallucinations generated by the brain. See also floater and retina.
