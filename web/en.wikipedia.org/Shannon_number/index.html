Shannon number

The Shannon number, named after the American mathematician Claude Shannon, is a conservative lower bound of the game-tree complexity of chess. Shannon calculated it to be approximately 10120 in his landmark 1950 paper "Programming a Computer for Playing Chess".

Calculation

Shannon based his estimate on an average of about 103 possibilities per pair of moves (one move by White followed by one by Black), and a typical game lasting about 40 such pairs of moves. Thus 103 raised to the power of 40 gives roughly 10120 possible games. More refined later estimates place the number of "sensible" chess games somewhat lower, but still astronomically large.

Significance

The Shannon number illustrates the intractability of solving chess by brute-force search: the number dwarfs the estimated number of atoms in the observable universe (around 1080). It motivated the development of selective search heuristics and, eventually, the evaluation functions used by modern chess engines.

Comparison

By comparison, the game-tree complexity of Go is vastly larger still, often estimated near 10360, while simpler games such as tic-tac-toe have tiny game trees that can be enumerated completely.
