Bid–ask spread

The bid–ask spread (also bid–offer or bid/ask and buy/sell in the case of a market maker) is the difference between the prices quoted (either by a single market maker or in a limit order book) for an immediate sale (offer) and an immediate purchase (bid) for stocks, futures contracts, options, or currency pairs such as forex. The size of the bid–ask spread in a security is one measure of the liquidity of the market and of the size of the transaction cost.

Quoted bid and ask prices; their difference is the spread.

Definition

If a trade is executed at quoted prices, the trade cost (in the absence of commissions) is the difference between the amount paid for the asset and the amount received if the asset were immediately sold again — the round-trip cost. The quoted spread is the difference between the highest bid price and the lowest ask price among market makers for a given security.

Example

If the bid price for a stock is $19.90 and the ask price is $20.00, the bid–ask spread is $0.10. A trader buying at the market pays $20.00 and, selling immediately, receives $19.90. Spreads are often expressed as a percentage of the midpoint price; here the spread is about 0.5% of the $19.95 midpoint.

Determinants

Spreads widen when a security is thinly traded, volatile, or risky to hold in inventory, and narrow in deep, liquid markets where competition among market makers is intense. Market makers earn the spread as compensation for providing liquidity and bearing adverse-selection and inventory risk.

Types of spread

The quoted spread is the difference between the best available bid and ask. The effective spread compares the actual execution price with the midpoint of the quotes at the time of the order, and can be smaller than the quoted spread when trades occur inside the quotes. The realized spread measures the market maker's revenue after prices adjust to the information content of the trade.
