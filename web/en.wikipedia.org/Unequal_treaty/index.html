Unequal treaty
Region: East and Southeast Asia, Ottoman Empire
Period: 19th–early 20th century
Features: Extraterritoriality, treaty ports, fixed tariffs

An unequal treaty is a treaty imposed by a stronger power on a weaker state on terms highly favourable to the stronger party, typically involving loss of tariff autonomy, extraterritorial legal rights for foreigners, and the opening of treaty ports. The term is most often applied to agreements imposed on Qing-dynasty China, Joseon Korea, Tokugawa and Meiji Japan, Siam, and the Ottoman Empire by Western powers in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

China

The first of China's unequal treaties was the Treaty of Nanking (1842), ending the First Opium War; it ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain and opened five treaty ports. It was followed by the treaties of Tientsin, the Convention of Peking, the Treaty of Shimonoseki with Japan, and the Boxer Protocol of 1901. In Chinese historical memory the period is remembered as the "century of humiliation". Most foreign privileges were relinquished during the Second World War, and Hong Kong was returned in 1997.

Japan and Korea

Japan was opened by the Convention of Kanagawa (1854) and the Harris Treaty (1858); the Meiji government spent decades renegotiating, recovering tariff autonomy by 1911. Korea's Treaty of Ganghwa (1876) with Japan is likewise classed as unequal.
