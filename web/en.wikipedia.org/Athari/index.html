Athari

Athari (Arabic: أثري), or traditionalist theology, is a movement among Sunni Muslim scholars who advocated a literalist interpretation of the Quran and hadith regarding the names and attributes of God, rejecting rationalistic theological speculation (kalām) as practised by the Ash'ari and Maturidi schools. The name derives from athar, meaning "tradition" or "narration" — that is, adherence to what has been transmitted from the Prophet and the early generations (salaf).

Beliefs

Atharis affirm the divine attributes mentioned in scripture — such as God's hand, face, and establishment above the Throne — as real and befitting God, while consigning their exact modality to God (tafwīḍ) or affirming their apparent meaning without likening God to creation. Their slogan regarding such texts is to accept them bilā kayf, "without [asking] how". On questions such as whether the Quran is created, they follow Ahmad ibn Hanbal's position that it is the uncreated speech of God.

History

The approach originated among the early ahl al-ḥadīth (people of hadith) in the 8th–9th centuries CE, in opposition to Mu'tazilite rationalism; Ahmad ibn Hanbal's steadfastness during the Mihna (inquisition) made him its emblematic figure. Later scholars such as Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) and Ibn Qayyim systematised traditionalist arguments while engaging philosophical theology on its own terms. In the modern era, Athari creed is closely associated with the Hanbali school and with Salafi movements, though its adherents historically were found across the legal schools.
