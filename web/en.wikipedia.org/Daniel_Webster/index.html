Daniel Webster

Daniel Webster (January 18, 1782 – October 24, 1852) was an American lawyer and statesman who represented New Hampshire and Massachusetts in the United States Congress and served as Secretary of State under Presidents William Henry Harrison, John Tyler and Millard Fillmore. Regarded as one of the greatest orators in American history, he formed, with Henry Clay and John C. Calhoun, the "Great Triumvirate" that dominated national politics for decades.

Daniel Webster in 1849.

Early life and legal career

Webster was born in Salisbury, New Hampshire, educated at Phillips Exeter Academy and Dartmouth College, and admitted to the bar in 1805. As a lawyer he argued landmark cases before the Supreme Court, including Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819), McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) and Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), helping to establish federal supremacy and broad congressional power over commerce.

Congressional career

Webster served in the House of Representatives from New Hampshire (1813–1817) and from Massachusetts (1823–1827), and in the Senate for most of the years from 1827 until his death. His Second Reply to Hayne (1830), answering the doctrine of nullification during the Webster–Hayne debate, closed with the famous peroration "Liberty and Union, now and forever, one and inseparable!" and became a canonical statement of American nationalism. A leader of the Whig Party, he sought the presidency several times without success. His support for the Compromise of 1850 — above all his Seventh of March speech defending it — won the Union a decade of respite but destroyed his standing with Northern abolitionists.

Secretary of State

As Secretary of State, Webster negotiated the Webster–Ashburton Treaty of 1842, settling the boundary between Maine and British North America, and handled the Caroline and Creole affairs with Britain. Returning to the post under Fillmore in 1850, he enforced the Fugitive Slave Act, a stance that further alienated antislavery opinion. He died at his home in Marshfield, Massachusetts, in October 1852; his last words are reported as "I still live."
