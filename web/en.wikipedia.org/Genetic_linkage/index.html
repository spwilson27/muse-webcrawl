Genetic linkage

Genetic linkage is the tendency of DNA sequences that are close together on a chromosome to be inherited together during the meiosis phase of sexual reproduction. Two genetic markers that are physically near to each other are less likely to be separated onto different chromatids during chromosomal crossover, and are therefore said to be more linked than markers that are far apart. In other words, the nearer two genes are on a chromosome, the lower the chance of recombination between them, and the more likely they are to be inherited together. Markers on different chromosomes are completely unlinked.

Linkage mapping
Crossing over can separate linked loci; frequency rises with distance

Discovery

Linkage was first described by William Bateson, Edith Rebecca Saunders and Reginald Punnett in sweet peas, and was placed on a firm chromosomal basis by Thomas Hunt Morgan's group working with the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster. Morgan's student Alfred Sturtevant, as an undergraduate in 1911, realised that recombination frequencies could be used to order genes linearly, constructing the first genetic map. Genes arranged on the same chromosome form a linkage group; the number of linkage groups equals the haploid chromosome number.

Recombination frequency and map units

The recombination frequency between two loci is measured in centimorgans (cM; also called map units): a frequency of 1% corresponds to 1 cM. Loci 50 cM or more apart behave as unlinked, assorting independently. Because double crossovers can restore parental combinations, recombination frequency underestimates true distance for widely separated loci, and mapping functions such as Haldane's and Kosambi's correct for this.

Linkage analysis

In humans and other organisms where experimental crosses are impractical, linkage is assessed by the LOD score (logarithm of the odds), a statistical method developed by Newton Morton. Linkage analysis in families enabled the mapping of many Mendelian disease genes — including those for Huntington's disease and cystic fibrosis — before the responsible genes were identified by positional cloning. Genome-wide association studies exploit linkage disequilibrium, the non-random association of alleles in populations, rather than linkage within families.
