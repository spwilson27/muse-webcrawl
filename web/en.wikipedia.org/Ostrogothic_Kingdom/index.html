The Ostrogothic Kingdom, officially the Kingdom of Italy (Regnum Italiae), was established by the Ostrogoths in Italy and neighboring areas from 493 to 553, after Theodoric the Great defeated Odoacer, the first barbarian king of Italy, and took Ravenna.

Reign of Theodoric

Theodoric (r. 493–526), who had been raised as a hostage in Constantinople, ruled Italy with the nominal sanction of the Eastern Roman emperor. He preserved the Roman Senate, administration, and law for his Roman subjects, while the Goths formed a military caste. His court at Ravenna patronized learning — his ministers included Boethius and Cassiodorus — and undertook major building works, including the Mausoleum of Theodoric and the Arian church now known as La Rotonda. Religious tension between the Arian Goths and Catholic Romans, and suspicion of treason late in his reign, led to the execution of Boethius in 524.

Decline and Gothic War

After Theodoric's death in 526, the kingdom weakened under his daughter Amalasuintha and a succession of contested rulers. The Eastern emperor Justinian I used the dynastic crisis as a pretext for reconquest. In 535 his general Belisarius invaded, beginning the devastating Gothic War (535–554). Under kings Totila and Teia, the Goths resisted fiercely, but by 553 organized Ostrogothic resistance had collapsed, and Italy was reincorporated into the Byzantine Empire — albeit ruined by two decades of war and plague.

Legacy

The Ostrogothic Kingdom is often seen as an attempt to fuse Roman civilization with Gothic kingship. Its legal codes, administrative continuity, and monuments in Ravenna remain key evidence for the transformation of the Roman world in late antiquity.
