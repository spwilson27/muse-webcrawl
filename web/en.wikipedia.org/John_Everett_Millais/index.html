Sir John Everett Millais, 1st Baronet (8 June 1829 – 13 August 1896) was an English painter and illustrator, a founding member of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, and later President of the Royal Academy.

Early life

A child prodigy from Jersey, Millais entered the Royal Academy Schools at age eleven, its youngest ever student, and won its gold medal. In 1848, with William Holman Hunt and Dante Gabriel Rossetti, he founded the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, advocating a return to the detail, intense colour and truth to nature of art before Raphael.

Pre-Raphaelite works

His Christ in the House of His Parents (1849–50) provoked outrage — Charles Dickens attacked it in print — for its unidealised realism. Ophelia (1851–52), showing Shakespeare's heroine floating in a river, modelled by Elizabeth Siddal posing in a bathtub, became one of the most famous British paintings of the century. Millais's personal life intersected with the critic John Ruskin: Millais fell in love with Ruskin's wife Effie Gray while painting Ruskin's portrait in Scotland; Effie's marriage was annulled and she married Millais in 1855.

Later career

From the 1860s Millais's style broadened, influenced by Whistler and Velázquez, and he became Britain's most popular and commercially successful painter, celebrated for narrative pictures such as The Boyhood of Raleigh, portraits, and Bubbles (1886), famously used to advertise Pears soap. He was a prolific illustrator for Trollope and periodicals. Created a baronet in 1885, he was elected President of the Royal Academy a few months before his death from throat cancer in 1896, and was buried in St Paul's Cathedral.
