Bitter orange

The bitter orange, also called sour orange, Seville orange, or bigarade orange, is the citrus tree Citrus × aurantium and its fruit. It is a hybrid between the pomelo and the mandarin orange, native to Southeast Asia and spread around the Mediterranean by Arab traders and later to the Americas by the Spanish.

Description

The tree is evergreen with fragrant white flowers and rough, deep-orange fruit. The fruit is too sour and bitter to eat raw for most people, owing to its high acidity and bitter compounds such as neohesperidin.

Uses

Seville oranges are the classic fruit for British marmalade. The peel yields essential oils used in perfumery (neroli from the flowers, petitgrain from the leaves and twigs) and as a flavouring in liqueurs such as Curaçao and Grand Marnier. The juice is used in cooking, notably in Cuban mojo sauce, and the dried peel is used in Belgian witbier and in herbal supplements, where its stimulant compound synephrine has raised safety questions.

Cultivation

Bitter orange is hardier than the sweet orange and is widely used as a rootstock for other citrus, though susceptibility to the tristeza virus has reduced this use. Seville's streets are famously lined with bitter orange trees.
