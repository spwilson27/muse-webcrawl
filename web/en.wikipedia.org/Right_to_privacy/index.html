Right to privacy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The right to privacy is an element of various legal traditions that intends to restrain governmental and private actions that threaten the privacy of individuals. Over 150 national constitutions mention the right to privacy. Article 12 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and Article 17 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights protect against arbitrary interference with privacy, family, home, and correspondence.

History

The modern concept is often traced to Samuel D. Warren and Louis Brandeis's 1890 Harvard Law Review article "The Right to Privacy", which argued for a right "to be let alone" in response to intrusive newspaper photography. William Prosser later catalogued four privacy torts: intrusion upon seclusion, public disclosure of private facts, false light, and appropriation of name or likeness.

United States

The U.S. Constitution does not mention privacy expressly, but the Supreme Court has located privacy protections in the "penumbras" of the Bill of Rights, beginning with Griswold v. Connecticut (1965). The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, and Katz v. United States (1967) framed the test as a reasonable expectation of privacy. Statutes such as the Privacy Act of 1974 and sectoral laws regulate government and commercial data handling.

Digital privacy

Mass data collection by governments and platforms has made informational privacy a central policy issue. The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (2016/679), enforceable from 2018, grants individuals rights of access, rectification, and erasure over personal data and is a model for laws such as California's CCPA.
