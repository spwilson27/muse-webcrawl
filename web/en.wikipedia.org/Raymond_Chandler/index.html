Raymond Thornton Chandler (23 July 1888 – 26 March 1959) was an American-British novelist and screenwriter, one of the founders of the hardboiled school of detective fiction. His private-eye hero Philip Marlowe — a wisecracking but incorruptible knight moving "down these mean streets" — set the template for the modern fictional detective, and his lyrical, simile-laden prose elevated crime writing into literature.

Life and career

Born in Chicago and educated at Dulwich College in London, Chandler worked as a civil servant, journalist and oil-company executive in Los Angeles, losing his job in the Great Depression. At forty-four he turned to pulp fiction, publishing his first story in Black Mask in 1933. His first novel, The Big Sleep (1939), introduced Marlowe, followed by Farewell, My Lovely (1940), The High Window (1942) and The Lady in the Lake (1943).

In Hollywood he co-wrote Billy Wilder's Double Indemnity (1944), earning an Academy Award nomination, and worked, contentiously, with Alfred Hitchcock on Strangers on a Train (1951). His essay "The Simple Art of Murder" (1944) remains a classic defence of realistic crime fiction. The late novel The Long Goodbye (1953), a melancholy meditation on friendship and betrayal, won the Edgar Award and is often considered his finest. Devastated by his wife Cissy's death in 1954, Chandler sank into alcoholism and died in 1959.
