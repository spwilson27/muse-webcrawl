Hearing aid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hearing aid is a small electronic device worn in or behind the ear that makes sounds louder so that a person with hearing loss can listen, communicate, and participate more fully in daily activities. A hearing aid consists of a microphone, an amplifier (now usually a digital signal processor), and a speaker (receiver). It can help people in both quiet and noisy situations, though it does not restore normal hearing.

Types

Styles include behind-the-ear (BTE), receiver-in-canal (RIC), in-the-ear (ITE), in-the-canal (ITC), and completely-in-canal (CIC) devices, as well as bone-anchored hearing systems for conductive losses. Modern digital aids are programmed to the individual's audiogram, with directional microphones, feedback cancellation, noise reduction, telecoils, and Bluetooth streaming from phones. In the United States, a 2022 FDA rule created an over-the-counter category for adults with perceived mild-to-moderate hearing loss, lowering prices and expanding access.

History

Non-electric aids such as ear trumpets were used from the seventeenth century. The first electric hearing aid, the Akouphone (1898), used a carbon microphone; vacuum-tube aids appeared in the 1920s, transistor aids in the early 1950s made devices truly wearable, and fully digital hearing aids reached the market in the mid-1990s. Despite their benefit, only a minority of people who could benefit from hearing aids use them, owing to cost, stigma, and fit difficulties — a concern given links between untreated hearing loss and cognitive decline and dementia risk.
