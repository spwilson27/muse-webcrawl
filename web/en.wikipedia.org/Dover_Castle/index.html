Dover Castle is a medieval castle in Dover, Kent, England, described as the "Key to England" owing to its defensive significance throughout history. Founded in the 11th century and rebuilt extensively under Henry II in the 1180s, it is one of the largest castles in England. The site may have been fortified since the Iron Age; it contains one of the best-preserved Roman lighthouses (pharos) in Britain and the Anglo-Saxon church of St Mary in Castro.

History

William the Conqueror strengthened the defences at Dover after the Battle of Hastings in 1066. Henry II built the present great keep (Great Tower) between 1179 and 1188, with inner and outer curtain walls. During the First Barons' War, Prince Louis of France besieged the castle in 1216 but Hubert de Burgh held out, one of the few places in England to resist. The castle was besieged again during the Second Barons' War (1265).

Napoleonic and modern era

During the Napoleonic Wars, extensive tunnels were dug into the chalk cliffs to house troops. In the Second World War the tunnels became the naval headquarters from which the Dunkirk evacuation (Operation Dynamo) was directed in May–June 1940 under Vice-Admiral Bertram Ramsay. During the Cold War the tunnels formed a Regional Seat of Government. The castle came into the care of English Heritage in 1984 and is a Scheduled Monument and Grade I listed site.

Layout

The castle occupies a commanding position on the White Cliffs above the port of Dover. Features include the Great Tower, the medieval underground works and siege tunnels, the Constable's Gate, the Princess of Wales's Royal Regiment Museum, and the Secret Wartime Tunnels visitor attraction.
