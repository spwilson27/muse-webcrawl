The Vitruvian Man (Italian: L'uomo vitruviano) is a drawing by Leonardo da Vinci, dated to about 1490, accompanied by notes based on the work of the ancient Roman architect Vitruvius. It depicts a nude man in two superimposed positions with arms and legs apart, inscribed within both a circle and a square — the ideal human body proportions as described in Vitruvius's De architectura. The drawing exemplifies Leonardo's fusion of art and science and the Renaissance ideal of man as the measure of all things.

Proportions

Leonardo's accompanying text (written in mirror script) lists relationships such as: the length of a man's outspread arms equals his height; four fingers make a palm, four palms a foot; the navel is the centre of the circle described by the outstretched limbs. Leonardo refined Vitruvius's scheme, shifting the centres of the circle (navel) and square (genitals) to make the geometry consistent.

History and influence

The sheet is kept in the Gallerie dell'Accademia in Venice and, being fragile, is rarely exhibited. It has become one of the most reproduced images in the world — appearing on the Italian 1-euro coin, in logos, and countless scientific and medical publications — as a universal symbol of humanism, anatomy, and the harmony of mathematics and nature.
