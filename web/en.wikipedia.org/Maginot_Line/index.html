The Maginot Line (French: Ligne Maginot) is a line of concrete fortifications, obstacles, and weapons installations built by France in the 1930s to deter invasion by Nazi Germany and to force any attacker into a longer, more easily resisted route. It is named after the French Minister of War André Maginot. The line ran along France's border with Germany and Luxembourg, with a separate Alpine Line facing Italy; weaker defences continued along the Belgian border.

The fortifications did not prevent the defeat of France in 1940: the German army invaded Belgium, Netherlands, and Luxembourg, bypassing the strongest works through the Ardennes, which French planners had judged impassable to a large mechanised force. "Maginot Line" has since become a byword for expensive defensive preparations that fail because they are oriented toward the wrong threat.

Design

The line's principal works, the ouvrages, were self-contained underground fortresses housing hundreds to over a thousand men, with artillery and machine-gun turrets, deep galleries, power plants, hospitals, and narrow-gauge railways, protected by metres of concrete and earth. Between the ouvrages stood interval casemates, anti-tank obstacles (including fields of upright rails), and inundation zones. The northeast sector facing Germany was by far the strongest; the extension along Belgium was lighter and incomplete.

Strategic rationale

France's strategy was shaped by the losses of the First World War and by demographics: France had a much smaller population than Germany. The Line would protect the industrial regions of Alsace and Lorraine, economise manpower, channel a German attack through Belgium — where French and British forces planned to advance to meet it — and buy time for mobilisation. Contrary to a common caricature, French planners did not expect the Line alone to win a war; they expected the decisive battle to be fought by mobile forces in Belgium.

The 1940 campaign

In May 1940, German forces attacked through the Netherlands and Belgium as expected, but the main blow (the Sichelschnitt plan) fell through the Ardennes forest, crossing the Meuse at Sedan and cutting off the Allied armies that had advanced into Belgium. Most Maginot ouvrages were never seriously engaged; some Alpine positions successfully resisted Italian attacks, and several works in the northeast fought effectively before surrendering after the armistice of 22 June 1940, under orders from the French government.

Legacy

After the war some ouvrages were reactivated during the Cold War, and a few were converted to other uses, including a mushroom farm and command facilities. Major works such as Hackenberg, Fermont, and Schoenenbourg are now museums. The expression "Maginot mentality" entered usage to describe a defensive, backward-looking mindset.
