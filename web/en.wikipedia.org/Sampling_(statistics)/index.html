Sampling (statistics)

In statistics, sampling is the selection of a subset of individuals or observations (a sample) from within a statistical population to estimate characteristics of the whole population. Sampling allows researchers to draw conclusions about populations that are too large, costly, or impractical to measure completely, and under good design can yield estimates with quantifiable accuracy.

Rationale

A well-chosen sample can be far cheaper and faster than a census while being nearly as accurate — and in destructive testing (for example, crash tests or quality control of perishable goods) sampling is the only option. The mathematical foundations of sampling rest on probability theory, which lets statisticians calculate how closely sample statistics are expected to match population parameters.

Probability sampling

In probability sampling every population member has a known, non-zero chance of selection. Main designs include:

Simple random sampling — each member has an equal chance, chosen by a random process.
Stratified sampling — the population is divided into strata (e.g. age groups or regions) and sampled within each, improving precision and guaranteeing representation.
Cluster sampling — groups (clusters) such as schools or city blocks are randomly selected and members within them surveyed; cheaper for geographically spread populations.
Systematic sampling — every kth member from a list is selected after a random start.

Non-probability sampling

Convenience, quota, purposive, and snowball sampling do not give every member a known chance of selection. They are cheaper and sometimes the only option for hard-to-reach populations, but they risk bias and do not support valid margin-of-error calculations.

Errors and bias

Sampling error is the random difference between sample and population values, which shrinks as sample size grows. Bias is systematic error: selection bias from an unrepresentative frame, non-response bias when respondents differ from non-respondents, and measurement bias from faulty instruments. A sample drawn from an incomplete sampling frame (for example, telephone polls that miss mobile-only households) can be badly wrong — as in the famous 1936 Literary Digest poll failure.

Sample size

The required sample size depends on the desired precision (margin of error), confidence level, and population variability; beyond a few thousand respondents, gains in accuracy are typically small for large populations. Weighting adjustments are often applied so that sample demographics match the population.
