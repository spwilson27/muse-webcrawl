Common-pool resource

A common-pool resource (CPR) is a type of good consisting of a natural or human-made resource system — such as a fishery, irrigation system, grazing ground, or aquifer — whose size or characteristics make it costly to exclude potential beneficiaries, but whose use by one person subtracts from what is available to others. CPRs thus combine the rivalry of private goods with the difficult exclusion of public goods.

The commons problem

Garrett Hardin's 1968 essay "The Tragedy of the Commons" argued that without regulation, rational users would inevitably overexploit such resources. Elinor Ostrom's later fieldwork, summarised in Governing the Commons (1990), showed that communities worldwide have devised durable institutions — clear boundaries, locally adapted rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions, and conflict-resolution mechanisms — to manage CPRs sustainably, work for which she received the 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.

Examples

Classic studied cases include the alpine meadows of Törbel, Switzerland, the huerta irrigation systems of Valencia, Spain, and inshore fisheries in Maine and Japan. Modern CPRs include the electromagnetic spectrum, groundwater basins, and the atmosphere's capacity to absorb greenhouse gases.
