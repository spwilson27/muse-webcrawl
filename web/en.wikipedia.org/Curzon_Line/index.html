Curzon Line

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Curzon Line was a demarcation line between the Second Polish Republic and Soviet Russia proposed in 1919–1920 by Lord Curzon, the British Foreign Secretary. It became a byword for the territorial settlement of Eastern Europe, and after the Second World War it formed the basis, with minor adjustments, for the modern eastern border of Poland.

Origins

Following the First World War and the collapse of the German, Austro-Hungarian and Russian empires, Poland's eastern frontier was unsettled. On 8 December 1919 the Allied Supreme Council declared a provisional line based broadly on the ethnic division between Poles and Ukrainians and Belarusians. In July 1920, as the Red Army advanced during the Polish–Soviet War, Lord Curzon's note to Moscow proposed an armistice along this line; the Soviets rejected it, and after Poland's victory the Treaty of Riga (1921) set the border well to the east of the line.

The Second World War settlement

The secret protocol of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of 1939 placed the partition of Poland roughly along the Curzon Line. At Tehran (1943) and Yalta (1945) the Allies agreed that Poland's eastern border should follow the line, with Poland compensated by German territory in the west. Between 1944 and 1948 some 1.5 million Poles were expelled or transferred westward from the territories east of the line, while Ukrainians, Belarusians and Lithuanians were moved eastward.

Legacy

The present borders of Poland with Lithuania, Belarus and Ukraine largely follow the Curzon Line, making it one of the most consequential boundary proposals of the twentieth century.
