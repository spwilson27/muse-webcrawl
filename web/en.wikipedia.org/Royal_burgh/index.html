Royal burgh

A royal burgh was a type of Scottish burgh which had been founded by, or subsequently granted, a royal charter. Although abolished by law in 1975, the term is still used by many former royal burghs for ceremonial purposes. At abolition there were 66 royal burghs, most of which dated from the medieval period, when the status conferred important trading privileges.

Origins and privileges

The first royal burghs were created by David I in the 12th century as part of his programme of economic and administrative reform. Royal burghs held their land directly from the Crown, enjoyed a monopoly of foreign trade within their liberties, could levy tolls and hold markets, and sent representatives to the Parliament of Scotland. Their councils elected a provost and bailies, and the burghs collectively formed one of the Three Estates of the realm. The Convention of Royal Burghs, meeting from the 16th century, coordinated their interests and settled disputes.

Abolition and legacy

Exclusive trading privileges faded in significance by the 19th century, and successive reforms unified burgh government. The Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973 abolished burgh councils in 1975. Former royal burghs retain the style in local identity, flags and ceremonies, and some continue to elect honorary provosts or preserve common good funds — property held for the benefit of the burgh's inhabitants under the old law.
