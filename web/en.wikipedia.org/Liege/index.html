Liege

Liege (French: Liège; Dutch: Luik; Walloon: Lîdje) is a city and municipality in eastern Belgium, the capital of the province of Liège in Wallonia. Situated on the river Meuse near the Dutch and German borders, it has long been a major economic and cultural centre of the Meuse valley. This article follows the unaccented spelling used for the headword.

History

The city grew around the shrine of Saint Lambert, martyred there around 705. From the late 10th century it was the seat of a prince-bishopric whose rulers governed a substantial territory of the Holy Roman Empire for eight centuries. Liège prospered through coal mining, metallurgy, and arms manufacture, becoming one of continental Europe's earliest industrial centres. The Liège Revolution of 1789 overthrew the prince-bishop, and the principality was absorbed into France, then the Netherlands, and finally Belgium in 1830.

Modern city

Liège's heavy industry declined in the later 20th century, but the city remains a river port — among Europe's largest inland ports — and a hub for logistics, steel, and space technology. Landmarks include the Palace of the Prince-Bishops, the Gothic St Paul's Cathedral, the Montagne de Bueren staircase, and the Calatrava-designed Liège-Guillemins railway station (2009). The cycling classic Liège–Bastogne–Liège, one of cycling's five Monuments, finishes in the city.
