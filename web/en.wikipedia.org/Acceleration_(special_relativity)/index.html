Acceleration (special relativity)

In special relativity, acceleration is more subtle than in Newtonian mechanics because velocities cannot simply be added and the acceleration measured in one inertial frame depends on the velocity relative to that frame. The physically meaningful quantity is usually the proper acceleration: the acceleration measured in the object's instantaneous rest frame, which is what an accelerometer measures and what an occupant of a rocket feels.

Three-acceleration and four-acceleration

The ordinary (coordinate) three-acceleration a = dv/dt depends on the frame. If an object moves with velocity v and accelerates parallel to its motion, its acceleration in a frame where it moves at speed u is reduced by factors of the Lorentz factor γ: the longitudinal coordinate acceleration transforms with γ⁻³ and the transverse with γ⁻². This is why no amount of constant coordinate acceleration can push a massive object to the speed of light.

The four-acceleration is the derivative of the four-velocity with respect to proper time. It is a spacelike four-vector whose magnitude equals the proper acceleration, and it is always orthogonal to the four-velocity.

Constant proper acceleration

An object undergoing constant proper acceleration α follows a hyperbolic worldline, x(τ) tracing a hyperbola in spacetime — hence "hyperbolic motion". Its velocity approaches the speed of light asymptotically while its proper time advances ever more slowly relative to the inertial frame. A traveller accelerating at 1 g could, in principle, cross the galaxy within a human lifetime of ship time, though billions of years would pass on Earth.

Related effects

Uniformly accelerated observers perceive a Rindler horizon, and the analysis of accelerating frames underlies discussions of the twin paradox, Bell's spaceship paradox, and the Unruh effect in quantum field theory.
