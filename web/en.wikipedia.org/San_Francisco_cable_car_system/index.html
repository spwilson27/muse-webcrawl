San Francisco cable car system

The San Francisco cable car system is the world's last manually operated cable car network and an icon of San Francisco, California. An inventor, Andrew Smith Hallidie, opened the first cable line, the Clay Street Hill Railroad, in 1873, using wire rope of the kind manufactured by his father's firm. The system is operated today by the San Francisco Municipal Railway (Muni) and is a designated National Historic Landmark.

Operation

Cable cars are pulled by a continuously moving steel cable running in a conduit beneath the street, driven from a central powerhouse (now the Washington–Mason powerhouse, which also houses the Cable Car Museum). The gripman operates a lever that closes a grip on the cable to start the car and releases it to coast or brake; braking is by wheel brakes, track brakes and emergency systems. All cars travel at the cable speed of 9.5 mph. Two car types run: single-ended cars on the Powell Street lines, turned manually on turntables, and double-ended cars on the California Street line.

History

At its peak the network had 23 lines, but electric streetcars, the 1906 earthquake and the automobile reduced it drastically; a citizens' campaign led by Friedel Klussmann saved the remaining lines in 1947. A complete rebuild between 1982 and 1984 restored the system. The cable cars are now largely a tourist attraction — carrying millions of riders a year at a premium fare — and remain the only mobile National Historic Landmark in the United States.
