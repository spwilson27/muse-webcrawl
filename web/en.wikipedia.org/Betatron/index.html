TypeCyclic particle accelerator (electrons)

A betatron is a type of cyclic particle accelerator used to accelerate electrons (beta particles). It works like a transformer in which the electron beam circulating in a toroidal vacuum tube acts as the secondary winding: a changing magnetic flux through the orbit induces an electric field that accelerates the electrons, while the guide field holds them on a constant-radius path. The name combines "beta particle" (a fast electron) with the suffix of "cyclotron".

Principle

The key condition, derived by Rolf Wideröe in the 1920s, is the "betatron condition": the average magnetic field inside the orbit must be twice the field on the orbit, so that as the flux increases, the beam both gains energy and stays at fixed radius. Electrons injected at the start of each rising quarter-cycle of the alternating magnet current make hundreds of thousands of orbits before being extracted onto a target.

Development and use

Donald Kerst built the first successful betatron at the University of Illinois in 1940 (2.3 MeV), followed by much larger machines, including a 300 MeV instrument. Betatrons produced high-energy X-rays for industrial radiography and were widely used in mid-20th-century radiation therapy for cancer before linacs largely replaced them. The small oscillations of particles about the design orbit in accelerators generally are named betatron oscillations after this machine.
