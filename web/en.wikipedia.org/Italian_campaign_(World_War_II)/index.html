Italian campaign (World War II)

The Italian campaign of the Second World War was the series of Allied operations in and around Italy from 1943 to 1945. It began with the invasion of Sicily in July 1943, precipitated the fall of Benito Mussolini and Italy's surrender, and then developed into a long, gruelling advance up the Italian peninsula against determined German resistance, fought alongside the Italian resistance movement.

Invasion of Sicily and the fall of Mussolini

Operation Husky, launched on 9–10 July 1943, landed British and American forces in Sicily. The island was conquered in five weeks. On 25 July the Fascist Grand Council voted against Mussolini, who was arrested by order of King Victor Emmanuel III; Marshal Pietro Badoglio formed a new government. Secret negotiations produced the Armistice of Cassibile, announced on 8 September 1943, whereupon Germany executed Operation Achse, disarming Italian forces and occupying the north and centre of the country. Mussolini was rescued by German commandos and installed as head of the puppet Italian Social Republic at Salò.

The mainland campaign

Allied landings on the mainland began at Reggio Calabria, Taranto and, principally, Salerno (Operation Avalanche, 9 September 1943). The Germans under Field Marshal Albert Kesselring conducted a skillful delaying defence through a succession of fortified lines across Italy's mountainous spine. The Volturno, Barbara and Bernhardt lines gave way slowly before the main Gustav Line, anchored on Monte Cassino. Four battles raged around Cassino between January and May 1944; the historic abbey was destroyed by Allied bombing on 15 February. The January Anzio landing (Operation Shingle), meant to outflank the Gustav Line, was contained in its beachhead for months. Cassino finally fell in May 1944, and Rome was liberated on 4 June 1944, two days before D-Day in Normandy.

Gothic Line and final offensive

In the autumn of 1944 the Allies broke into the Gothic Line in the northern Apennines but were halted by winter weather and exhaustion. Behind the lines, Italian partisans waged a guerrilla war and, in April 1945, rose in the northern cities. The final Allied spring offensive (Operation Grapeshot) shattered the German front; Bologna, Genoa, Milan and Turin were liberated, Mussolini was captured and executed by partisans on 28 April 1945, and German forces in Italy surrendered at Caserta, effective 2 May 1945.

Legacy

The campaign cost several hundred thousand casualties on all sides and devastated much of Italy, but it knocked Italy out of the Axis, tied down German divisions, and opened Mediterranean supply routes. Italian troops who fought with the Allies as co-belligerents, and the resistance fighters commemorated each 25 April on Liberation Day, became central to the founding myth of the Italian Republic.
