Atmospheric entry


Plasma sheath around a re-entering capsule

Atmospheric entry (commonly called re-entry for returning spacecraft) is the movement of an object from outer space into and through the gases of a planet's, dwarf planet's, or natural satellite's atmosphere. Objects entering an atmosphere at orbital or interplanetary speeds encounter intense aerodynamic heating as kinetic energy is converted into heat in a shock layer of compressed, ionised gas.

Physics

A vehicle returning from low Earth orbit travels at about 7.8 km/s (Mach 25); a sample-return capsule from deep space may exceed 12 km/s. Because the heating rate rises steeply with velocity, blunt-body shapes are used to push the shock wave away from the surface and shed heat. The surrounding plasma can reach thousands of degrees and blocks radio signals, causing the familiar communications blackout. The entry corridor must be threaded precisely: too steep an entry causes unsurvivable deceleration or heating, too shallow and the vehicle may skip off the atmosphere back into space.

Thermal protection

Two main strategies protect vehicles. Heat sinks and, more commonly, ablative heat shields — such as the AVCOAT used on Apollo and Orion, or PICA used on SpaceX Dragon and the Stardust capsule — char and erode away, carrying heat with them. Reusable vehicles like the Space Shuttle used insulating tiles and reinforced carbon–carbon panels instead. The failure of such protection caused the loss of the Space Shuttle Columbia in 2003.

Applications

Atmospheric entry technology is essential for crewed spaceflight, sample-return missions (Stardust, Hayabusa, OSIRIS-REx), planetary landers that use aerobraking and aeroshells at Mars and Titan, ballistic missile warheads, and the disposal of space debris, most of which burns up on entry. Natural objects entering the atmosphere become meteors, and the rare large survivors reach the ground as meteorites.
