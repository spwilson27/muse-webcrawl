Corrective lens

A corrective lens is a transmissive optical device that is worn on or in front of (in the case of spectacles) or on the eye itself (in the case of contact lenses) to correct visual defects. Corrective lenses are prescribed by optometrists or ophthalmologists after an eye examination, and are used to treat refractive errors including myopia (near-sightedness), hyperopia (far-sightedness), astigmatism, and presbyopia (age-related loss of near focusing).

Types

Spherical lenses correct myopia (with diverging, minus-power lenses) and hyperopia (with converging, plus-power lenses). Cylindrical lenses correct astigmatism, in which the eye's optical power differs between meridians. For presbyopia, bifocal lenses combine two powers in one lens, trifocals three, and progressive lenses provide a continuous gradient of power from distance to near. Lens power is measured in dioptres. Modern lenses are most often made of plastic (such as CR-39 or polycarbonate) rather than glass, and may carry anti-reflective, scratch-resistant, photochromic or blue-light filtering coatings.

History

Wearable spectacles were invented in Italy in the late 13th century, initially using convex lenses to aid reading. Concave lenses for myopia appeared by the 16th century, and Benjamin Franklin is credited with popularising bifocals in the 1780s. Contact lenses, conceived by Leonardo da Vinci in 1508 and first successfully fitted in the late 19th century, became widespread with soft hydrogel lenses in the 1970s. Refractive surgery, such as LASIK, offers a permanent alternative to lenses for many patients.
