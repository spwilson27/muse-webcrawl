Kepler orbit

An elliptical Kepler orbit with eccentricity 0.7, showing the standard orbital elements.

In celestial mechanics, a Kepler orbit (or Keplerian orbit, named after the German astronomer Johannes Kepler) is the motion of one body relative to another, as an ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola, which forms a two-dimensional orbital plane in three-dimensional space. A Kepler orbit can also form a straight line. It considers only the point-like gravitational attraction of two bodies, neglecting perturbations due to gravitational interactions with other objects, atmospheric drag, solar radiation pressure, a non-spherical central body, and so on. It is thus said to be a solution of a special case of the two-body problem, known as the Kepler problem.

Introduction

As a theory in classical mechanics, Kepler orbits do not take into account the effects of general relativity. Actual orbits in real systems are typically well approximated by Keplerian elements computed for a given epoch (osculating orbits), which then evolve under perturbations. Kepler's laws of planetary motion, published in 1609 and 1619, describe the orbits of planets around the Sun and follow from Newton's law of universal gravitation.

Properties

The shape of a Kepler orbit is determined by its orbital eccentricity: ellipses for eccentricity less than 1 (bound orbits), parabolas for exactly 1, and hyperbolas for greater than 1 (unbound trajectories). Six orbital elements completely describe the orbit: the semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, longitude of the ascending node, argument of periapsis, and the position of the body along the orbit (true anomaly or mean anomaly at epoch). The position of the orbiting body as a function of time is found by solving Kepler's equation, which relates the mean anomaly to the eccentric anomaly and generally requires numerical methods.

r = a(1 − e²) / (1 + e cos θ)

where r is the distance from the central body, a the semi-major axis, e the eccentricity, and θ the true anomaly.

Mathematical formulation

Under Newton's law of universal gravitation, the two-body problem reduces to a one-body problem with an inverse-square central force, whose solutions are conic sections with the central mass at one focus. Conservation of energy and angular momentum classify the possible trajectories. For a body of negligible mass orbiting a body of mass M, the specific orbital energy determines whether the orbit is closed (negative energy, ellipse) or open (zero or positive energy, parabola or hyperbola).
