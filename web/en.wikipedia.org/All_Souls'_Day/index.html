All Souls' Day

All Souls' Day, also known as the Commemoration of All the Faithful Departed and the Day of the Dead, is a day of prayer and remembrance for the souls of those who have died, observed by Catholics and other Christian denominations annually on 2 November. In Western Christianity, it follows All Saints' Day (1 November) and completes the season sometimes called Allhallowtide.

The day is dedicated to prayer for the souls in purgatory, that they may be cleansed and admitted to heaven. The practice is associated in particular with Saint Odilo of Cluny, who in the late 10th and early 11th centuries instituted annual commemoration of the faithful departed in the Cluniac monasteries; the custom spread widely through the Church in the following centuries.

Observances

On All Souls' Day, the faithful traditionally attend Mass, visit cemeteries, and pray for deceased relatives and friends, often decorating graves with flowers and lighting candles. In many countries requiem Masses are celebrated and lists of the names of the dead are read aloud. Priests are traditionally permitted to celebrate three Masses on the day, a privilege extended by Pope Benedict XV in 1915.

In Mexico and other parts of Latin America, the commemoration overlaps with the customs of Día de los Muertos, the Day of the Dead, in which families build home altars (ofrendas) with photographs, food, and marigolds to welcome the spirits of their ancestors. In many European countries, cemeteries are filled with candlelight on the evening of All Souls' Day.
