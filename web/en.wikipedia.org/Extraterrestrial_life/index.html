Extraterrestrial life

Extraterrestrial life, or alien life, is life that does not originate from Earth. No extraterrestrial life has yet been conclusively detected, but the possibility is investigated by the sciences of astrobiology and xenobiology, and it has been a major theme of science fiction. Candidate life ranges from simple microorganisms to hypothetical intelligent civilizations capable of communication.

Scientific search

Within the Solar System, Mars, Jupiter's moon Europa, and Saturn's moons Enceladus and Titan are considered promising environments, past or present, because of evidence for liquid water or organic chemistry; missions and sample analyses search for biosignatures such as anomalous chemical disequilibria. Beyond the Solar System, thousands of exoplanets have been catalogued, and astronomers study the atmospheres of planets in habitable zones for gases like oxygen and methane that might indicate biology. The Drake equation organizes speculation about communicative civilizations, while the Fermi paradox asks why, if life is common, no evidence of it has been found. SETI programmes listen for artificial radio or optical signals; the "Wow!" signal of 1977 remains unexplained but unrepeated.

Assumptions and alternatives

Most searches assume life based on carbon chemistry in liquid water, because carbon forms versatile bonds and water is abundant; alternative biochemistries based on silicon or solvents such as methane and ammonia have been proposed. Earthly extremophiles — organisms thriving in boiling acid, deep rock, or intense radiation — have greatly widened the range of environments considered potentially habitable.
