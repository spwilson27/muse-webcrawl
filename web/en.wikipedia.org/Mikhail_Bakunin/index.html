Mikhail Alexandrovich Bakunin (30 May 1814 – 1 July 1876) was a Russian revolutionary anarchist and founder of collectivist anarchism. One of the most influential figures of the 19th-century revolutionary movement, he participated in uprisings across Europe, spent years in prisons and Siberian exile, and became Karl Marx's chief rival within the International Workingmen's Association (First International).

Life

Born into the landowning aristocracy, Bakunin served as an artillery officer before abandoning the army to study philosophy in Moscow and Berlin, where he joined the Young Hegelians. He fought in the Prague Slav Congress uprising (1848) and the Dresden uprising (1849), was sentenced to death twice, extradited to Russia, and exiled to Siberia — from which he escaped in 1861, travelling via Japan and America to London.

Thought

Bakunin advocateded the abolition of the state, the church, and hereditary property, to be replaced by a free federation of communes and workplaces. His major writings — Federalism, Socialism, and Anti-Theologism (1867), the posthumously published God and the State (1882), and Statism and Anarchy (1873) — warned that a Marxist "dictatorship of the proletariat" would produce a new tyranny.

Conflict with Marx and legacy

Bakunin's faction was expelled from the First International at the 1872 Hague Congress, splitting the socialist movement into anarchist and Marxist wings. His federalist ideas shaped the anarchosyndicalist labor movements of Spain, Italy, and Latin America.
