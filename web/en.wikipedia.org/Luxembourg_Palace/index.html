The Luxembourg Palace (French: Palais du Luxembourg) is a palace in the 6th arrondissement of Paris, built in the early 17th century for Marie de' Medici, widow of Henry IV and regent of France. Modelled in part on the Palazzo Pitti of her native Florence, it has served, after varied careers as royal residence, prison and museum, as the seat of the French Senate — the upper house of Parliament — since 1799. It gives its name to the surrounding Luxembourg Gardens and the Latin Quarter neighbourhood.

History

Marie de' Medici bought the hôtel of François-Henri de Montmorency, Duke of Luxembourg, in 1611 and commissioned architect Salomon de Brosse to build a grand palace on the site. Construction ran from 1615 to about 1631, interrupted by the regent's political fall: on the "Day of the Dupes" (1630) Louis XIII exiled his mother, and she fled France the following year, dying in Cologne in 1642, never to live in her palace. Peter Paul Rubens painted a monumental cycle of 24 canvases celebrating her life for its gallery (now in the Louvre).

The palace passed to the Orléans branch of the royal family, served as a prison during the Revolution, and housed the first public museum of painting in France (opened 1750 in its east gallery, an ancestor of the Musée du Luxembourg). In 1795 it became the seat of the Directory government; Napoleon Bonaparte took power from its chambers in the coup of 18 Brumaire (1799) and installed the Sénat conservateur here. Under the Bourbon Restoration it housed the Chamber of Peers; architect Alphonse de Gisors enlarged it in the 1830s–50s, advancing the garden façade, to create the present Senate hemicycle.

During the German occupation (1940–1944) the palace was the headquarters of the Luftwaffe in France; Hermann Göring used it when in Paris. It was liberated in August 1944 and resumed its senatorial role under the Fourth and Fifth Republics.

Architecture

De Brosse's design combines French château planning — a cour d'honneur enclosed by pavilions — with Florentine rustication recalling the Palazzo Pitti. The garden façade, refaced by Gisors, presents a central domed pavilion flanked by wings. Inside are the Senate chamber, the richly gilded Salle du Livre d'Or, the conference hall in the former throne room, and Delacroix's paintings in the library. The palace adjoins the Petit Luxembourg, residence of the President of the Senate.
