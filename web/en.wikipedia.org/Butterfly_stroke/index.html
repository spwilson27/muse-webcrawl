Butterfly stroke

The butterfly (shortened to fly) is a swimming stroke swum on the chest, with both arms moving symmetrically, accompanied by the butterfly kick (also known as the "dolphin kick"). While other styles like the breaststroke, front crawl, or backstroke can be swum adequately by beginners, the butterfly is widely regarded as the most difficult stroke, requiring very good technique as well as strong muscles.

Technique

The butterfly technique begins with the swimmer extending both arms forward. The arms then pull in a simultaneous sweeping motion: out to the sides, back through the water and recovering over the surface together. The body undulates in a wave-like motion driven by two dolphin kicks per arm cycle — the legs move together, bending at the knees and whipping the feet downward. Breathing is typically coordinated with the arm recovery, when the head lifts clear of the water.

History

The butterfly evolved from the breaststroke in the 1930s. Swimmers such as Henry Myers began recovering their arms over the water, and in 1933–34 the overarm style was combined with a fishtail (dolphin) kick developed by Jack Sieg at the University of Iowa. The stroke was controversial within breaststroke rules for two decades until FINA recognized butterfly as a separate stroke in 1952; it made its Olympic debut at the 1956 Melbourne Games.

Competition

Butterfly is contested over 50 m, 100 m and 200 m at the Olympic Games, always in long-course pools at the elite level, and forms the first leg of the individual medley. Under World Aquatics rules the arms must move simultaneously, the legs must kick together without alternating or breaststroke kicks, and the swimmer must surface within 15 m of the start and each turn.
