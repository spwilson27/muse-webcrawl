Flow separation

In fluid dynamics, flow separation (or boundary layer separation) occurs when the boundary layer of a fluid flowing over a solid surface detaches from that surface, forming a region of recirculating, often turbulent flow. Separation is driven by an adverse pressure gradient — pressure increasing in the direction of flow — which decelerates the near-wall fluid until it reverses direction.

Mechanism

Fluid particles in the boundary layer have low momentum because of viscous friction. If the pressure rises along the surface, these particles are brought to rest and then pushed backward, lifting the boundary layer away from the wall. The separation point is where the wall shear stress falls to zero. Downstream of separation, eddies and a broad wake form, greatly increasing pressure (form) drag.

Consequences

On aircraft wings, separation at high angles of attack leads to stall and a sharp loss of lift. On cars and other bluff bodies it accounts for most aerodynamic drag. In diffusers and turbomachinery, separation reduces efficiency and can cause unsteady loading. Engineers delay separation by streamlining, by using vortex generators, by suction or blowing, or by keeping the boundary layer turbulent, since a turbulent boundary layer carries more near-wall momentum and resists separation longer.

Separation in unsteady flow

In oscillating or impulsively started flows, separation points move and vortex shedding may occur, as in the von Kármán vortex street behind a cylinder. The study of separation remains a central challenge in computational fluid dynamics because separated flows are highly sensitive to turbulence modeling.
