Self-esteem

Self-esteem is an individual's subjective evaluation of their own worth. It encompasses beliefs about oneself (for example, "I am competent" or "I am unworthy") as well as emotional states such as pride, shame, triumph and despair. Psychologists usually treat self-esteem as an enduring personality characteristic (trait self-esteem), though short-term fluctuations (state self-esteem) are also recognised. It is related to, but distinct from, self-concept (the whole set of beliefs about oneself), self-efficacy (belief in one's ability to perform specific tasks) and narcissism (an inflated and often fragile self-view).

History of the concept

The philosopher David Hume wrote of the importance of self-regard, but the modern psychological construct is usually traced to William James, who in 1892 proposed that self-esteem equals success divided by pretensions — that is, it rises with achievement and falls with unmet aspirations. In the mid-20th century self-esteem became central to humanistic psychology in the work of Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers, who linked it to self-actualisation and unconditional positive regard. Morris Rosenberg developed the most widely used measure, the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (1965), and in the 1980s–90s the "self-esteem movement," especially in California, promoted boosting children's self-esteem as a remedy for social problems — a programme later criticised for outrunning the evidence.

Measurement and development

Self-esteem is usually assessed by self-report questionnaires, explicitly, and increasingly also by implicit measures such as the name-letter task. It tends to rise from adolescence into middle age and decline in later life; it is influenced by parenting, peer acceptance, social comparison, success and failure, and broader cultural values. Twin studies suggest a moderate heritable component alongside environmental effects.

Correlates and controversies

Higher self-esteem is associated with greater life satisfaction, resilience and willingness to attempt challenging tasks; very low self-esteem is associated with depression, anxiety and eating disorders. Research reviews, notably by Roy Baumeister and colleagues (2003), concluded that high self-esteem does not by itself cause better school performance, occupational success or healthier behaviour, and that inflated self-esteem overlaps with narcissism and can be linked to aggression when threatened. Many psychologists now argue that secure, realistic self-acceptance — rather than self-esteem maximised at any cost — is the healthier goal.
