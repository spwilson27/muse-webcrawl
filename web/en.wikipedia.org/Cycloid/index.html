Cycloid

A cycloid generated by a circle rolling along a straight line.

A cycloid is the curve traced by a point on the rim of a circular wheel as the wheel rolls along a straight line without slipping. It is an example of a roulette, a curve generated by a point attached to a curve rolling on another curve. The cycloid was one of the first curves studied with the new methods of seventeenth-century mathematics and is sometimes called "the Helen of geometers" for the disputes it provoked.

Equations

For a circle of radius r rolling along the x-axis, with the tracing point starting at the origin, the cycloid is given parametrically by:

x = r(θ − sin θ),    y = r(1 − cos θ)

One arch of the cycloid spans a horizontal distance of 2πr and reaches a maximum height of 2r. The area under one arch is exactly 3πr² — three times the area of the generating circle — and the length of one arch is exactly 8r, both results obtained in the 1630s–1650s by Roberval and Wren respectively.

Famous properties

Brachistochrone

In 1696 Johann Bernoulli challenged mathematicians to find the curve of fastest descent under gravity between two points. The answer — published by Bernoulli, Newton, Leibniz, L'Hôpital and Jakob Bernoulli — is an arc of an inverted cycloid: the brachistochrone curve. The problem launched the calculus of variations.

Tautochrone

Christiaan Huygens showed in 1659 that the cycloid is also the tautochrone: a bead sliding frictionlessly down an inverted cycloid reaches the bottom in the same time regardless of its starting point. Huygens exploited this in designing pendulum clocks, constraining the bob between cycloidal "cheeks" so that its period is independent of amplitude.

History

The curve was studied by Nicholas of Cusa and Charles de Bovelles around 1500, named by Galileo in 1599, and investigated by Mersenne, Roberval, Torricelli, Pascal, Wallis and Huygens. Pascal's 1658 contest on the cycloid, published under the pseudonym Amos Dettonville, was a landmark in the prehistory of integral calculus.

Related curves

If the tracing point lies inside or outside the circle, the curve is a trochoid (curtate or prolate cycloid). Cycloids generated by circles rolling on other circles give epicycloids and hypocycloids, of which the cardioid and astroid are famous special cases.
