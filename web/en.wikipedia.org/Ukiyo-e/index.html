Ukiyo-e (Japanese: 浮世絵, lit. 'picture[s] of the floating world') is a genre of Japanese art that flourished from the 17th through 19th centuries. Its artists specialized in woodblock prints and paintings of such subjects as female beauties (bijin-ga), kabuki actors (yakusha-e), sumo wrestlers, scenes from history and folk tales, travel scenes and landscapes (meisho-e), flora and fauna, and erotica (shunga).

The term ukiyo originally described the Buddhist concept of the transience of life, but in the Edo period it came to evoke the pleasure quarters of the great cities, especially Edo (modern Tokyo) — a hedonistic "floating world" of theatres, teahouses and courtesans. Inexpensive to produce, ukiyo-e prints were aimed at the growing merchant class of townspeople, who could not afford original paintings.

History

Ukiyo-e developed from earlier painting schools under artists such as Iwasa Matabei, and was transformed by Hishikawa Moronobu, who in the 1670s produced the first woodblock prints in the genre. Early prints were monochrome or hand-coloured; full-colour printing (nishiki-e, "brocade pictures") arrived in the 1760s with the work of Suzuki Harunobu, using multiple blocks, one for each colour.

The genre reached a peak with the beauty prints of Kitagawa Utamaro and the actor prints of Tōshūsai Sharaku in the 1790s. In the 19th century, Katsushika Hokusai's Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (c. 1830), including The Great Wave off Kanagawa, and Utagawa Hiroshige's The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (1833–34) and One Hundred Famous Views of Edo established landscape as a major subject. The last masters of the tradition, such as Tsukioka Yoshitoshi, worked into the Meiji period.

Technique

Production was a collaboration between four specialists: the publisher, who commissioned and marketed the work; the artist, who drew the design in ink; the carver, who pasted the drawing to a block of cherry wood and carved a separate block for each colour; and the printer, who aligned the paper on the blocks using registration marks (kentō) and rubbed it with a baren to transfer the water-based pigments. Special effects included embossing (karazuri), mica dusting, and gradation (bokashi).

Influence

After Japan opened to foreign trade in the 1850s, ukiyo-e prints flooded into Europe, where they profoundly influenced Impressionist and Post-Impressionist artists including Monet, Degas, Toulouse-Lautrec and Van Gogh — the phenomenon known as Japonisme. The flat areas of colour, bold cropping and everyday subject matter of ukiyo-e reshaped Western composition and printmaking.
