The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in the Greenwich Village neighbourhood of Manhattan, New York City, on Saturday, 25 March 1911, was the deadliest industrial disaster in the history of the city and one of the deadliest in American history. The fire caused the deaths of 146 garment workers — 123 women and girls and 23 men — who died from the fire, smoke inhalation, or falling and jumping to their deaths. Most victims were recent Italian or Jewish immigrant women and girls aged 14 to 23.

The fire

The factory, located on the 8th, 9th and 10th floors of the Asch Building, occupied lofts filled with flammable fabric offcuts. When fire broke out near quitting time, workers found one stairwell door locked — a common practice intended to prevent theft and unauthorised breaks — and the single fire escape collapsed under the weight of those fleeing. Elevator operators made repeated trips rescuing workers until the cars could no longer run. Dozens jumped from the windows to escape the flames; firefighters' ladders reached only to the sixth floor and their nets could not hold jumpers from that height.

Aftermath

Owners Max Blanck and Isaac Harris were tried for manslaughter and acquitted, though they lost a civil suit and collected substantial insurance. Public outrage led New York State to create the Factory Investigating Commission, chaired by Robert F. Wagner with Al Smith as vice-chair and Frances Perkins among its investigators. Its work produced dozens of new laws on fire safety, building codes, working hours and child labour, making New York a model of progressive labour reform and shaping later New Deal policy.

Legacy

The disaster is commemorated annually and remains a touchstone of the labour movement. The building, now known as the Brown Building, is a National Historic Landmark; in 2023 a memorial listing the victims' names was installed on its facade.
