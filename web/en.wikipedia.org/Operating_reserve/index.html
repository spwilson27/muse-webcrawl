Operating reserve

In electricity networks, the operating reserve is generating capacity that is available to a system operator within a short interval of time to meet demand in case a generator goes down or there is another disruption to the supply. Keeping sufficient operating reserve is a central requirement for the reliable operation of power grids.

Reserve capacity lets grid operators respond to sudden losses of generation.

Types of reserve

Spinning reserve

Spinning reserve is extra generating capacity provided by generators already connected (synchronised) to the grid, which can be activated immediately by increasing their output. It is the fastest-responding form of reserve.

Non-spinning and supplemental reserve

Non-spinning reserve comes from generators not currently connected but able to start and reach full output within minutes, or from interruptible loads that can be curtailed. Supplemental reserve is similar capacity available over a longer window, typically within an hour.

Sizing and regulation

Grid codes commonly require reserves at least equal to the largest single generating unit or interconnector in service, so that its sudden loss (an "N-1" event) does not cause a shortfall. In North America, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) sets standards for contingency reserves; European transmission system operators coordinate through ENTSO-E. Reserve can also be provided by demand response, battery storage and pumped hydro.

Markets

In liberalised electricity markets, operating reserve is procured through ancillary-services markets, in which providers are paid for availability and for energy actually delivered when called.
