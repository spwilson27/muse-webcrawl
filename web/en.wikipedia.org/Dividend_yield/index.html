Dividend yield

The dividend yield or dividend–price ratio of a share is the dividend per share divided by the price per share. It is also a company's total annual dividend payments divided by its market capitalization, assuming the number of shares is constant. It is often expressed as a percentage.

Dividend yield = annual dividend per share / price per share

For example, if a company pays an annual dividend of $2 per share and its shares trade at $50, the dividend yield is 4%.

Trailing and forward yield

The trailing dividend yield uses the actual dividends paid over the preceding 12 months. The forward dividend yield uses the most recent quarterly dividend multiplied by four (or the indicated annual dividend) divided by the current share price. The two measures differ when a company has recently raised or cut its dividend.

Uses and interpretation

Investors use dividend yield to compare the income-generating potential of shares and to value companies, particularly in mature industries such as utilities and telecommunications where dividends form a large part of total return. Historically, the dividend yield of broad indices such as the Dow Jones Industrial Average served as a valuation gauge: yields were high when stocks were considered cheap and low at market peaks in the late 1920s and late 1990s.

A very high yield can signal distress rather than value, since yields rise as prices fall; investors describe unsustainably high yields as a possible "dividend trap". Analysts therefore examine the payout ratio and dividend coverage alongside the yield, and note that changes in dividend policy — such as those prompted by the Dodd–Frank Act stress tests for banks — can alter yields abruptly.
