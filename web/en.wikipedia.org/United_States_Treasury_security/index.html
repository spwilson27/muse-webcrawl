United States Treasury security

United States Treasury securities, also called Treasuries, are government debt instruments issued by the United States Department of the Treasury to finance government spending as an alternative to taxation. Treasury securities are among the safest investments in the world because they are backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government, and the market for them is the largest and most liquid government securities market in the world.

IssuerU.S. Department of the Treasury
TypesTreasury bills, notes, bonds, TIPS, FRNs
CurrencyUnited States dollar
Market sizeTens of trillions of dollars outstanding
Treasury securities finance the operations of the federal government.

Types

Treasury bills (T-bills) are short-term securities maturing in one year or less, sold at a discount from face value. Treasury notes have maturities of two, three, five, seven, and ten years and pay interest every six months. Treasury bonds mature in 20 or 30 years and also pay semiannual interest. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), introduced in 1997, have principal adjusted with the Consumer Price Index. Floating Rate Notes (FRNs), introduced in 2014, pay interest tied to a 13-week bill index. Savings bonds are non-marketable securities sold to individuals.

Market

Treasuries are sold at regular auctions conducted by the Treasury, with primary dealers obligated to bid. The yield on the ten-year Treasury note is a widely watched benchmark for global interest rates, and Treasury yields form the "risk-free" rate used throughout finance. Foreign governments and central banks hold large quantities of Treasuries as reserve assets.
