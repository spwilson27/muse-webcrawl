Dialogue

Dialogue (sometimes spelled dialog in American English) is a written or spoken conversational exchange between two or more people, and a literary and theatrical form that depicts such an exchange. As a narrative, philosophical, or didactic device, it is chiefly associated in the West with the Socratic dialogue as developed by Plato, but antecedents are also found in other traditions including Indian literature.

As a literary genre

The philosophical dialogue flourished in classical antiquity: Plato's dialogues, in which Socrates questions interlocutors, became foundational texts of Western philosophy. Cicero, Lucian, and later Galileo (in his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems), Berkeley, and Hume all used dialogue form to present competing arguments. As a genre it also appears in Jewish, Islamic, and East Asian literature.

In fiction and drama

In fiction, dialogue is the exchange of spoken words between characters, used to reveal character, advance the plot, and convey information. Playwrights and screenwriters rely almost entirely on dialogue and stage directions. Conventions for punctuating dialogue differ between languages and style guides, but in English fiction dialogue is usually enclosed in quotation marks and attributed with tags such as "he said".

Dialogue as practice

The word also denotes structured conversation aimed at mutual understanding, as in interfaith dialogue or dialogue groups; thinkers such as Martin Buber and David Bohm developed accounts of dialogue as a mode of genuine encounter and collective thinking.
