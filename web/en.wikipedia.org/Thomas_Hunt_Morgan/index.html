Thomas Hunt Morgan (1866–1945) was an American evolutionary biologist, geneticist and embryologist. He won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1933 for discoveries elucidating the role of the chromosome in heredity—the first Nobel awarded for work in genetics.

The fly room

At Columbia University from 1910, Morgan's laboratory—the famous "fly room"—bred the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster in milk bottles. The discovery of a white-eyed mutant male fly, and the sex-linked inheritance of the trait, provided the first concrete evidence that genes reside on chromosomes. With his students Alfred Sturtevant, Calvin Bridges and Hermann Muller, Morgan developed the theory of genetic linkage and produced the first genetic maps; Sturtevant, still an undergraduate, constructed the first map of gene positions on a chromosome in 1913. Their synthesis appeared in The Mechanism of Mendelian Heredity (1915).

Later career

In 1928 Morgan moved to the California Institute of Technology to build its biology division. His early scepticism toward both Mendelian genetics and chromosomal theory, overcome by his own fly experiments, became a famous example of scientific self-correction. The unit of genetic map distance, the centimorgan, is named in his honour.
