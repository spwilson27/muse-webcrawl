Uruk was one of the first major cities in the history of the world, and the chief city of Sumer in the 4th millennium BC.

Uruk (Sumerian: 𒌷𒀕, Unug; Akkadian: Uruk; Biblical Hebrew: Erech) was an ancient city of Sumer — and later of Babylonia — situated east of the present bed of the Euphrates River on the dried-up ancient channel of the Euphrates, some 30 km east of modern Samawah in Iraq. Uruk is the type site of the Uruk period (c. 4000–3100 BC). It played a leading role in the early urbanization of Sumer in the mid-4th millennium BC.

By about 3200 BC, Uruk was the largest settlement in southern Mesopotamia — if not the world — covering some 250 hectares and housing perhaps 50,000–80,000 people at its peak, making it the largest city in the world at the time. According to the Sumerian King List, Uruk was founded by Enmerkar, and its most famous king was Gilgamesh, hero of the Epic of Gilgamesh, who is credited with building the city's massive walls.

History

Uruk period

Archaeological strata show occupation from the Ubaid period onwards. During the Uruk period, the settlement grew from a cluster of villages into a true city with temples, monumental architecture, administrative bureaucracy, cylinder seals, mass-produced bevelled-rim bowls, and — most consequentially — the earliest known system of writing, cuneiform, which appears in the archaic tablets from the Eanna precinct around 3400–3200 BC.

Uruk's influence spread widely across the Near East in the so-called "Uruk expansion", with colonies and outposts as far as southeastern Turkey, southwestern Iran and northern Syria, suggesting an early network of trade in metals, timber and luxury goods.

Later history

Uruk continued to flourish in the Early Dynastic period and remained an important religious and scholarly centre under the Akkadian, Ur III, Babylonian, Achaemenid, Seleucid and Parthian empires. The great temple complexes of Eanna, dedicated to the goddess Inanna (Ishtar), and the Anu district, with its White Temple and later the enormous ziggurat-like towers, were rebuilt many times. The city declined after the 7th century AD and was eventually abandoned as the Euphrates shifted course.

Archaeology

The site, known today as Warka, was first surveyed by William Lofts in 1849 and excavated by German teams from 1912 onwards, with major campaigns before and after the Second World War. Excavations revealed the Eanna precinct, the Anu ziggurat with the White Temple on its summit, thousands of cuneiform tablets, the Warka Vase, the Warka Mask (Mask of Warka), and the Lion Hunt Stele.

Religion

Uruk was the cult centre of Inanna, goddess of love and war, whose Eanna temple remained active for over three thousand years, and of the sky god Anu, worshipped on the Anu terrace. In later periods the Gareus temple, built in the Parthian era, shows the city's religious life continuing into the Roman age.
