Jewish Brigade

The Jewish Infantry Brigade Group, more commonly known as the Jewish Brigade, was a military formation of the British Army in the Second World War, composed of Jewish volunteers from Mandatory Palestine and commanded by British Jewish officers. Formed in September 1944 after years of Zionist lobbying, it was the only explicitly Jewish military formation to serve under a Jewish flag in the war — its shoulder insignia bore the Star of David. The brigade fought in the final offensive in Italy in spring 1945, and after the war its members played significant roles in aiding Holocaust survivors, in the Bericha movement smuggling Jews to Palestine, in acts of vengeance against former Nazis, and later as a cadre of the Israel Defense Forces.

Formation

From the outbreak of war, the Jewish Agency under Chaim Weizmann and David Ben-Gurion pressed the British government to raise a Jewish fighting force; Ben-Gurion declared that Jews would "fight the war as if there were no White Paper, and fight the White Paper as if there were no war." Tens of thousands of Palestinian Jews volunteered for British service, many serving in Palestinian units of the Royal Army Service Corps, pioneers, and the Buffs, and Jewish muleteer and commando traditions reached back to the Zion Mule Corps of Gallipoli. Only in July 1944 did the War Cabinet approve a brigade, announced by Prime Minister Winston Churchill in September. The brigade was built around three battalions of the Palestine Regiment, with artillery, engineers, signals, and medical units, and trained in Egypt before embarking for Italy.

Service in Italy

The brigade joined the Eighth Army in Italy in early 1945 and entered the line on the Senio River front in March, holding positions near Alfonsine and engaging German forces in patrol clashes that caused its first combat casualties. In April 1945 it took part in the Spring Offensive (Operation Grapeshot), crossing the Senio and advancing to the Po valley as German resistance in Italy collapsed; the war in Europe ended on 8 May with the brigade in the Tarvisio area near the Austrian and Yugoslav borders.

Aftermath: survivors, vengeance, and Bericha

Posted after the surrender to garrison duties in Italy, Austria, Belgium, and the Netherlands, brigade soldiers encountered Holocaust survivors in the displaced persons camps — famously the meeting dramatised in Zionist memory as soldiers tearing off insignia to reveal the Star of David. Many assisted the Bericha ("flight") networks that moved tens of thousands of survivors toward Mediterranean ports and Palestine, diverting trucks, documents, and supplies. A small secret group within the brigade, the Nakam ("vengeance") squads associated with the Tilhas Tizig Gesheften, hunted and killed a number of identified SS men and camp guards extra-legally; estimates of those killed vary and remain contested by historians. Members of the brigade also founded kibbutz Yagur's and other connections in what became clandestine arms purchasing and training for the Haganah.

Disbandment and legacy

The brigade was disbanded in the summer of 1946 as British policy in Palestine hardened. Its veterans — some 5,000 trained and battle-experienced soldiers — formed a backbone of the Haganah and then of the Israel Defense Forces in the 1948 Arab–Israeli War; several, including Yigal Allon (a brigade-associated Palmach commander of the wider generation) and brigade officers who became IDF generals, rose to senior command. The brigade remains an emblem of organised Jewish participation in the defeat of Nazi Germany.
