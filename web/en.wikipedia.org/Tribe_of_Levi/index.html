Tribe of Levi

According to the Hebrew Bible, the Tribe of Levi is one of the tribes of Israel, traditionally descended from Levi, son of Jacob. The tribe is set apart from the others by its religious functions. Unlike the twelve territorial tribes, the Levites received no land inheritance; instead they were given cities among the other tribes and were supported by tithes, in return for their service at the sanctuary.

Biblical role

The Book of Numbers describes the Levites as dedicated to the service of the Tabernacle — carrying it in the wilderness, guarding it, and assisting the priests. Within the tribe, the descendants of Aaron formed the priesthood (kohanim), who alone offered sacrifices, while the remaining Levites served as assistants, musicians, gatekeepers, and teachers. The tribe's zeal at the incident of the Golden Calf is given in Exodus as a reason for its selection.

Levitical cities

Joshua 21 assigns the Levites forty-eight cities among the territories of the other tribes, six of which were also cities of refuge. Because they had no tribal territory, Levi is sometimes omitted from lists of the twelve tribes, with the tribe of Joseph counted twice through his sons Ephraim and Manasseh.

In later Judaism

After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, the sacrificial service ended, but hereditary status continued: to this day, Jews who identify as Levites (Levi'im) are called second to the Torah reading in synagogue, assist in the priestly blessing ritual by washing the kohanim's hands, and retain certain other honours and restrictions distinct from those of ordinary Israelites.
