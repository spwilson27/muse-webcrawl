Clyfford Still

Clyfford Still (November 30, 1904 – June 23, 1980) was an American painter, one of the leading figures in the first generation of abstract expressionists, who developed a non-figurative approach to painting in the years immediately following World War II. Still was a painter of large-scale works covered with dense, irregular fields of colour with jagged, flame-like edges. He was one of the earliest and most uncompromising practitioners of colour-field painting, though he resisted labels and critical categorization throughout his career.

Life and career

Still was born in Grandin, North Dakota, and grew up in Spokane, Washington, and in Alberta, Canada, where the harsh prairie landscape left a lasting impression on his art. He studied at Spokane University and taught at Washington State College and later at the California School of Fine Arts in San Francisco, where he influenced a generation of West Coast painters. His first solo exhibition in New York was at Peggy Guggenheim's Art of This Century gallery in 1946, placing him at the heart of the emerging New York School alongside Jackson Pollock, Mark Rothko, and Barnett Newman.

Fiercely independent, Still broke with commercial galleries in the 1950s, sold little, and controlled access to his work strictly. He moved to Maryland in 1961 and painted in relative isolation until his death in 1980.

Work and legacy

Still's mature paintings—often titled only with dates and numbers—confront the viewer with vast expanses of black, red, yellow, and ochre broken by vertical fissures of contrasting colour, evoking landscapes, caverns, and elemental forces at monumental scale. Following the terms of his will, his estate of some 2,400 works was sealed until a city agreed to build a museum devoted solely to his art. Denver, Colorado, was selected, and the Clyfford Still Museum opened in 2011, holding about 95 percent of his output and making his achievement fully visible for the first time.
