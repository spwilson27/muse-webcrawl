Financial economics

Financial economics is the branch of economics concerned with the allocation of resources in financial markets: the pricing of assets, the structure of financial institutions, corporate financial decisions, and the behavior of investors under risk and uncertainty. It applies microeconomic theory, probability, and statistics to questions of valuation and risk.

Core theories

The field's foundations include the time value of money and discounted cash flow analysis; Harry Markowitz's portfolio theory (1952), which formalized diversification and the risk–return trade-off; the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) of Sharpe, Lintner, and Mossin; the efficient market hypothesis associated with Eugene Fama; the Modigliani–Miller theorems on the irrelevance of capital structure under ideal conditions; and the Black–Scholes–Merton model (1973) for pricing options, based on no-arbitrage and dynamic hedging. The arbitrage pricing theory and multi-factor models extended CAPM, while behavioral finance — associated with Kahneman, Tversky, Shiller, and Thaler — incorporated systematic psychological deviations from rational expectations.

Subject matter

Financial economics studies asset pricing (equities, bonds, derivatives, real assets), corporate finance (investment appraisal, capital structure, payout policy), market microstructure, financial intermediation and banking, risk management, and increasingly household finance and the interaction of finance with macroeconomic stability, as emphasized by the 2008 financial crisis.

Methods

The discipline is heavily mathematical, using stochastic calculus, optimization, and econometrics, and is closely allied with mathematical finance and financial engineering, which focus on derivatives pricing and hedging. Nobel Prizes in Economics have recognized many of its architects, including Markowitz, Sharpe, Miller, Scholes, Merton, Fama, and Shiller.
