Augustine of Canterbury

Augustine of Canterbury (c. 534 – 26 May 604) was a Christian monk who became the first Archbishop of Canterbury in the year 597. He is considered the "Apostle to the English" and a founder of the Church of England. A prior of a monastery in Rome, he was sent by Pope Gregory the Great in 595 to Christianise the Anglo-Saxons of Kent.

Augustine's Gregorian mission reached Kent in 597.

The Gregorian mission

Augustine and about forty companions landed on the Isle of Thanet in 597 and were received by King Æthelberht of Kent, whose Frankish wife Bertha was already a Christian. The king converted and was baptised, and Augustine established his seat at Canterbury, founding the abbey later known as St Augustine's and the church that became Canterbury Cathedral. Gregory's letters, preserved by Bede, advised Augustine to adapt pagan temples and festivals to Christian use rather than simply destroying them.

Archbishop

Consecrated bishop in Gaul, Augustine received the pallium from Rome in 601, along with plans for an English church of two provinces, at London and York — Canterbury, not London, remained the southern metropolitan see. His attempts to assert authority over the existing British (Celtic) bishops failed after meetings remembered as Augustine's Oak, a failure later writers connected with the subsequent Battle of Chester. Before his death in 604, Augustine consecrated Laurence as his successor. He is venerated as a saint, with a feast day on 26 May.
