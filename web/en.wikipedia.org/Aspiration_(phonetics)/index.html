Aspiration (phonetics)

In phonetics, aspiration is the strong burst of breath or puff of air that accompanies the release of some obstruent consonants, most typically voiceless stops. In the International Phonetic Alphabet, aspirated consonants are written with a superscript small h, [ʰ], as in [pʰ], [tʰ] and [kʰ]. The interval between the release of the stop and the onset of voicing of the following sound is called voice onset time (VOT); aspirated stops have a long-lag VOT.

In English

In English, the voiceless stops /p, t, k/ are aspirated when they begin a stressed syllable — as in pin [pʰɪn], tin, kin — but unaspirated after /s/ (spin, sting, skin) and in unstressed positions. This aspiration is allophonic: it does not distinguish words, though it is a cue that helps listeners identify the stops, since unaspirated [p] after /s/ sounds like the voiced /b/ of bin to English ears.

Cross-linguistic role

In many languages aspiration is phonemic, distinguishing words. Hindi and other Indo-Aryan languages have a four-way stop contrast (voiceless unaspirated, voiceless aspirated, voiced, and breathy-voiced aspirated); Thai and Mandarin Chinese contrast aspirated with unaspirated voiceless stops; and Ancient Greek distinguished ph, th, kh from p, t, k, a contrast preserved in the romanized spellings. Icelandic and some varieties of German and Danish also have phonemic aspiration.
