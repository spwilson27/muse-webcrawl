Domestication and foreignization

Domestication and foreignization
Field: Translation studies
Key figure: Lawrence Venuti
Origin of terms: Friedrich Schleiermacher (1813)

Domestication and foreignization are strategies in translation regarding the degree to which translators make a text conform to the target culture. Domestication adapts the source text to the values and conventions of the target language, producing a fluent, transparent style that minimizes the foreignness of the original. Foreignization deliberately retains elements of the source culture, signaling the text's otherness to the reader.

Theory

The distinction derives from Friedrich Schleiermacher's 1813 lecture on translation methods: the translator either leaves the reader in peace and moves the author toward them, or leaves the author in peace and moves the reader toward the author. The terms were developed in their modern form by Lawrence Venuti in The Translator's Invisibility (1995), which criticized the dominance of domesticating, fluent translation in Anglo-American culture as an ethnocentric erasure of foreign difference.

Examples and debate

Domesticating choices include replacing foreign idioms, currency, or foods with familiar equivalents; foreignizing choices include calques, retaining source-language terms, and preserving unusual syntax. The choice is often treated as ethical as well as stylistic, though critics note the two strategies form a spectrum rather than a strict binary.
