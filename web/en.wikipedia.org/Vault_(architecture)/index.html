Vault (architecture)

In architecture, a vault (French voûte, from Italian volta) is a self-supporting arched form, usually of stone or brick (sometimes concrete), serving to cover a space with a ceiling or roof. Vaults channel the loads of the structure down into its supporting walls and piers, exerting outward thrust that must be resisted by mass or buttressing.

Types

The simplest form is the barrel vault, an extended semicircular arch. Intersecting two barrel vaults produces a groin vault. Roman builders used both extensively, often in concrete. Medieval builders developed the pointed rib vault, in which a framework of stone ribs carries lighter infill panels; rib vaulting is a defining feature of Gothic architecture, permitting tall, light-filled interiors. Other forms include fan vaults, cloister vaults, domical vaults and Catalan (timbrel) vaults of thin tile.

History

Vaulting appears in ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian mudbrick construction and was brought to monumental scale by the Romans in baths, basilicas and warehouses. It was revived and elaborated in Romanesque and Gothic Europe and used by Islamic and Byzantine architects in forms related to the dome and muqarnas.
