The Book of the Dead is the modern name for an ancient Egyptian funerary text — a collection of spells, hymns, and instructions written on papyrus and placed in tombs to help the deceased navigate the afterlife (the Duat) and achieve immortality. The ancient Egyptian name transliterated as rw nw prt m hrw means "Book of Coming Forth by Day".

Development

The Book of the Dead developed from earlier funerary corpora: the Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom, inscribed on royal tomb walls, and the Coffin Texts of the Middle Kingdom, painted on coffins of non-royal people. From the New Kingdom onward, the spells were written on papyrus scrolls commissioned by individuals, with the contents varying from copy to copy; there was never a single fixed "book". Around 200 distinct spells are known, of which a typical scroll might contain a few dozen.

Notable spells

The most famous scene is Spell 125, the judgment of the dead: the deceased makes a "negative confession" of sins not committed before 42 divine assessors, and the heart is weighed against the feather of Maat, goddess of truth and order. A heart heavy with sin would be devoured by the monster Ammit; a balanced heart admitted the deceased to the Field of Reeds. Other spells protect against snakes and crocodiles, preserve the heart and name, enable the deceased to transform into animals or gods, and provide the passwords needed to pass the gates of the underworld.

Discovery and study

The term "Book of the Dead" was coined by the German Egyptologist Karl Richard Lepsius, who published a translation of a Ptolemaic manuscript in 1842 and assigned the spell numbers still used today. The finest surviving example, the Papyrus of Ani, is held by the British Museum.
