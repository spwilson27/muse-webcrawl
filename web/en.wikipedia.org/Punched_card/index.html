Punched card

Punched card
Also called: punch card, Hollerith card, IBM card
Standard format: 80 columns × 12 rows
Invented for computing: Herman Hollerith (1880s)
Peak use: 1900s–1970s

A punched card (also known as a punch card or Hollerith card) is a piece of stiff paper that holds digital data represented by the presence or absence of holes in predefined positions. Punched cards were once common in data processing and in directly controlling automated machinery, and were the primary medium for computer input, storage and programming from the 1890s until the mid-1970s.

History

Cards with holes were used to control textile looms from the eighteenth century, culminating in the Jacquard machine of 1804. Herman Hollerith adapted the idea for tabulating the 1890 United States census; his company eventually became IBM. Charles Babbage had earlier planned to use punched cards to control his Analytical Engine and store its programs, an idea Ada Lovelace described in her notes.

Formats

The most common format was IBM's 80-column card, introduced in 1928, measuring 7⅜ by 3¼ inches with rectangular holes arranged in 12 rows. Each column encoded one character; digits used a single punch, letters and symbols two or three. Cards were produced with keypunch machines, read by electromechanical or photoelectric readers, and processed by sorters, tabulators and unit-record equipment before and alongside electronic computers.

Decline and legacy

Magnetic tape, disk storage and interactive terminals displaced cards during the 1970s, though they persisted in some niches, including voting systems, into the twenty-first century. The card's legacy survives in the 80-character line width of terminals and text editors, and in the phrase "do not fold, spindle or mutilate", printed on cards sent to customers.
