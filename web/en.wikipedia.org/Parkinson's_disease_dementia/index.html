Parkinson's disease dementia

Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD) is a dementia that develops in the course of Parkinson's disease. It is diagnosed when cognitive decline — affecting memory, attention, executive function, and visuospatial abilities — emerges at least a year after the onset of the motor symptoms of Parkinson's. By convention, dementia arising before or within a year of motor symptoms is instead classified as dementia with Lewy bodies; the two conditions share the same underlying pathology of Lewy bodies and are often grouped together as the Lewy body dementias.

Cognitive impairment is very common in Parkinson's disease: about a quarter to a third of patients have mild cognitive impairment at any given time, and studies suggest that a large majority — up to 75–90% — of those who live with the disease long enough eventually develop dementia. Risk factors include older age, more severe motor disease, and hallucinations.

Management

There is no cure, and treatment is symptomatic. The cholinesterase inhibitor rivastigmine is licensed for PDD, and other cholinesterase inhibitors are used off-label. Management also involves reviewing medications that can worsen cognition or provoke psychosis, treating depression and sleep disorders, and supporting carers. Antipsychotic drugs must be used with great caution because patients with Lewy body disease can be highly sensitive to them.
