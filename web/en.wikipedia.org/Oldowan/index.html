Oldowan

Oldowan industry
PeriodLower Paleolithic, c. 2.6 – 1.7 million years ago
Type siteOlduvai Gorge, Tanzania
ToolmakersHomo habilis and possibly Australopithecus / Paranthropus
Succeeded byAcheulean

The Oldowan (or Mode 1) is an archaeological industry of stone tools, the earliest widespread tradition of stone tool manufacture by hominins. Dating from about 2.6 million years ago, during the Lower Paleolithic, Oldowan tools were made by striking flakes from pebbles and cores, producing simple choppers, scrapers, and sharp flakes used for cutting, scavenging, and processing food.

Discovery and naming

The industry is named after Olduvai Gorge in northern Tanzania, where Louis Leakey first recognized and described the tools in the 1930s. The oldest known Oldowan tools come from Gona, Ethiopia, dated to about 2.6 million years ago; tools from Lomekwi, Kenya, dated to 3.3 million years ago (the Lomekwian), predate the Oldowan and the genus Homo itself.

Technology

Oldowan knappers used hard hammer percussion: a hammerstone struck a core to detach flakes with sharp edges. Even these simple tools required an understanding of fracture mechanics and the selection of suitable raw materials such as quartz, quartzite, basalt, and chert. Microscopic wear analysis and cut-marked bones show the flakes were used to butcher carcasses, while the core tools may have processed plant foods.

Makers and distribution

The tools are most often attributed to Homo habilis ("handy man"), so named by Louis Leakey because of the association, though australopithecines such as Paranthropus may also have made them. Oldowan sites are found across East and southern Africa, and later across North Africa, the Levant, and eventually Eurasia. The Oldowan was eventually supplemented and replaced by the more refined bifacial handaxes of the Acheulean industry, beginning about 1.76 million years ago.
