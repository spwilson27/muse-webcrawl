Superabundant number

In mathematics, a superabundant number is a natural number n such that, for all m < n, σ(n)/n > σ(m)/m, where σ denotes the divisor summatory function. Superabundant numbers were first defined by Leonidas Alaoglu and Paul Erdős in 1944.

Properties

The first superabundant numbers are 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 120, and 240. Every superabundant number is a highly composite number, and all superabundant numbers greater than 1 are even. The concept is closely related to colossally abundant numbers.

Relation to the Riemann hypothesis

Superabundant numbers are connected to the Riemann hypothesis through Robin's criterion: the hypothesis is true if and only if σ(n) < eγ n log log n for all n > 5040, and potential counterexamples must be superabundant numbers. Akbary and Friggstad showed in 2009 that any counterexample to the related Lagarias inequality in this context must also be superabundant.
