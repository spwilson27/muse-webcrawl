Speed reading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Speed-reading techniques aim to reduce fixations and subvocalisation

Speed reading is any of several techniques claiming to increase one's ability to read quickly. Methods include chunking — reading groups of words rather than word by word — and minimising subvocalisation, the silent pronunciation of words while reading. Typical adult reading speeds are around 200–300 words per minute, while speed-reading courses claim rates of 1,000 words per minute or more.

History

The modern speed-reading movement began in the late 1950s with Evelyn Wood, an American schoolteacher who founded Reading Dynamics and claimed her methods allowed students to read thousands of words per minute. The techniques gained prominence when U.S. presidents John F. Kennedy and Jimmy Carter were reported to have studied them.

Scientific evaluation

Research on reading indicates that speed and comprehension trade off against one another: extremely rapid "reading" is generally skimming, with reduced comprehension and retention. A 2016 review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest concluded that claims of very high speeds with normal comprehension are not supported by evidence, because visual and linguistic constraints limit how much information can be taken in per fixation.
