Quantile

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In statistics and probability, quantiles are cut points dividing the range of a probability distribution into continuous intervals with equal probabilities, or dividing the observations in a sample in the same way. There is one fewer quantile than the number of groups created: for example, quartiles divide data into four groups, so there are three quartiles. The median is the most familiar quantile — the value below which half of the probability (or data) falls.

Common quantiles

Quantiles with special names include:

  • The two terciles/tertiles dividing data into three parts.
  • The three quartiles dividing data into four parts (the second quartile is the median).
  • The four quintiles dividing data into five parts, often used in economics for income distribution.
  • The nine deciles dividing data into ten parts.
  • The 99 percentiles dividing data into one hundred parts; the p-th percentile is the value below which p percent of observations fall.

Definition

For a random variable with cumulative distribution function F, the quantile function Q(p) = inf{x : F(x) ≥ p} gives, for a probability p between 0 and 1, the smallest value such that the probability of not exceeding it is at least p. For finite samples, different conventions for interpolating between data points give slightly different estimates, and statistical software packages implement several competing definitions.

Applications

Quantiles are used in descriptive statistics (box plots use quartiles), in risk management (value at risk is a quantile of a loss distribution), in quantile regression, which models conditional quantiles rather than the conditional mean, and in defining measures such as the interquartile range, a robust measure of spread. Quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots compare two distributions by plotting their quantiles against each other.
