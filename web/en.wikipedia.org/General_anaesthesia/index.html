General anaesthesia

General anaesthesia (or anesthesia) is a medically induced loss of consciousness that renders a patient unarousable even by painful stimuli. It is achieved with a combination of medications that produce unconsciousness, amnesia, analgesia, and usually skeletal muscle relaxation, allowing surgical procedures to be performed without pain or distress.

Stages and conduct

A general anaesthetic typically proceeds through induction, maintenance, and emergence. Induction is commonly achieved intravenously with propofol or by inhalation of a volatile agent such as sevoflurane. Because anaesthetic agents suppress protective airway reflexes and respiratory drive, the airway is usually secured with an endotracheal tube or laryngeal mask, and ventilation is controlled mechanically. Depth of anaesthesia is monitored clinically and, in some settings, with processed EEG indices.

Agents

Intravenous agents include propofol, etomidate, ketamine, and the barbiturate thiopental. Volatile inhalational agents — sevoflurane, desflurane, and isoflurane — are delivered through a calibrated vaporiser. Adjuncts include opioids for analgesia, benzodiazepines for anxiolysis and amnesia, and neuromuscular blocking drugs such as rocuronium to facilitate intubation and surgery.

Physiology and risks

Anaesthetic drugs act largely on ligand-gated ion channels in the central nervous system, including GABAA and NMDA receptors, though their full mechanism remains incompletely understood. Complications range from common minor effects such as nausea, sore throat, and shivering to rare but serious events including aspiration, malignant hyperthermia, anaphylaxis, and anaesthesia awareness. Risk is strongly influenced by the patient's underlying health, classified using the ASA physical status system.

History

The era of modern anaesthesia began in 1846 with William T. G. Morton's public demonstration of ether anaesthesia in Boston. Chloroform and nitrous oxide followed, and the specialty developed through the 20th century with muscle relaxants, intravenous induction agents, and modern monitoring standards.
