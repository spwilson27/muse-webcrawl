Steller's sea cow

A reconstruction of Hydrodamalis gigas, which grew far larger than any living sirenian.

Steller's sea cow (Hydrodamalis gigas) was a large marine mammal of the order Sirenia, native to the Commander Islands in the Bering Sea. Described scientifically in 1741 by the naturalist Georg Wilhelm Steller, it was hunted to extinction by 1768 — only 27 years after its discovery — making it a classic case of rapid human-caused extinction.

Description

Adults reached lengths estimated at 8 to 9 metres and weights of 8 to 10 tonnes, dwarfing the living manatees and dugong. The animal had thick, black, bark-like skin, a small head, no teeth — instead using horny plates to crush kelp — and a forked, whale-like tail fluke. It fed at the surface on kelp and could not fully submerge, which made it easy prey.

Discovery

Steller encountered the sea cow while shipwrecked with Vitus Bering's expedition on Bering Island in 1741–42. His posthumously published account, De bestiis marinis (1751), remains the primary source on the animal's behaviour and anatomy.

Extinction

The last known population, probably only about 2,000 animals around the Commander Islands, was slaughtered by fur traders and crews provisioning for the sea-otter trade, who valued its meat, fat and hide. The last individual is generally recorded as killed in 1768. Fossil and subfossil remains show the species was once distributed across the North Pacific rim, and its remnant population may already have been reduced by earlier hunting.
