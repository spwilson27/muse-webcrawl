Arabic calligraphy is the artistic practice of handwriting and calligraphy based on the Arabic alphabet. It is known in Arabic as khatt (خط), derived from the word "line", "design", or "construction". Kufic is the oldest form of the Arabic script.

From an artistic point of view, Arabic calligraphy has been known and appreciated for its diversity and great potential for development. In fact, it has been linked in Arabic culture to various fields such as religion, art, architecture, education and craftsmanship, which in turn have played an important role in its advancement.

History

Although most Islamic calligraphy is in Arabic and most Arabic calligraphy is Islamic, the two are not identical. Coptic or other Christian manuscripts in Arabic, for example, make use of calligraphy, as do works in other languages written in Arabic script. Islamic calligraphy developed from two major styles: Kufic and Naskh. The former, angular and monumental, was used for early Qur'an manuscripts; the latter, cursive and legible, became the standard book hand.

Under the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates, calligraphy was refined by masters such as Ibn Muqla (10th century), who systematised the cursive scripts using a proportional system based on the dot and the alif, and Ibn al-Bawwab, who refined his system. Under the Ottomans, calligraphers such as Sheikh Hamdullah and Hâfiz Osman brought the art to new heights, and the Diwani script was developed for imperial decrees.

Styles

Major scripts include Kufic, the angular early script; Naskh, the rounded book hand used for most printed Arabic; Thuluth, a large, elegant display script used in architecture; Diwani, the ornate Ottoman chancery script; Maghrebi, the rounded script of North Africa and al-Andalus; and Ruqʿah, a simple everyday hand. In Persian and Urdu contexts, Nastaliq became the dominant style.

Instruments and materials

The traditional instrument of the Arabic calligrapher is the qalam, a pen made of dried reed or bamboo. Ink, paper (often burnished), and in later periods gold leaf for illumination, complete the toolkit. Calligraphic compositions may take the form of panels (levha), albums (muraqqa), or monumental inscriptions on mosques and other buildings.
