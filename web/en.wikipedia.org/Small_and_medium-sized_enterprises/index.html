Small and medium-sized enterprises


A small business: independent shops typify the SME sector

Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) or small and medium-sized businesses (SMBs) are businesses whose personnel and revenue numbers fall below certain limits. The abbreviation "SME" is used by many national agencies and international organizations such as the World Bank, the OECD, the European Union, the United Nations, and the World Trade Organization (WTO).

In any given national economy, SMEs sometimes outnumber big companies by a wide margin and also employ many more people. For example, Australian SMEs makeup 98% of all Australian businesses, produce one-third of the total GDP, and employ 4.7 million people. In Chile, in the commercial year 2014, 98.5% of the firms were classified as SMEs. In Tunisia, the self-employed workers alone account for about 28% of the total non-farm employment, and firms with fewer than 100 employees account for about 62% of total employment. In the United States, the vast majority of firms are SMEs which employ nearly half of the private-sector workforce.

Definitions

There is no single, universally agreed definition of an SME. Definitions vary by country and commonly use employee numbers, annual turnover, or balance sheet totals. The European Union defines micro-enterprises as having fewer than 10 employees, small enterprises fewer than 50, and medium-sized enterprises fewer than 250, together with turnover or balance-sheet ceilings. In the United States, the Small Business Administration sets size standards by industry, often up to 500 or more employees in manufacturing.

Economic importance

SMEs are major drivers of employment, innovation, and competition. They are often more flexible than large firms and act as suppliers and service providers within larger value chains. At the same time, they face disproportionate difficulties in accessing finance, complying with regulation, and weathering economic downturns, which is why many governments operate dedicated support programmes, loan guarantee schemes, and simplified tax regimes for the sector.

Finance

Limited access to finance is frequently cited as the principal constraint on SME growth, particularly in developing economies. SMEs typically rely on retained earnings and bank lending; the "finance gap" between what firms need and what lenders will supply has led to the development of microfinance, factoring, venture capital, and government-backed lending schemes targeted at smaller firms.
