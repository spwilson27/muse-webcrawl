In law, preclusion refers to the doctrines that prevent a party from relitigating matters that have already been decided by a competent court. In common-law systems the two principal forms are claim preclusion (traditionally res judicata) and issue preclusion (traditionally collateral estoppel).

Claim preclusion

Claim preclusion bars a party from suing again on the same claim once a final judgment on the merits has been rendered. It generally requires the same parties (or their privies), the same claim or cause of action, and a valid final judgment. Claims that could have been raised in the earlier action are usually barred along with those actually litigated.

Issue preclusion

Issue preclusion prevents relitigation of a specific issue of fact or law that was actually litigated, necessarily decided, and essential to a prior judgment, even when the second action involves a different claim. Modern doctrine may allow nonmutual use of issue preclusion, defensively or offensively, subject to fairness limitations.

Purpose

The preclusion doctrines serve judicial economy, consistency of outcomes, and repose: they protect parties from the burden of repeated litigation over the same matters and preserve public confidence in the finality of judgments. Related doctrines include the law of the case and estoppel.
