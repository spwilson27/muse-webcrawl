Video game modding

Video game modding (short for "modification") is the process by which players or fans alter one or more aspects of a video game, such as how it looks or behaves, creating content ranging from small tweaks to total conversions. Mods may add new items, characters, models, textures, levels, story lines, music, or game modes, and have historically extended the lifespan and popularity of many games.

History

Modding grew with PC gaming in the 1980s and 1990s. id Software's Wolfenstein 3D (1992) and Doom (1993), whose WAD file format separated game data from the engine, encouraged a flourishing mod scene. First-person shooters such as Quake and Half-Life shipped with tools that produced major mods; Counter-Strike, created as a Half-Life mod in 1999, became a commercial franchise, and Defense of the Ancients, a Warcraft III custom map, popularized the MOBA genre.

Types of mods

Mods range from cosmetic "skins" and texture packs, to gameplay rebalances, unofficial patches fixing bugs, new quests and maps, and total conversions that create an almost entirely new game. Some developers release official software development kits (SDKs) and level editors, while others tolerate or restrict modification through end-user licence agreements.

Distribution

Mods are distributed through community sites such as Mod DB and Nexus Mods, and through integrated platforms such as Steam Workshop. In 2015 Valve and Bethesda briefly introduced paid mods for The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim, withdrawing the scheme after community backlash; curated paid-content programs have since continued in other forms, such as Bethesda's Creation Club.

Legal and industry aspects

Modding exists in a legal grey area: mods are derivative works, but many publishers encourage them as community engagement. Successful modders have been hired by studios, and some mods have been acquired and released commercially, including Counter-Strike, Team Fortress, Dota 2, and DayZ.
