Mooring (oceanography)

In oceanography, a mooring is a collection of instruments suspended in the water column from a line anchored to the sea floor, used to make long-term measurements of currents, temperature, salinity and other properties at a fixed location. Moorings are a fundamental tool of physical oceanography, allowing scientists to observe ocean variability at one place over months or years.

A typical subsurface mooring: anchor, line, instruments and buoy.

Components

A mooring consists of an anchor (often scrap railway wheels or a concrete block), a line of wire rope or synthetic line, flotation — glass spheres in hard hats or syntactic foam buoys — that holds the line upright, scientific instruments attached along its length, and an acoustic release near the bottom. To recover the mooring, researchers send an acoustic signal that triggers the release; the flotation then carries the line and instruments to the surface. In surface moorings a buoy floats at the surface, supporting meteorological sensors and telemetering data by satellite, as in the tropical moored buoy arrays used for El Niño monitoring. Subsurface moorings keep all equipment below the surface to avoid storms, ice and vandalism.

Measurements

Instruments commonly include current meters and acoustic Doppler current profilers, CTDs (conductivity, temperature, depth), temperature loggers, sediment traps and hydrophones. Long mooring lines in deep water can extend for kilometres, and strong currents may drag the top of a mooring hundreds of metres downward ("knockdown"), which analysts must correct for.
