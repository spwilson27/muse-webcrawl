The Three Lords and Nine Ministers system (Chinese: 三公九卿; pinyin: Sāngōng Jiǔqīng) was a central administrative structure of government in imperial China, in which the three highest officials (the "Three Lords" or "Three Dukes") and nine ministers directed the principal functions of state under the emperor. Its roots lay in the Zhou dynasty, and it was fully developed during the Han dynasty.

The Three Lords

The Three Excellencies, as they were also called, were the Chancellor (Chéngxiàng), who headed the civil administration; the Imperial Counselor (Yùshǐ Dàfū), who served as deputy chancellor and headed the censorate; and the Grand Commandant (Tàiwèi), nominally the highest military officer. Their exact titles and powers changed over the dynasties, and in the Eastern Han they were the Grand Chancellor (Sītú), Grand Commandant (Tàiwèi) and Grand Minister of Works (Sīkōng). They received enormous salaries, ranked at "fully 10,000 bushels", but much real power often rested with the inner court and the imperial secretariat.

The Nine Ministers

The Nine Ministers (or Nine Chamberlains) each headed a major office of state: ceremonies and rites (Minister of Ceremonies), palace attendants and secretaries (Minister of the Household), palace guards (Minister of the Guards), horses and imperial stables (Minister of Coachmen), justice (Minister of Justice), state visits and foreign peoples (Minister of Heraldry), the imperial clan (Minister of the Imperial Clan), finance and agriculture (Minister of Finance), and the imperial household's private purse (Minister of the Privy Treasury). Subordinate bureaus under each minister handled the day-to-day administration of the empire.

Evolution

The system was gradually superseded as emperors built up the Secretariat and Chancellery to bypass the Three Lords. Under the Sui and Tang dynasties the mature central government took the form of the Three Departments and Six Ministries, a structure that, with modifications, endured until the end of imperial China in 1912. The Three Lords meanwhile became largely honorary titles bestowed on senior statesmen.
