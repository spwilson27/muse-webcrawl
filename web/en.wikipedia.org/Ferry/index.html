Ferry

A ferry is a vessel used to carry passengers, and sometimes vehicles and cargo, across a body of water. Most ferries operate on regular, frequent, return services. A passenger-only ferry serving commuters is sometimes called a water taxi or water bus. Many places in the world rely on ferries as integral parts of their public transport systems.

Types

Ferries include chain/reaction ferries powered by river current, cable ferries, double-ended ferries that need not turn around, roll-on/roll-off (ro-ro) ferries carrying road vehicles, high-speed catamaran "fast ferries", hydrofoils, and train ferries with rails on deck. The largest ferries operate on routes such as the English Channel, the Baltic Sea, and across the Cook Strait in New Zealand; notable commuter services include the Staten Island Ferry in New York and the Star Ferry in Hong Kong.

History

Ferry crossings are among the oldest transport services; in Greek mythology Charon ferried the dead across the Styx. Steam ferries became important in the 19th century, and many historic ferry routes were later replaced by bridges or tunnels — though ferry services remain vital where geography, cost, or traffic volumes make fixed links impractical.

Safety

Major ferry disasters, such as the Herald of Free Enterprise (1987) and the Estonia (1994), led to stricter international safety rules for ro-ro vessels under the SOLAS convention, including improved bow-door standards and stability requirements.
