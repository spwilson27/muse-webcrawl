Paul Victor Jules Signac (11 November 1863 – 15 August 1935) was a French Neo-Impressionist painter who, working with Georges Seurat, helped develop the Pointillist style. He was also the movement's principal theorist: his treatise D'Eugène Delacroix au Néo-Impressionnisme (1899) set out the principles of Neo-Impressionism for a generation of younger artists.

Life and work

Signac was born in Paris to a prosperous family of saddle-makers. Originally intending to study architecture, he turned to painting after seeing an exhibition of Claude Monet's work in 1880. In 1884 he met Seurat, whose systematic approach to colour division he immediately adopted; the same year he helped found the Société des Artistes Indépendants, organising its annual Salon des Indépendants, which became the main venue for avant-garde art in Paris.

After Seurat's sudden death in 1891, Signac became the leader and publicist of Neo-Impressionism. His own brushwork gradually loosened from small dots into broader, mosaic-like blocks of pure colour, as in the monumental, utopian In the Time of Harmony (1893–95). An ardent sailor, he painted ports and coasts all around France; in 1892 he settled for part of each year at Saint-Tropez, then a small fishing village, which his presence helped turn into a gathering place for the Fauves — Henri Matisse painted Luxe, Calme et Volupté there under Signac's influence in 1904.

Politics and later years

Like many of his circle, Signac held anarchist sympathies, contributing drawings to Jean Grave's publications and titling works after anarchist theory. He remained president of the Société des Artistes Indépendants from 1908 until his death, using the position to champion young modernists. His later watercolours of French harbours, painted on extensive travels, are among his most celebrated works.
