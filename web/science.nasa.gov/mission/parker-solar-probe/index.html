Parker Solar Probe

The first spacecraft to fly through the Sun's atmosphere — the corona — coming closer to our star than any mission before it, and travelling faster than any human-made object.

About the Mission

Parker Solar Probe launched on 12 August 2018 from Cape Canaveral aboard a Delta IV Heavy. Using seven Venus gravity assists, it has wound its orbit ever tighter around the Sun. In April 2021 it crossed into the corona for the first time, “touching the Sun”. Its closest approach came on 24 December 2024, when it passed within about 6.1 million kilometres (3.8 million miles) of the solar surface at roughly 690,000 km/h (430,000 mph) — about 0.064% of the speed of light — with further record-equalling passes in 2025.

The spacecraft survives behind a carbon-composite heat shield about 11 centimetres thick, which faces temperatures near 1,370 °C while keeping the instruments close to room temperature. Parker is studying why the corona is far hotter than the Sun's surface, and how the solar wind is accelerated — questions first posed theoretically by the mission's namesake, Eugene Parker, in the 1950s. He was the first person to have a NASA mission named after them while still living, and watched the launch at age 91. The mission is managed by the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, which also built the spacecraft.

Fast Facts

Launch12 August 2018, Delta IV Heavy, Cape Canaveral
Closest approach~6.1 million km from the Sun's surface (24 Dec 2024; repeated in 2025)
Top speed~690,000 km/h (~430,000 mph) — fastest human-made object
Heat shieldCarbon composite, ~11 cm thick; front face ~1,370 °C
Gravity assistsSeven Venus flybys, 2018–2024
NamesakeEugene Parker, who predicted the solar wind in 1958
Lead centreJohns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory (APL)
