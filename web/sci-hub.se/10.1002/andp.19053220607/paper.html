Annalen der Physik

Über die von der molekularkinetischen Theorie der Wärme geforderte Bewegung von in ruhenden Flüssigkeiten suspendierten Teilchen
(On the Movement of Small Particles Suspended in Stationary Liquids Required by the Molecular-Kinetic Theory of Heat)

A. Einstein
Bern, Switzerland
DOI: 10.1002/andp.19053220607
Abstract. In this paper it will be shown that, according to the molecular-kinetic theory of heat, bodies of microscopically visible size suspended in a liquid will perform movements of such magnitude that they can be easily observed in a microscope, on account of the molecular motions of heat. The movement discussed here is presumably the same as the so-called "Brownian molecular movement", although the available data on the latter are so imprecise that no definite judgement can be formed. The theory of the movement is developed: suspended particles exert an osmotic pressure obeying the gas laws; their diffusion coefficient is D = (RT/N) · 1/(6πηa); and their mean square displacement in time t is ⟨x2⟩ = 2Dt. If the predictions are confirmed, an exact determination of Avogadro's number N and of molecular dimensions becomes possible; if they are not, that would be a weighty argument against the molecular-kinetic conception of heat.

Introduction

In this paper it will be shown that according to the molecular-kinetic theory of heat, bodies of microscopically-visible size suspended in liquids must, as a result of thermal molecular motions, perform motions of such magnitude that they can easily be detected with a microscope. It is possible that the motions to be discussed here are identical with the so-called "Brownian molecular motion"; however, the information available to me regarding the latter is so lacking in precision, that I can form no judgement in the matter.

If the movement discussed here can actually be observed (together with the laws it is expected to obey), then classical thermodynamics can no longer be considered applicable with precision even to bodies of dimensions distinguishable in a microscope, and an exact determination of actual atomic sizes becomes possible. On the other hand, if the prediction of this movement were to prove incorrect, that would be a weighty argument against the molecular-kinetic conception of heat.

§1. On the osmotic pressure of suspended particles

Let a liquid fill the volume V, and let n small particles (spheres of radius a, say) be suspended in a partial volume V* of it, separated from the pure liquid by a wall permeable to the liquid but not to the particles. If the suspended particles, taken as a whole, neither attract nor repel, the kinetic theory assigns to them the behaviour of dissolved molecules: they exert on the semipermeable wall an osmotic pressure p given by van't Hoff's law,

p V* = (R/N) n T,

where N is the number of actual molecules in a gram-molecule. This requires only that the free energy assignable to the suspended particles be counted exactly as for molecules in solution: the particles, however large compared with molecules, participate in the thermal agitation as independent kinetic units, each carrying mean kinetic energy (3/2)(R/N)T in three dimensions.

§3. Theory of the diffusion of small spheres suspended in a liquid

Let the suspended particles be spheres of radius a in a liquid of viscosity η. A force K acting on one particle imparts to it, by Stokes's law for the resistance of a sphere, the velocity K/(6πηa). Combining this mobility with the osmotic driving force of §2, the number of particles crossing unit area per unit time is −D ∂ν/∂x with the diffusion coefficient

D = (RT/N) · 1 / (6πηa).

The mean free path and collision details of the liquid do not enter: only the temperature, the viscosity, the particle radius, and Avogadro's number. This relation, which connects a directly measurable transport coefficient with the size of molecules, is the pivot of the experimental test.

§4. On the irregular motion of particles suspended in a liquid, and its relation to diffusion

We turn now to the motion of the individual particle. In a short time interval τ — long compared with the time between molecular impacts, short compared with times of observation — the particle's displacement Δ along the x-axis may be regarded as a chance quantity, independent of its previous history, distributed according to some function φ(Δ) with ∫φ(Δ) dΔ = 1 and φ(Δ) = φ(−Δ). If f(x, t) dx is the number of particles found between x and x + dx at time t, the redistribution during τ gives

f(x, t + τ) = ∫ f(x − Δ, t) φ(Δ) dΔ.

Expanding in powers of τ and Δ and retaining the first non-vanishing terms (the linear term in Δ vanishes by symmetry), one obtains the diffusion equation

∂f/∂t = D ∂2f/∂x2,    with   D = (1/2τ) ∫ Δ2 φ(Δ) dΔ,

the same D as in §3 — as it must be, for the irregular wandering of the particles and their collective diffusion are one and the same phenomenon. With all particles initially at the origin, the solution is the Gaussian

f(x, t) = (n / √(4πDt)) e−x2/4Dt,

and the root-mean-square displacement in time t is

√⟨x2⟩ = √(2Dt) = √{ t · (RT/N) · 1/(3πηa) }.

The typical displacement grows only as the square root of the time: the path is not a trajectory in the ordinary sense but a random walk, and the "velocity" of a suspended particle is not a well-defined observable.

§5. Formula for the mean displacement and the determination of Avogadro's number

For spherical particles of diameter 0.001 mm suspended in water at 17°C (η = 1.35 × 10−2 in c.g.s. units), the formula gives a mean displacement in one minute of about 6 × 10−4 cm — roughly 6 microns — and in one hour about ten times less than a tenth of a millimetre, since the displacement grows as √t. Such displacements are readily measurable under the microscope. Conversely, an observer who measures √⟨x2⟩, t, η and a obtains

N = (RT t) / (3πηa ⟨x2⟩),

a new determination of Avogadro's number, and hence of the true sizes of atoms, from the dance of visible grains. It is to be hoped that some researcher will soon succeed in deciding the question raised here, which is so important for the theory of heat.
