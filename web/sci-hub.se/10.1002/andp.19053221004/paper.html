Annalen der Physik
322 (10): 891–921 (1905) · received 30 June 1905 · English rendering of the German original

Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper
(On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies)

A. Einstein
Bern, Switzerland
DOI: 10.1002/andp.19053221004
Abstract. Maxwell's electrodynamics, as usually understood, applied to moving bodies leads to asymmetries which do not appear to be inherent in the phenomena; and the failure of all attempts to detect a motion of the Earth relative to the "light medium" suggests that the concepts of absolute rest and of a luminiferous ether are superfluous. The paper develops a kinematics and electrodynamics founded on two postulates: the principle of relativity — the laws of physics take the same form in all inertial coordinate systems — and the principle of the constancy of the velocity of light, independent of the motion of the source. From these follow the relativity of simultaneity, the Lorentz transformations, the contraction of lengths and retardation of clocks in motion, the theorem of addition of velocities, the transformation of the electromagnetic field, the Doppler principle and aberration, and a dynamics of the electron in which its inertia increases with velocity and no velocity can exceed that of light.

Introduction

It is known that Maxwell's electrodynamics — as usually understood at the present time — when applied to moving bodies, leads to asymmetries which do not appear to be inherent in the phenomena. Take, for example, the reciprocal electrodynamic action of a magnet and a conductor. The observable phenomenon here depends only on the relative motion of the conductor and the magnet, whereas the customary view draws a sharp distinction between the two cases in which either the one or the other of these bodies is in motion. For if the magnet is in motion and the conductor at rest, there arises in the neighbourhood of the magnet an electric field with a certain definite energy, producing a current at the places where parts of the conductor are situated. But if the magnet is stationary and the conductor in motion, no electric field arises in the neighbourhood of the magnet; in the conductor, however, we find an electromotive force, to which in itself there is no corresponding energy, but which gives rise — assuming equality of relative motion in the two cases discussed — to electric currents of the same path and intensity as those produced by the electric forces in the former case.

Examples of this sort, together with the unsuccessful attempts to discover any motion of the earth relatively to the "light medium", suggest that the phenomena of electrodynamics as well as of mechanics possess no properties corresponding to the idea of absolute rest. They suggest rather that, as has already been shown for quantities of the first order, the same laws of electrodynamics and optics will be valid for all frames of reference for which the equations of mechanics hold good. We will raise this conjecture (the purport of which will hereafter be called the "principle of relativity") to the status of a postulate, and also introduce another postulate, which is only apparently irreconcilable with the former, namely, that light is always propagated in empty space with a definite velocity c which is independent of the state of motion of the emitting body. These two postulates suffice for the attainment of a simple and consistent theory of the electrodynamics of moving bodies based on Maxwell's theory for stationary bodies. The introduction of a "luminiferous ether" will prove to be superfluous inasmuch as the view here to be developed will not require an "absolutely stationary space" provided with special properties.

I. Kinematical Part

§1. Definition of simultaneity

Let us take a system of coordinates in which the equations of Newtonian mechanics hold good. In order to render our presentation more precise and to distinguish this system of coordinates verbally from others which are introduced hereafter, we call it the "stationary system". If a material point is at rest relatively to this system, its position can be defined by measurements with rigid rods. But the time of an event requires more care. The time obtained with a clock at the origin of coordinates serves only for events in its immediate neighbourhood. To speak of the time of a distant event we require a definition of simultaneity: two clocks at A and B are synchronous if a light signal sent from A at time tA, reflected at B at time tB, and returning to A at t′A, satisfies

tB − tA = t′A − tB,    i.e.   tB = ½(tA + t′A).

It is assumed that this definition can be given without contradiction for any number of points, and that the relations so established are consistent. "Time" so defined is the set of readings of synchronised clocks at rest in the stationary system.

§2. On the relativity of lengths and times

Consider a second system k moving with velocity v along the x-axis of the stationary system K. A rigid rod laid along the axis of k has, judged from K, the length rab = l√(1 − v2/c2), different from its length l in its own system: a rigid body in motion is shortened in the direction of motion. And a clock moving with the system k, observed from K, marks time more slowly: a period which is τ in k is τ/√(1 − v2/c2) in K. More fundamental is the result for simultaneity itself: two events simultaneous in K (Δt = 0, Δx ≠ 0) are not simultaneous in k. Observers in relative motion each pronounce the other's clocks out of synchrony; simultaneity is not absolute but depends on the state of motion of the system of reference.

§3. Theory of the transformation of co-ordinates and times

From the definitions of §§1–2, requiring that a spherical light wave x2 + y2 + z2 = c2t2 in K shall also satisfy ξ2 + η2 + ζ2 = c2τ2 in k, and that the transformation be linear and consistent with the relativity principle, one obtains uniquely (up to a factor φ(v) shown to equal 1):

τ = β ( t − vx/c2 ),    ξ = β ( x − vt ),    η = y,    ζ = z,

with   β = 1 / √(1 − v2/c2).

These are the Lorentz transformations. For v << c they pass into the Galilean transformations. If v = c were admitted the factor β would become infinite; if v > c the transformation would cease to be real. The velocity of light thus plays the part of an infinitely great velocity in this kinematics.

§4. Physical meaning of the equations obtained

Applied to a rigid sphere at rest in k, the transformation gives, as seen from K, an ellipsoid contracted in the ratio √(1 − v2/c2) : 1 along the direction of motion. A clock at the equator of the system k, compared with an identical clock at its pole, appears from K to run the slower by the fraction ½v2/c2 (to second order). These are not artefacts of the definitions: they state the behaviour of real rods and clocks, whose constitution must in the end be explained by the same principles.

§5. The theorem of the addition of velocities

A point moving in k with components (wξ, wη) has in K the velocity components

ux = (wξ + v) / (1 + v wξ/c2),    uy = wη √(1 − v2/c2) / (1 + v wξ/c2).

Hence velocities compounded in this way never exceed c: if wξ = c, then ux = c. Applied to the propagation of light in a moving liquid (Fizeau's experiment), with refractive index n, the dragging coefficient becomes 1 − 1/n2: Fresnel's coefficient follows from the addition theorem without any hypothesis about the ether.

II. Electrodynamical Part

§6. Transformation of the Maxwell–Hertz equations

Requiring Maxwell's equations for empty space to hold in K and to keep their form in k under the transformations of §3, the electric and magnetic force components (X, Y, Z) and (L, M, N) in K are connected with those (′) in k by

X′ = X,   Y′ = β(Y − vN/c),   Z′ = β(Z + vM/c),

L′ = L,   M′ = β(M + vZ/c),   N′ = β(N − vY/c).

The electric and magnetic forces therefore have no separate existence independent of the state of motion of the coordinate system: what is a purely electric field in one system is a mixture of electric and magnetic field in another. The asymmetry of the introduction — magnet moving versus conductor moving — disappears, for the electromotive force in the conductor is in both cases the same physical field in the conductor's own system.

§7. Theory of Doppler's principle and of aberration

For a plane light wave of frequency ν whose normals make the angle φ with the x-axis, the transformation gives a frequency in k:

ν′ = ν (1 − (v/c) cos φ) / √(1 − v2/c2),

and for φ = 0 the simple form ν′ = ν √((1 − v/c)/(1 + v/c)): the relativistic Doppler effect, including a transverse effect of second order. The direction of the ray transforms by cos φ′ = (cos φ − v/c)/(1 − (v/c) cos φ), which for light from a distant star is the law of aberration. For infinitely great source velocity the formula continues to give a finite received frequency — the light of a body receding at nearly c is shifted, not extinguished.

§8. Transformation of the energy of light rays; theory of radiation pressure

The energy of a light complex transforms as its frequency, A′2 = A2 (1 − (v/c)cosφ)2/(1 − v2/c2), so that the ratio of energy to frequency is an invariant of the motion. Reflection at a perfectly reflecting mirror moving with velocity v gives a radiation pressure which differs from the stationary value by terms in v/c; the mirror problem, treated with the transformed quantities, satisfies energy conservation exactly in both systems.

§9. Dynamics of the slowly accelerated electron

Let an electron of charge e move in an electromagnetic field. In its instantaneous rest system the equation of motion is m d2x/dt2 = eX, etc. Transforming to K, in which the electron has velocity v, its acceleration components involve two different inertial coefficients: the "longitudinal mass" mβ3 and the "transverse mass" mβ. The kinetic energy of the electron is found to be

W = mc2 ( 1/√(1 − v2/c2) − 1 ),

which becomes infinitely great as v approaches c. Thus velocities greater than that of light have no possibility of existence for a material particle initially at rest; and the expression of the energy suggests that the inertia of a body is connected with its energy content in a manner to be developed elsewhere. The results for the electron's motion agree in form with those of Lorentz's theory, but are obtained without any hypothesis about the electron's shape or contraction.
