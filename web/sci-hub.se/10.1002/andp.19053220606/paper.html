Annalen der Physik
322 (6): 132–148 (1905) · received 18 March 1905 · English rendering of the German original

Über einen die Erzeugung und Verwandlung des Lichtes betreffenden heuristischen Gesichtspunkt
(On a Heuristic Point of View Concerning the Production and Transformation of Light)

A. Einstein
Bern, Switzerland
DOI: 10.1002/andp.19053220606
Abstract. A profound formal difference exists between the theoretical conceptions of gases and other ponderable bodies, in which matter is treated as built of discrete atoms, and Maxwell's theory of electromagnetic processes in empty space, in which the state is described by continuous spatial functions. This difference comes to a head in the theory of black-body radiation, photoluminescence, the emission of cathode rays by ultraviolet light, and other phenomena of the production and transformation of light. It is shown that the entropy of monochromatic radiation in the range of validity of Wien's law depends on volume exactly as the entropy of an ideal gas: radiation therefore behaves, thermodynamically, as if it consisted of mutually independent energy quanta of magnitude hν. Adopting this heuristic point of view as a working hypothesis for emission and absorption yields simple laws for Stokes's rule, for the photoelectric effect — in particular a linear relation between the frequency of the light and the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons — and for the ionisation of gases by ultraviolet light.

Introduction

The wave theory of light, operating with continuous spatial functions, has proved itself splendidly in the description of purely optical phenomena and will probably never be replaced by another theory. Nevertheless we must bear in mind that optical observations refer to time averages, not to instantaneous values; and it is conceivable that despite the complete confirmation of the theory of diffraction, reflection, refraction and dispersion by experiment, the theory of light, operating with continuous spatial functions, may lead to contradictions with experience when it is applied to the phenomena of the production and transformation of light.

Indeed it seems to me that the observations on "black-body radiation", photoluminescence, the production of cathode rays by ultraviolet light, and other groups of phenomena associated with the production and transformation of light can be understood better under the assumption that the energy of light is distributed discontinuously in space. In this paper I wish to present the train of thought and cite the facts which suggested this path to me, in the hope that the point of view to be presented may prove useful to some researchers in their investigations.

§1. On a difficulty concerning the theory of black-body radiation

Let us first consider the standpoint of Maxwell's theory and electron theory. A resonator electron in a cavity filled with radiation at temperature T must, by equipartition of energy, possess on average the kinetic energy (R/N)T; applying the relation between resonator energy and radiation density, one obtains for the spectral distribution

ρν = (8πν2/c3) (R/N) T,

a law which not only contradicts experience (it is not confirmed by any experiment at large ν/T) but also states that in the distribution of energy between ether and matter no definite distribution can exist at all: the shorter the wavelength, the more energy must on this theory reside in the radiation, without limit. We shall see below that this conclusion, which follows necessarily from the received foundations, is contradicted by a consideration of the entropy of radiation in the domain where Wien's law holds.

§2. On Planck's determination of the elementary quanta

[Planck's radiation formula and the values of its constants are reviewed: with β = 4.866 × 10−11 deg·sec and R = 8.31 × 107, the energy element is (R/N)βν with N = 6.17 × 1023. We shall use this determination in what follows.]

§3. On the entropy of radiation

The entropy of radiation of frequency ν enclosed in a volume V, with energy E, may be obtained from Wien's distribution law (valid for large ν/T) in the form

S = − (E/βν) { ln( E / (αν3V) ) − 1 }.

Hence, for one and the same energy, the difference of entropy between volumes V and V0 is

S − S0 = (E / βν) ln(V/V0).

§4. Limiting law for the entropy of monochromatic radiation at low density

For an ideal gas of n molecules, or any system of n mutually independent entities moving in a volume, Boltzmann's principle gives for a change of volume at constant energy

S − S0 = (R/N) n ln(V/V0).

Comparison of the two expressions yields the conclusion: Monochromatic radiation of low density (within the range of validity of Wien's radiation formula) behaves thermodynamically as if it consisted of mutually independent energy quanta of magnitude (R/N)βν = hν. If, therefore, the entropy of radiation behaves as that of a gas of independent quanta, it is natural to examine whether the laws of the production and transformation of light are not also constituted as if light consisted of such energy quanta. On this point the following sections are based; it is to be emphasised that the conception is a heuristic working hypothesis, to be judged by its fruits.

§5. Molecular-theoretical investigation of the volume-dependence of the entropy of gases and dilute solutions

[The probability basis of the comparison is secured: for n independent particles the probability that they are found by chance in the fraction V/V0 of a volume is W = (V/V0)n, whence with S = (R/N) ln W the gas formula of §4 follows. The analogy of §4 is therefore not formal but expresses a genuine identity of statistical behaviour.]

§6. Stokes's rule

In photoluminescence, light of frequency ν produces light of lower frequency ν′. If each incident quantum hν is wholly transformed into one emergent quantum, energy conservation gives hν′ ≤ hν, i.e. ν′ ≤ ν: the emitted light must be of lower frequency than the exciting light. This is Stokes's rule, here obtained without any assumption about the mechanism, and the known exceptions are explained when several quanta cooperate or thermal energy contributes.

§7. The production of cathode rays by illumination of solid bodies (photoelectric effect)

According to the conception developed here, the energy of an incident light quantum is transferred entire to a single electron. An electron so struck will leave the body with kinetic energy

½mv2max = hν − P,    i.e.   Π = (R/N)βν − P,

where P is the work the electron must perform in leaving the body (the work function), and Π is expressed in terms of the retarding potential which just stops the emitted electrons. Three testable consequences follow. First, the maximum electron energy depends linearly on the frequency of the light, with a slope (R/N)β = h/e that is the same for all substances. Second, there exists for each body a threshold frequency ν0 = P/h below which no electrons are emitted, however intense the light. Third, the number of emitted electrons is proportional to the intensity of the light (the number of quanta), while their energies are not. Lenard's observations — in particular that the electron energies are independent of the intensity — are in qualitative accord with all three statements; a quantitative test awaits more precise measurements.

§8. On the ionisation of gases by ultraviolet light

If each absorbed light quantum ionises a single gas molecule, then the number of ions formed per unit energy absorbed must be proportional to 1/hν, and ionisation must cease for wavelengths longer than a limit set by the ionisation energy of the molecule. The data of Lenard on the absorbing power of gases and the ionisation produced are consistent with this picture.

Conclusion

The conception that light in emission and absorption behaves as if composed of independent quanta of energy hν has here been applied to three groups of phenomena and in each case has yielded the observed laws in their simplest form. It is not proposed as a complete theory of radiation — how the quanta are to be reconciled with the wave phenomena of propagation remains an open question — but as a heuristic point of view whose usefulness these applications may serve to demonstrate.
