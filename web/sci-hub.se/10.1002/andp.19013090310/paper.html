Annalen der Physik
309 (3): 553–563 (1901) · received 7 March 1901 · English rendering of the German original

Ueber das Gesetz der Energieverteilung im Normalspektrum
(On the Law of Distribution of Energy in the Normal Spectrum)

M. Planck
Berlin
DOI: 10.1002/andp.19013090310
Abstract. The recent measurements of the energy distribution in the normal spectrum, in particular those of Rubens and Kurlbaum extending to long wavelengths, show departures from Wien's distribution law that no modification of that law in its original form can remove. It is shown here that the spectral equation reported by the author in October 1900 follows necessarily from the electromagnetic theory of radiation combined with Boltzmann's principle relating entropy to probability, provided the energy of a resonator is treated as composed of a definite integral number of finite, equal energy elements ε = hν. The constants h and k so introduced are universal; from the radiation measurements their values, and with them Avogadro's number and the elementary quantum of electricity, can be computed with an accuracy exceeding previous determinations.

Introduction

By Kirchhoff's theorem, the energy density uν of black radiation between frequencies ν and ν + dν is a universal function of the frequency and the temperature alone: uν dν = ν3 f(ν/T) dν. Wien showed that the function f must, on thermodynamical grounds supplemented by the Doppler principle, take the form

uν = α ν3 e−βν/T,

and for a time the measurements of Lummer and Pringsheim seemed to confirm this law. The newer observations, however — above all the isotherms of Rubens and Kurlbaum for residual rays of wavelength up to 60 μ, obtained with fluorspar and rock salt — establish beyond doubt that at large values of λT the energy density is proportional to the temperature itself, u ∝ T, whereas Wien's formula requires it to fall far below this behaviour. The spectral equation which I communicated to the German Physical Society on 19 October 1900,

uν = C ν3 / ( ecν/T − 1 ),

represents all the measurements, including those at the longest wavelengths, within the limits of observational error. In what follows I shall show that this equation is not a fortunate interpolation merely, but follows with necessity from a definite hypothesis concerning the distribution of energy among the resonators which emit and absorb the radiation.

1. The entropy of a resonator

Consider a linear resonator of proper frequency ν, situated in a stationary radiation field. Between its mean energy U and the energy density of the radiation at that frequency there subsists the relation, derived earlier by the author from Maxwell's theory,

U = (c3 / 8πν2) uν.

The whole problem therefore reduces to finding U as a function of T. We proceed by way of the entropy S of the resonator, defined by dS = dU/T. If one assumes Wien's law, one obtains S as a function whose second derivative is d2S/dU2 = −α/U. The measurements demanding u ∝ T at long wavelengths require instead, for small energies, a behaviour leading to d2S/dU2 → −const./U2 … The function which satisfies both limits, and which reproduces the empirical spectral equation exactly, is

d2S/dU2 = − α / ( U ( β′ + U ) ),

whence by integration, with the integration constants fixed so that S vanishes with U,

S = k { (1 + U/ε) ln(1 + U/ε) − (U/ε) ln(U/ε) },

where ε is a quantity depending on the frequency alone, and k is a universal constant. From 1/T = dS/dU there follows U = ε / (eε/kT − 1), and with the resonator relation above, precisely the spectral equation quoted in the introduction, if one sets ε = hν. It remains to give this result a physical foundation: to explain why the entropy must have the form written, and why the energy element ε is proportional to the frequency.

2. Energy elements and the number of complexions

We adopt Boltzmann's principle, S = k log W, according to which the entropy of a system in a given state is proportional to the logarithm of the probability of that state. Consider N resonators of the same frequency ν, with total energy E = NU. If the energy were divisible without limit, the number of possible distributions would be infinite and the principle inapplicable. We therefore make the hypothesis — the essential point of the whole calculation — that the energy E is composed of a completely determined number of finite equal parts: P energy elements, each of magnitude ε, so that E = Pε.

The number of ways ("complexions") in which P indistinguishable energy elements can be distributed among N distinguishable resonators is a familiar combinatorial count:

W = (N + P − 1)! / ( (N − 1)! P! ).

The entropy of the whole system is SN = k log W; applying Stirling's formula and dividing by N, the entropy of a single resonator becomes

S = k { (1 + U/ε) ln(1 + U/ε) − (U/ε) ln(U/ε) },    U/ε = P/N,

exactly the function demanded by the measurements in §1. The hypothesis has led to the required result; probability considerations supply what the interpolation formula lacked, a theory.

3. The spectral law and the constant h

For resonators of different frequencies the energy elements cannot be taken equal if the spectral equation is to have the observed form for all ν simultaneously and satisfy Wien's displacement law, uν dν = ν3 f(ν/T) dν. This requires ε to be proportional to ν:

ε = hν,

where h is a universal constant, a new constant of nature. There results the complete distribution law

uν = (8πhν3 / c3) · 1 / ( ehν/kT − 1 ),

or, expressed in wavelengths,

uλ = (8πhc / λ5) · 1 / ( ehc/λkT − 1 ).

For small ν/T this reduces to Wien's law; for large λT it gives u ∝ T, as Rubens and Kurlbaum found; in between it reproduces every measured isotherm quantitatively.

4. Numerical values of the universal constants

Fitting the two constants of the law to the most reliable measurements (those of Kurlbaum for the total radiation, and of Lummer and Pringsheim for the spectral distribution) one obtains

h = 6.55 × 10−27 erg·sec,    k = 1.346 × 10−16 erg/deg.

From k there follow, by the relations of the kinetic theory of gases, values of quantities hitherto known only roughly. The number of molecules in one gram-molecule is N = 6.17 × 1023; the mass of the hydrogen atom is 1.64 × 10−24 g; and the elementary quantum of electricity, obtained from Faraday's constant, is e = 4.69 × 10−10 electrostatic units. These numbers agree with independent determinations (for instance those from the kinetic theory of gases and from cathode rays) as well as can be expected, and in the author's judgement the radiation measurements determine them more certainly than any method previously available.

5. Concluding remarks

The theory here developed rests on the conception that the energy of a resonator is an integral multiple of the element hν. Whether this discreteness belongs to the mechanism of emission and absorption themselves, or only to the statistical manner in which we here reckon the distributions, is a question the present considerations leave open; the entropy formula requires the counting of complexions, and the counting requires finite elements. Should experience compel us to take the elements as physically real, the consequences for the theory of radiation, and indeed for the whole of molecular physics, will be far-reaching.
