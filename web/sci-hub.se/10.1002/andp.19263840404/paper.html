Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem
(Quantisation as an Eigenvalue Problem)

E. Schrödinger
Physical Institute of the University of Zurich
DOI: 10.1002/andp.19263840404
Abstract. The usual rules of quantisation are here replaced by a principle of a different kind, in which no mention is made of "allowed orbits" or integer conditions imposed from outside. Starting from the Hamilton–Jacobi equation and the optical–mechanical analogy, and taking seriously de Broglie's conception of phase waves associated with a material particle, the mechanical process is represented by a wave process governed by a variational principle. The requirement that a certain quadratic integral be stationary leads to a partial differential equation — the wave equation — whose solutions exist, finite and single-valued throughout configuration space, only for discrete values of the energy parameter E. Quantisation thus appears as an eigenvalue problem, exactly as the discrete frequencies of a vibrating string arise from its boundary conditions. The method is carried through for the hydrogen atom, yielding the Balmer terms En = −2π2me4/(h2n2) without any quantum condition being postulated.

§1. The Hamilton–Jacobi equation and the wave ansatz

The quantum conditions of Bohr and Sommerfeld, for all their success, retain an arbitrary character: integers enter the theory by decree, and the mechanics of the atom is patched rather than explained. A hint toward a deeper understanding lies in Hamilton's analogy between mechanics and geometrical optics. The Hamilton–Jacobi equation for a particle of mass m in a potential V,

( ∇S )2 = 2m ( E − V ),

describes surfaces S = const. advancing in configuration space like wave fronts, the trajectories being their orthogonal paths, just as rays are orthogonal to optical wave fronts. In optics the eikonal equation is itself only the short-wavelength approximation to a genuine wave equation. We therefore ask: what wave process has S as its phase? Following de Broglie, we seek a scalar function ψ(x, y, z) satisfying a wave equation such that S is related to ψ by

S = K ln ψ,

with a constant K yet to be determined (K will turn out to be h/2π, possibly multiplied by i; in this first communication the theory is developed for the stationary, real case). The phase velocity of the wave belonging to energy E is u = E / √(2m(E − V)).

§2. The variational principle and the wave equation

Substituting the ansatz into the Hamilton–Jacobi equation transforms it into a condition on ψ that may be written as the vanishing of a variation. We postulate: the function ψ is to make the integral

J = ∫ { (h2/8π2m) [ (∂ψ/∂x)2 + (∂ψ/∂y)2 + (∂ψ/∂z)2 ] − V ψ2 } dτ

stationary, subject to the normalisation ∫ ψ2 dτ = 1, the integrals extending over all configuration space. The Euler–Lagrange equation of this variational problem, with the Lagrange multiplier identified as the total energy E, is

Δψ + (8π2m/h2) ( E − V ) ψ = 0,

that is, with K = h/2π,

∇2ψ + (2m/K2) ( E − V ) ψ = 0.

This is the wave equation of the problem. The quantum conditions have disappeared. In their place stands a boundary-value requirement: ψ must be finite, positive, single-valued and twice continuously differentiable at every point of configuration space. Experience with the analogous problems of acoustics and elasticity teaches that such conditions can be met, in general, only for a discrete set of values of the parameter E — the eigenvalues — to which there belong definite eigenfunctions ψ. Quantisation is an eigenvalue problem.

§3. Application to the hydrogen atom

For an electron (charge −e, mass m) in the field of a fixed nucleus, V = −e2/r, and the wave equation becomes

Δψ + (2m/K2) ( E + e2/r ) ψ = 0.

Seeking solutions in polar coordinates as products of a radial function and a spherical surface harmonic, the radial equation reduces, after the substitution ψr = χ(r)/r, to an equation soluble by Laplace's method in terms of the generalised Laguerre polynomials. The demand that the solution remain finite at the origin and vanish at infinity can be satisfied for negative E only when a certain combination of the parameters is a positive integer n. The admissible energies are

En = − ( 2π2 m e4 / h2 ) · 1/n2,    n = 1, 2, 3, …

exactly the Balmer terms of the hydrogen spectrum, obtained here without quantisation rules: the integer n counts, in effect, the nodes of the eigenfunction. For positive E the boundary conditions admit a continuum of solutions, corresponding physically to the ionised atom and the hyperbolic orbits of the older theory; the sharp division of the spectrum into a discrete and a continuous part emerges of itself. The eigenfunctions belonging to the same eigenvalue may be degenerate, the degeneracy being that of the spherical harmonics; their orthogonality is a general property of the variational problem.

§4. Discussion

The proposed representation dispenses with the electron orbit as a primitive concept. What oscillates is a scalar field in configuration space; the "states" of the atom are its proper vibrations, and radiation may be pictured as arising from the beating of eigenfunctions of neighbouring eigenvalues against one another, the emitted frequency being determined by the difference of the corresponding terms, as Bohr's frequency condition requires. It is not claimed that the physical meaning of ψ is exhausted by these remarks. Whether ψ describes a real process in space and time, or is an instrument of calculation, further investigation must show. But the replacement of arbitrary integer conditions by the natural boundary conditions of a partial differential equation appears to the author an advance in principle, whatever interpretation is finally adopted. The extension of the method to the harmonic oscillator, the rotator and the diatomic molecule, and to systems under perturbation, is reserved for subsequent communications.
