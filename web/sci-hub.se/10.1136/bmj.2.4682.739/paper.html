Smoking and Carcinoma of the Lung: Preliminary Report

Richard Doll and A. Bradford Hill

London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine; Medical Research Council Statistical Research Unit

British Medical Journal 2 (4682): 739–748 (30 September 1950). DOI: 10.1136/bmj.2.4682.739

SUMMARY

In a retrospective inquiry, 649 men and 60 women with carcinoma of the lung were compared with an equal number of control patients of the same sex, age and hospital distribution but without lung cancer. The groups differed strikingly in smoking habits: non-smokers formed a much smaller fraction of the carcinoma patients than of the controls, and heavy smokers a much larger fraction. Among men, of patients with carcinoma of the lung 26 per cent were in the heaviest smoking category (25 or more cigarettes a day) compared with about 13.5 per cent of controls, while non-smokers were 0.3 per cent of the carcinoma group against 4.2 per cent of controls (percentages as remembered). The probability of lung carcinoma rose with the amount smoked. The authors conclude that smoking is a factor, and an important factor, in the production of carcinoma of the lung, while expressly reserving final judgment pending confirmatory and prospective studies. 1. Introduction: a new epidemic

Between 1922 and 1947 the annual number of deaths attributed to cancer of the lung in England and Wales rose roughly fifteen-fold, a change far exceeding any improvement in diagnosis. Two candidate causes dominated discussion: the general pollution of the air by smoke, exhaust fumes and tarred roads, and the personal habit of smoking tobacco, whose consumption had grown over the same decades and whose smoke passes directly over the bronchial epithelium. Earlier inquiries abroad (Müller in Cologne, 1939; Schairer and Schöniger in Jena, 1943) and a simultaneous American study (Wynder and Graham, 1950) had suggested the smoking hypothesis, but each was open to objections of method. The Medical Research Council therefore commissioned a large, carefully controlled retrospective study in London hospitals, the preliminary report of which is this paper.

2. Method

Twenty London hospitals notified patients admitted with carcinoma of the lung, stomach or large bowel; trained almoners and investigators interviewed them with a standard questionnaire covering residence, occupation, heating and — in detail — smoking history: age at starting, amount smoked, changes in habit, inhalation, pipe versus cigarettes. The lung-carcinoma patients (709 in all: 649 men, 60 women) were matched with control patients from the same hospitals, of the same sex and five-year age group, who did not have lung carcinoma — drawing partly on the stomach- and bowel-cancer patients and partly on general medical and surgical cases. Care was taken to handle the obvious traps: patients were classified by habits before any change induced by illness, and analyses allowed for the London/non-London distribution of the groups.

3. Results

The contrast was large and consistent. Among men, all but 2 of the 649 lung-carcinoma patients had been smokers of some kind, against a significantly higher non-smoking fraction among controls; among women the same pattern held in miniature (about 68 per cent of carcinoma patients smoked, against about 46 per cent of controls, as remembered). The association was stronger for cigarettes than for pipes, was present for patients from greater London and from elsewhere, in manual and non-manual occupations, and persisted after the various standardisations the data allowed. Patients with carcinoma of the stomach or large bowel, interviewed identically, showed no comparable excess — a control on the method as much as on the patients.

4. Discussion

The authors weigh alternative explanations with notable care: that diagnostic fashion inflated lung-cancer deaths (true, they allow, but not enough); that carcinoma patients might recall or report habits differently (guarded against by the identical interview and the negative control diseases); that an unknown predisposition might cause both smoking and cancer (the constitutional hypothesis later pressed by Fisher — possible, but strained by the dose-response gradient). Atmospheric pollution should, they argue, have produced a different urban-rural pattern than observed. The dose relationship and the size of the difference carry the argument: "smoking is a factor, and an important factor, in the production of carcinoma of the lung" (as quoted in later literature). As epidemiologists they stop where the data stop — association demonstrated, mechanism (the carcinogens in tar, benzpyrene among them) left to the laboratory, and confirmation assigned to a prospective study of doctors, which Hill and Doll launched the following year and which reported for fifty years.

5. Aftermath

The paper, published alongside Wynder and Graham's American study, converted a suspicion into the central fact of twentieth-century public health. The British Doctors Study (1951–2001) turned the retrospective association into prospective certainty, showing smokers of 25+ cigarettes a day dying of lung cancer at rates more than twenty times non-smokers, and that stopping smoking, even in middle age, recovers much of the risk. Doll and Hill's 1950 report remains a founding document of modern epidemiology — and of the case-control method itself.
