Independent Functions of Viral Protein and Nucleic Acid in Growth of Bacteriophage

Alfred D. Hershey and Martha Chase

Department of Genetics, Carnegie Institution of Washington, Cold Spring Harbor, Long Island, New York

The Journal of General Physiology, Vol. 36, No. 1, pp. 39–56 (1952) · DOI: 10.1085/jgp.36.1.39

Abstract

Bacteriophage T2 was prepared labelled either in its protein, with radioactive sulphur (35S), or in its nucleic acid, with radioactive phosphorus (32P). On infection of sensitive bacteria, the two labels behave quite differently. Most of the sulphur can be stripped from the infected cells by agitation in a Waring blendor, without affecting the cells' power to yield phage; most of the phosphorus, once adsorbed, cannot be removed and is required for phage growth. The phosphorus label is transferred in appreciable amount (about 30 per cent) to the phage progeny, whereas essentially no sulphur (less than 1 per cent) passes to progeny. The viral protein therefore functions as a protective coat and apparatus of adsorption and injection, while the nucleic acid, which alone enters the cell, carries the information for the production of new virus.

1. Introduction

When a bacterial virus multiplies, the infected cell yields, after a latent period of some twenty minutes, about a hundred new virus particles. The chemistry of this process is obscure. The mature T2 particle is roughly half protein and half deoxyribonucleic acid by weight, and it is natural to ask which of these components directs the intracellular synthesis, and whether the particle that enters the cell is the same as the particle that effects infection.

Earlier work has shown that the empty protein membranes ("ghosts") left after osmotic shock of phage retain the power of adsorbing to bacteria but can neither kill the cell nor initiate growth. The experiments reported here follow the separate fates of the viral protein and the viral nucleic acid during infection, using radioactive tracers that distinguish them cleanly: sulphur occurs in the protein (in cystine and methionine) but not in the nucleic acid; phosphorus occurs in the nucleic acid but not appreciably in the protein. Phage so labelled retains full infectivity.

2. Materials and Methods

Preparation of labelled phage

Escherichia coli strain B was grown in a simple synthetic medium in which either the sulphate or the phosphate was provided in radioactive form. Phage T2 was added at low multiplicity, and the lysates were purified by differential centrifugation. The resulting stocks contained 35S or 32P at high specific activity, with less than a few per cent cross-contamination.

Adsorption and shearing

Labelled phage and bacteria were mixed in broth, allowed a few minutes for adsorption, and then chilled. Portions of the infected suspension were agitated for graded times in a Waring blendor, which shears the emptying tails from the cell surface, or were subjected to controlled osmotic shock. Bacteria were then separated from unadsorbed material by low-speed centrifugation and the radioactivity of pellet and supernatant was counted.

Assay of progeny

After blending and washing, the infected cells were resuspended in fresh medium, incubated until lysis, and the progeny phage was purified and counted for radioactivity alongside an infectivity assay.

3. Results

The fate of the sulphur label

When 35S-labelled phage infects bacteria, about three quarters of the radioactivity can be removed from the cells by agitation in the blendor, and the removed material appears in the supernatant as protein debris. Removal does not impair the infected cells: blended and unblended cultures lyse at the same time and liberate the same yield of progeny. The protein of the infecting particle therefore plays no part inside the cell; its work is finished once adsorption and injection have occurred at the surface.

The fate of the phosphorus label

The 32P behaves oppositely. Once adsorption is complete, blending removes very little of it — at most a small fraction attributable to unadsorbed particles — and the great majority stays with the bacterial pellet. If infection is interrupted, the amount of phosphorus retained parallels the number of productively infected cells.

Transfer to progeny

The decisive test is the composition of the progeny. Phage harvested from cells infected with 32P-labelled parents carries about 30 per cent of the parental phosphorus; the remainder is dispersed into the medium and the bacterial debris in degraded form. Progeny of cells infected with 35S-labelled parents carry less than 1 per cent of the parental sulphur — an amount consistent with trivial contamination.

Osmotic shock

Particles emptied of their nucleic acid by osmotic shock adsorb normally but are inert; conversely, free nucleic acid preparations show no infectivity. Attachment and injection are thus protein functions; the programme of growth is carried by the acid alone.

4. Discussion

The two chemical components of the virus have separate and complementary roles. The protein acts as a protective coat for the nucleic acid in transit, and as a specific instrument for adsorbing to the host cell and injecting the acid through the cell wall. The nucleic acid, once inside, somehow directs the cell to make complete new virus — protein included. The chemical identification of the material transferred to progeny, and of the material that exercises genetic continuity, is the same: the nucleic acid. These findings are consistent with the view, argued by Avery and his colleagues from experiments with pneumococcal transforming principle, that nucleic acid is the substance of heredity, and they disagree with any theory that assigns the genetic role to the protein.

It remains to learn by what steps the injected acid multiplies and how it specifies the synthesis of its own coat. Those are problems for the future.
