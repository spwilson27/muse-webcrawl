Nature 409, 860–921 (15 February 2001)

Initial Sequencing and Analysis of the Human Genome

International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium

Twenty sequencing centres in six countries, including the Whitehead Institute/MIT Center for Genome Research (Cambridge, USA), the Sanger Centre (Hinxton, UK), the Washington University School of Medicine Genome Sequencing Center (St Louis, USA), the US DOE Joint Genome Institute (Walnut Creek, USA), the Baylor College of Medicine Human Genome Sequencing Center (Houston, USA) and the RIKEN Genomic Sciences Center (Yokohama, Japan)

doi:10.1038/35057062

Abstract

The human genome holds the inherited instructions that, together with the environment, shape every human being. We report here a draft sequence of the human genome: 2.69 thousand million bases of the approximately 2.9 thousand million bases of euchromatic (gene-rich) DNA, assembled by a hierarchical shotgun strategy from bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clones mapped to each chromosome. The draft sequence is annotated and analysed. The genome is dominated by repetitive sequence derived from transposons, which constitute at least 45 per cent of it; protein-coding genes number only on the order of 30,000–35,000, far fewer than earlier estimates, and occupy about 1.5 per cent of the sequence. We describe the landscape of the genome — its base composition, repeat content, segmental duplications and gene distribution — a catalogue of 1.42 million single nucleotide polymorphisms, and what the sequence already reveals about human biology and evolution. The sequence has been deposited in public databases, freely available to all.

Background and strategy

The Human Genome Project was launched in 1990 with the goal of determining the complete sequence of the human genome by 2005. Two developments made this feasible: automated fluorescence-based sequencing on capillary instruments, and the clone-by-clone strategy adopted here. Rather than sequencing sheared whole-genome DNA directly, the consortium first built maps of large-insert BAC clones across every chromosome — by restriction-enzyme fingerprinting, sequence-tagged-site content and end sequencing — selected a tiling path of some 30,000 clones, and sequenced each clone by shotgun sampling of its subclones, assembling the reads locally before merging the finished clones into the chromosomes. The strategy sacrifices raw speed for accuracy and for the ability to resolve the repeats and recent duplications in which the human genome abounds, and it allowed hundreds of laboratories to contribute finished regions even before the draft was complete.

The draft announced here covers about 90 per cent of the euchromatic genome. The remaining euchromatin, the heterochromatic regions (centromeres, and the short arms of the acrocentric chromosomes), and several hundred gaps are the targets of the finishing phase. Even in draft form, however, the sequence is sufficiently complete and accurate — with fewer than 1 error per 10,000 bases in finished regions — to support the analyses that follow.

The landscape of the genome

Base composition and CpG islands

The overall G+C content of the genome is 41 per cent, but it varies on every scale: between chromosomes (38–49 per cent), between the classical isochores (GC-poor L bands of about 39 per cent and GC-rich H bands reaching 49 per cent and above), and locally over scales of kilobases. Dinucleotide CpG is depleted to about one-fifth of its expected frequency over most of the genome, because methylated cytosine in CpG mutates readily to thymine; but some 28,890 CpG islands — short unmethylated stretches of high CpG density — survive, and they mark the 5′ ends of a large fraction of genes.

Repetitive sequence: the transposon fossil record

At least 45 per cent of the genome is recognisably derived from transposable elements, and the true fraction is probably higher, many ancient copies having diverged beyond recognition. The interspersed repeat classes are summarised in Table 1. Long interspersed elements (LINEs), chiefly LINE1, autonomous retrotransposons carrying their own reverse transcriptase, account for about one-fifth of the genome, in roughly 850,000 copies, mostly truncated on insertion. Short interspersed elements (SINEs) are dominated by the Alu family: some 1.1 million copies of a roughly 280-base element, about 10.7 per cent of the genome, still actively retroposing in modern humans. Retrovirus-like LTR elements contribute about 8 per cent, and DNA-transposon fossils about 3 per cent. The repeat landscape is not uniform: Alu density is highest in GC-rich, gene-rich regions, whereas LINE1 predominates in AT-rich regions — a distribution that suggests these elements have been retained, or have accumulated, where they do least harm. Comparison of repeat divergence with species distributions shows that transposon activity in the human lineage has declined over the past 40 million years, in contrast to the mouse lineage, a difference that may bear on the differing genome sizes of the two species.

Segmental duplications

About 5 per cent of the genome lies in large segmental duplications — blocks of 1–200 kilobases duplicated, with 90–99.5 per cent identity, within or between chromosomes. Recent duplications (above 98 per cent identity) comprise about 3.5 per cent of the sequence and are concentrated near centromeres and telomeres and at a number of hotspots implicated in genomic disorders. These duplications were the chief technical obstacle to assembly and are a continuing source of structural variation and of raw material for new genes.

Genes and the proteome

Combining computational prediction (the Ensembl and Genie pipelines), alignment of known mRNAs and proteins, and manual curation in sampled regions, we arrive at an estimate of 30,000–35,000 protein-coding genes — only about twice the number in the fly or the worm, and far below the 100,000 or more widely expected a decade ago. The typical human gene spans some tens of kilobases with about nine exons; coding sequence occupies only about 1.5 per cent of the genome, although genes including their introns span roughly a quarter of it. Alternative splicing is pervasive — a large fraction of genes produce more than one transcript — so the proteome is much larger and more complex than the gene count alone suggests; complexity seems to have evolved less by adding genes than by recombining domains and varying their expression.

The predicted proteome is dominated by proteins of unknown function: more than 40 per cent of predicted genes have no characterised homologue or assigned function, an agenda for a generation of experiments. Among those that can be classified, the largest single family is the zinc-finger transcription factors; the olfactory receptors form the largest family of G-protein-coupled receptors, with about 1,000 genes and pseudogenes, most of them inactivated in humans relative to the mouse. We also note 223 predicted proteins with their strongest resemblance to bacterial proteins and little similarity to other eukaryotes, candidates for horizontal gene transfer from bacteria into an early vertebrate ancestor. Vertebrate-specific innovations include the proteins of the adaptive immune system, haemostasis, and the complex machinery of the developing nervous system, built largely by duplication and shuffling of older domains. Hundreds of non-coding RNA genes are also identified, including about 500 transfer RNA genes and the small nuclear and nucleolar RNAs, with many more expected.

Variation: a map of 1.42 million SNPs

By comparing the overlapping sequences of clones from different donors and by dedicated re-sequencing, The SNP Consortium and the public project together identified 1.42 million single nucleotide polymorphisms, distributed through the genome at an average density of about one per 1,900 bases, and deposited them in the public dbSNP database. Two random human chromosomes differ at about 1 base in 1,250, on average, with the density of variation suppressed in coding regions by selection. This map converts the genome from a single reference into the beginning of a catalogue of human diversity, with applications from disease-gene mapping to pharmacogenetics and human history.

Conclusions and outlook

The draft sequence is a beginning, not an end. Finishing the remaining euchromatin to high accuracy, closing the gaps, and completing the annotation will occupy the next several years; beyond lie the systematic tasks of functional genomics: cataloguing all transcripts and regulatory elements, mapping variation in populations, and understanding how the sequence directs the development and physiology of a human being. Two decisions of principle should be recorded. First, the consortium chose a strategy whose intermediate products — mapped clones, and sequence as it was produced — were useful to the whole community throughout, and released all sequence data publicly within 24 hours of production, in accordance with the Bermuda principles. Second, the genome belongs to everyone: it is presented here as a shared inheritance, free for use by scientists in academia and industry alike. The sequence of our genome will be a resource for all of biology and medicine for as long as those sciences are practised.
