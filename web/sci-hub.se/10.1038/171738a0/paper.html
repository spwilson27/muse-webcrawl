MOLECULAR STRUCTURE OF NUCLEIC ACIDS

Molecular Structure of Deoxypentose Nucleic Acids

M. H. F. Wilkins, A. R. Stokes and H. R. Wilson
Medical Research Council Biophysics Research Unit, King's College, London
DOI: 10.1038/171738a0

Until now1 most of the X-ray evidence for deoxypentose nucleic acid has concerned the crystalline or semi-crystalline material obtained from fibres pulled from viscous solutions of the sodium salt of thymus nucleic acid. The interpretation of the diffraction patterns of material in this state has been hampered by uncertainty as to the perfection of the material. We have recently succeeded in obtaining much better X-ray diffraction diagrams, from specimens of deoxypentose nucleic acid extracted from a variety of sources — including calf thymus, herring sperm, trout sperm and human tissue — and also from living sperm heads, in which the material is present in the native state. In all cases the diffraction pattern is essentially the same. Since the proportions of the nitrogenous bases differ markedly between deoxyribose nucleic acids from different sources, we may conclude that the general configuration of the molecule — more precisely, of the polynucleotide chains — is independent of the sequence of the bases along the chains. The common structural features must therefore belong to the sugar–phosphate backbone.

The helical character of the patterns

The X-ray diagrams show a set of layer lines corresponding to a repeat of about 34 Å along the fibre axis, with a strong meridional reflexion at 3.4 Å. The distribution of intensity has the characteristic form, first analysed in this laboratory and in the accompanying theoretical work of Cochran, Crick and Vand2, that is produced by a helical structure: the reflexions lie along arms of a cross, and reflexions on the meridian are absent except at orders corresponding to the residue repeat, in accordance with the selection rules of a continuous helix. The theory of diffraction by helices permits the main parameters to be read from the pattern: the pitch of the helix is 34 Å, there are ten residues in each turn, and the radius at which the phosphorus atoms lie is about 10 Å, so that the molecule has a diameter of about 20 Å.

Comparison with the proposed structure

The structure proposed by Watson and Crick in the preceding communication consists of two intertwined helical polynucleotide chains with the phosphates on the outside and the bases on the inside, the chains being joined by hydrogen bonds between the bases. Calculating the diffraction to be expected from such an arrangement, we find that the main features of our diagrams — the layer-line spacing, the cross, the position and relative intensity of the principal reflexions, and the systematic absences — are accounted for by a structure of this general type with the parameters given above. In particular, the density of the material and the water content of the fibres are consistent with two chains per repeat unit and incompatible with three intertwined chains with phosphate groups close to the axis, as in the model of Pauling and Corey, in which the phosphates would lie at a radius of about 4 Å and the bases on the outside. A structure with the bases on the outside also fails to give the observed distribution of intensity.

Our evidence is not yet sufficiently detailed to determine the structure uniquely; in particular, the precise arrangement of the bases and the locus of the second chain relative to the first must be fixed by more refined intensity measurements, which are in progress. But the evidence does show that the molecule of deoxypentose nucleic acid, from whatever source obtained, is a helix of diameter about 20 Å and pitch 34 Å with the phosphate groups at the periphery, and that the structure of Watson and Crick, of two such helices coaxially intertwined, is consistent with all the X-ray data at present available.
