Nature 391, 806–811 (19 February 1998)

Potent and Specific Genetic Interference by Double-Stranded RNA in Caenorhabditis elegans

Andrew Fire, SiQun Xu, Mary K. Montgomery, Steven A. Kostas, Samuel E. Driver & Craig C. Mello

Department of Embryology, Carnegie Institution of Washington, Baltimore, Maryland 21210, USA; and Program in Molecular Medicine and Department of Cell Biology, University of Massachusetts Medical Center, Worcester, Massachusetts 01605, USA

doi:10.1038/35888

Abstract

We report that double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) is a potent and specific agent of genetic interference in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans. Injection of dsRNA corresponding to a target gene into adult animals produces a phenotype closely resembling a null mutation in that gene, both in the injected animals' progeny and (for several genes) in the injected animals themselves. dsRNA is effective at quantities several orders of magnitude below those required for antisense RNA alone, and far more effective than either sense or antisense strand separately: a few molecules of dsRNA per cell appear sufficient. Interference is sequence-specific, acts post-transcriptionally (the targeted messenger RNA disappears from the embryo), spreads from the site of injection to distant tissues, and can be inherited by the next generation. We suggest that dsRNA-directed interference may reflect a natural cellular defence and regulatory mechanism, and that it can be exploited as a tool for probing gene function throughout the genome.

Background: the antisense paradox

Injecting antisense RNA into C. elegans was introduced as a way to block a gene of interest, and it often works. But the controls in such experiments behaved oddly. In the experiments of Guo and Kemphues on the par-1 gene, sense RNA — the strand with the same sequence as the messenger RNA, which on any simple annealing model should be inert — produced interference as strong as the antisense strand. Similar anomalies have accumulated elsewhere, and analogous puzzles attend the phenomena of co-suppression in plants and quelling in fungi, where introducing extra copies of a gene silences the gene itself. Our working suspicion was that the active agent in standard RNA preparations might not be the single strand at all: in vitro transcription reactions produce, besides the intended strand, a variable contamination of aberrant products, including molecules in which sense and antisense sequences are joined. If a small amount of double-stranded RNA were the true interferer, the paradoxes would be explained: both sense and antisense preparations can carry dsRNA contamination, and purified single strands should be weak.

Results

Double-stranded RNA, not either strand alone, is the potent interferer

We purified sense RNA, antisense RNA and annealed double-stranded RNA corresponding to segments of several genes whose loss gives recognisable phenotypes: unc-22 (a muscle structural gene; loss gives an easily scored twitching phenotype), unc-54 (muscle myosin heavy chain; loss gives paralysis), fem-1 (sex determination), and reporter transgenes encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP) and lacZ. Preparations were injected into the gonad or body cavity of adult hermaphrodites and interference scored in the progeny.

For every target, annealed dsRNA was dramatically more effective than the purified single strands (Table 1). A segment of unc-22 dsRNA produced the twitcher phenotype in essentially all progeny at doses where sense or antisense alone showed little or no activity; similar results held for unc-54, fem-1 and the GFP reporter, where fluorescence was extinguished in embryos and larvae. Crucially, simultaneous injection of separately purified sense and antisense strands was also effective, and re-annealing was not required beforehand — what matters is that both strands be present. Single-stranded preparations regained activity precisely to the extent that they contained dsRNA contaminants.

Potency, spread and inheritance

Three features of the effect are remarkable. First, potency: interfering activity is detectable at dilutions corresponding to only a few molecules of dsRNA per cell, several orders of magnitude below stoichiometric estimates for a mechanism in which the RNA pairs with and blocks each messenger. Some amplification or catalytic step must intervene between the injected dsRNA and the destruction of the target mRNA. Second, spread: RNA injected into one part of the animal interferes in distant tissues, and progeny of injected animals show interference in tissues (such as body-wall muscle) far removed from the gonad into which the injection was made; the interfering signal is mobile within the animal. Third, inheritance: for several targets, interference persists into the generation following the injected one, although it fades thereafter, resembling an epigenetic rather than a mutational change.

The mechanism is post-transcriptional and destroys the messenger RNA

In situ hybridisation to embryos shows that the endogenous messenger RNA for the targeted gene (for example mex-3) is drastically reduced in animals exposed to the corresponding dsRNA, while nuclear transcripts and unrelated mRNAs are spared (Fig. 1). Interference therefore acts after transcription, by eliminating the mature message. It is also exquisitely sequence-specific: dsRNA from one member of a gene family does not interfere with its relatives unless the shared segment is included in the duplex, and segments directed at introns or at sequences outside the mature message are ineffective, consistent with action on the processed cytoplasmic mRNA.

Discussion

The observations have two implications. As a method, dsRNA interference — RNAi, as it is coming to be called — gives the experimenter a way to produce the equivalent of a loss-of-function phenotype for any gene of known sequence, in an otherwise wild-type animal, at any stage, and in the progeny of a single injected mother. Because the worm's genome sequence is now essentially complete, this makes systematic functional screening of whole chromosomes a practical prospect, and the principles should extend to other organisms, given a route for delivering dsRNA.

As biology, the existence of so potent and specific a response suggests that cells possess machinery for recognising double-stranded RNA and using its sequence to destroy matching messages. Such machinery makes sense as a defence: double-stranded RNA is a hallmark of many viral infections and of transposon activity, and a system that silences any gene whose sequence appears in dsRNA form would suppress both. The parallels with co-suppression in plants, quelling in Neurospora, and the silencing of transgenes in many organisms suggest an ancient and conserved pathway, whose components — presumably including an activity that cleaves the target message guided by the duplex — now become objects of biochemical and genetic search. How a few molecules of input duplex can eliminate an abundant message, how the signal spreads between cells, and why it can persist into the next generation are the outstanding questions.

Methods (summary)

RNA was transcribed in vitro from cloned cDNA or genomic templates with T3 and T7 RNA polymerases, purified by gel electrophoresis or phenol extraction as specified, and annealed by heating and slow cooling where double-stranded material was required. Injections were performed into the syncytial gonad or the intestinal lumen of young adult hermaphrodites under standard microinjection conditions. Interference was scored by phenotype in the F1 (and, where noted, F2) generations, by direct inspection of reporter fluorescence, and by in situ hybridisation and Northern analysis of target mRNA abundance.
