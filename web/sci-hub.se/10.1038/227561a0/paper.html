Nature 227, 561–563 (8 August 1970)

Central Dogma of Molecular Biology

Francis Crick

Medical Research Council, Laboratory of Molecular Biology, Hills Road, Cambridge, England

doi:10.1038/227561a0

Abstract

The central dogma, as originally formulated in 1958, is restated and clarified in the light of recent discoveries, in particular the synthesis of DNA on an RNA template by RNA tumour viruses. Information here means the precise determination of sequence, either of bases in a nucleic acid or of amino-acid residues in a protein. The transfers of information that occur in all cells (DNA to DNA, DNA to RNA, RNA to protein) are distinguished from special transfers (RNA to RNA, RNA to DNA, DNA to protein) and from three transfers for which no evidence exists and which are held to be impossible (protein to protein, protein to DNA, protein to RNA). The essence of the dogma is a single prohibition: once information has passed into protein it cannot get out again.

Why a restatement is needed

The central dogma was put forward in 1958, at a symposium of the Society for Experimental Biology, to give molecular biologists a clear rule about the permitted directions of flow of sequence information. It has since been widely misunderstood. A persistent misreading takes the dogma as a statement about the whole machinery of the cell, or confuses it with the simpler (and quite different) slogan “DNA makes RNA makes protein”. The recent discovery, by Temin and Mizutani and by Baltimore, of enzymes in RNA tumour viruses that synthesise DNA using an RNA template has been greeted in some quarters as the overthrow of the dogma. It is nothing of the kind, and the misunderstanding shows that a careful restatement is overdue.

The classification of information transfers

By information is meant the precise determination of sequence: the order of bases in a nucleic acid, or of amino-acid residues in a polypeptide chain. A transfer is any process in which the sequence of one polymer is determined by the sequence of another. There are nine conceivable transfers between the three classes of polymer. They fall into three groups.

Class of transferTransfers
General transfers (occur in all cells)DNA → DNA; DNA → RNA; RNA → protein
Special transfers (occur only in special circumstances)RNA → RNA; RNA → DNA; DNA → protein
Unknown transfers (no evidence; postulated not to occur)protein → protein; protein → DNA; protein → RNA

Table. The nine possible transfers of sequence information and the class to which each belongs.

The general transfers are the familiar ones: replication of DNA, transcription into RNA, and translation of RNA into protein. They are believed to occur in all cells without exception. Whether there are any other general transfers, anywhere in nature, is doubtful; in particular the transfer RNA → RNA seen in RNA viruses is confined to special systems.

The special transfers are those known, or reasonably expected, to occur only in special circumstances. RNA → RNA transfer is the replication of RNA virus genomes. RNA → DNA transfer is precisely what has now been demonstrated in the RNA tumour viruses; it had always been regarded as a theoretical possibility, since there is no chemical reason why sequence information should not be copied from one kind of nucleic acid to the other. DNA → protein transfer — direct translation of DNA without an RNA intermediate — has been reported in cell-free systems under artificial conditions and is included among the special transfers for completeness.

What the dogma asserts

The central dogma deals only with the three unknown transfers. It states:

“Once 'information' has passed into protein it cannot get out again.”

In more detail: the transfer of information from nucleic acid to nucleic acid, or from nucleic acid to protein, may be possible, but transfer from protein to protein, or from protein to nucleic acid, is impossible. Information here means the precise determination of sequence, either of bases in the nucleic acid or of amino-acid residues in the protein.

The reason for believing this is mechanistic as well as empirical. The apparatus of translation reads a nucleic acid sequence, three bases at a time, and builds the corresponding amino-acid sequence. No machinery is known — and it is difficult to imagine one — that could do the reverse: read off an amino-acid sequence and from it dictate, unambiguously, a base sequence. The genetic code is degenerate, so a protein sequence does not even contain the information needed to specify a unique nucleic acid sequence. A reverse translation is thus not merely undiscovered; the information it would need has already been discarded on the way in.

Conclusion

The discovery of RNA-dependent DNA synthesis in tumour viruses enlarges the list of special transfers; it does not touch the dogma, which was never a claim that information flows only from DNA to RNA to protein. What the dogma forbids is any flow of sequence information out of protein. That prohibition still stands, and no known experiment contradicts it. A statement that has survived a dozen years of molecular biology without a single exception may reasonably continue to guide experiment: it tells the investigator which kinds of mechanism are worth looking for, and which are not.
