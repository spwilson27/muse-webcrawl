Nature 249, 810–812 (28 June 1974)

Stratospheric Sink for Chlorofluoromethanes: Chlorine Atom-Catalysed Destruction of Ozone

Mario J. Molina & F. S. Rowland

Department of Chemistry, University of California, Irvine, California 92664

doi:10.1038/249810a0

Abstract

Chlorofluoromethanes — principally CFCl3 and CF2Cl2, used in large and growing quantities as refrigerants and aerosol propellants — are chemically inert in the lower atmosphere and have no known natural sink there. They therefore accumulate and are transported intact to the stratosphere, where ultraviolet photolysis releases chlorine atoms. Each chlorine atom destroys ozone catalytically, in a chain of the order of one hundred thousand ozone molecules before removal. Continued release at present rates leads, in the steady state, to a reduction of the ozone column of the order of ten per cent, with the consequent increase in ultraviolet radiation at the surface of the Earth.

The problem: inert gases must go somewhere

The chlorofluoromethanes are entirely man-made. Two compounds dominate production, CFCl3 (fluorocarbon-11) and CF2Cl2 (fluorocarbon-12), with world production of each of the order of several hundred thousand tonnes per year and rising steeply. They were adopted precisely because they are non-toxic, non-flammable and extraordinarily unreactive: they do not burn, hydrolyse, or react with the common reagents of the troposphere.

Lovelock's electron-capture measurements have shown that CFCl3 released at the surface is present throughout the troposphere of both hemispheres, in amounts consistent with most of the material ever manufactured still being in the air. No natural removal process of any significance is known: the compounds are too insoluble for efficient rain-out, are not attacked by hydroxyl radicals, and do not absorb sunlight at wavelengths reaching the lower atmosphere. The expected atmospheric lifetimes are measured in decades to a century or more (Table 1). Essentially every molecule released will eventually be carried upward into the stratosphere. The question is what happens to it there.

Stratospheric photolysis

The chlorofluoromethanes absorb ultraviolet light in a continuous band below about 230 nm. Such wavelengths are screened out below about 30 km by molecular oxygen and ozone itself, but in the middle and upper stratosphere the solar flux there is strong. Photolysis cleaves a carbon–chlorine bond:

CFCl3 + hν → CFCl2 + Cl

CF2Cl2 + hν → CF2Cl + Cl

Further oxidation of the fragments releases the remaining chlorine as atoms or in inorganic form. The stratosphere thus receives a growing burden of reactive chlorine far exceeding the natural input from methyl chloride, the only significant natural chlorine carrier. It is the chemistry of this chlorine with ozone that concerns us.

The chlorine catalytic cycle

Atomic chlorine attacks ozone rapidly, and the resulting chlorine monoxide is reconverted to atomic chlorine by reaction with atomic oxygen, which is abundant in the upper stratosphere from the photolysis of ozone itself:

Cl + O3 → ClO + O2

ClO + O → Cl + O2

net:   O + O3 → 2 O2

The chlorine atom is regenerated, so the pair of reactions forms a catalytic chain: one Cl atom can destroy ozone molecules again and again. The chain is broken only when chlorine is sequestered in an unreactive reservoir, principally hydrogen chloride, formed by reaction with methane,

Cl + CH4 → HCl + CH3,

or chlorine nitrate, ClONO2, formed from ClO and NO2. Even these reservoirs are not permanent: HCl is slowly reconverted by hydroxyl radicals, and ClONO2 is photolysed. Taking the known rate coefficients and stratospheric abundances, the average chain length — the number of ozone molecules destroyed by one chlorine atom during its stratospheric residence — is of the order of 105.

Estimated effect on the ozone column

Combining the release rates implied by industrial production figures with the photochemical model, we can estimate the steady-state burden of stratospheric chlorine if production were frozen at 1972 levels, and the resulting depletion of ozone. The chlorine loading rises for decades, because of the long tropospheric lifetimes, and asymptotes only when destruction balances release. The corresponding reduction in the total ozone column is of the order of 5–15 per cent at steady state, with the larger losses at high altitude, where the ozone concentration is most sensitive to the catalytic cycle. If production continues to grow at recent rates of about 10 per cent per year, the depletion is correspondingly larger, and because of the long atmospheric lifetimes the effect is, on any human timescale, irreversible once it has occurred: stopping release does not restore ozone for many decades.

A reduction of the ozone column of this size implies an increase in the biologically damaging ultraviolet flux (290–320 nm) reaching the surface of roughly twice the percentage decrease in ozone — a change with consequences for skin cancer incidence and for ecosystems that are difficult to quantify but impossible to dismiss.

Conclusions

The very inertness that made the chlorofluoromethanes attractive guarantees their accumulation and eventual delivery to the one environment where they are dangerous. No competing sink of consequence has been identified, and every assumption in the calculation — of lifetime, of chlorine chain length, of production growth — would have to be wrong together to remove the problem. Measurements of the stratospheric abundances of the chlorofluoromethanes themselves, of ClO, and of the ozone trend are urgently needed. In the meantime, the assumption that a substance harmless at the ground is harmless to the atmosphere should no longer be made on the basis of chemical intuition alone.
