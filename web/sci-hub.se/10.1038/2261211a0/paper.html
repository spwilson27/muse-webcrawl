Nature 226, 1211–1213 (27 June 1970)

Viral RNA-Dependent DNA Polymerase: RNA-Dependent DNA Polymerase in Virions of RNA Tumour Viruses

David Baltimore

The Rockefeller University, New York, New York 10021

doi:10.1038/2261211a0

Abstract

Virions of Rauscher murine leukaemia virus contain an enzyme system which, when the particles are disrupted with non-ionic detergent, synthesises DNA. The reaction requires all four deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates and is abolished by pretreatment of the disrupted virions with ribonuclease, showing that the DNA is copied from an RNA template — the 70S RNA of the virus. The product is DNA, at first associated with the template in an RNA–DNA hybrid, and it hybridises specifically with viral RNA. An RNA-dependent DNA polymerase of this kind provides the biochemical mechanism demanded by the provirus hypothesis of Temin: a DNA copy of the viral RNA which can be integrated into the DNA of the host cell.

Introduction

The RNA tumour viruses reproduce in a manner unlike that of any other RNA virus. Infection of a susceptible cell leads, after a short interval, to a stably transformed cell which produces virus indefinitely and passes the capacity to do so to its daughter cells, although viral RNA is itself turned over. To account for this heritable persistence, Temin proposed the provirus hypothesis: the RNA of the infecting virus is copied into DNA, and this DNA, integrated into the chromosomes of the cell, serves as the template both for viral RNA synthesis and for the viral genes responsible for transformation. The hypothesis requires an enzyme with an unprecedented activity — a DNA polymerase directed by an RNA template.

In the accompanying communication, Temin and Mizutani report that particles of Rous sarcoma virus contain a DNA polymerase whose activity depends on the viral RNA. Here I describe experiments with Rauscher murine leukaemia virus (R-MLV), a virus which replicates in cultured mouse cells and causes leukaemia in mice. Disrupted virions of this virus carry out an extensive synthesis of DNA in vitro. The properties of the reaction show that the DNA product is transcribed from the RNA of the virion, and that the responsible enzyme is a true RNA-dependent DNA polymerase packaged within the virus particle.

Methods

Virus. R-MLV was collected from the medium of a continuously producing mouse cell culture, concentrated by centrifugation, and purified by banding in sucrose density gradients. The purified virus was suspended in buffer and used fresh, or stored frozen for short periods.

Assay of DNA synthesis. Virus was incubated at 37°C with the four deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates, one of them labelled (usually 3H-thymidine triphosphate), in the presence of Mg2+ and the non-ionic detergent Nonidet P-40 to disrupt the virion envelope. Incorporation of radioactivity into acid-insoluble material was followed by precipitation with trichloroacetic acid and collection on filters. Product was characterised by its sensitivity to deoxyribonuclease and alkali, by equilibrium banding in caesium chloride gradients, and by hybridisation with RNA extracted from purified virions.

Results

Requirements of the reaction

Intact virions show little activity; disruption of the envelope with detergent uncovers a vigorous synthesis of acid-insoluble product. The reaction proceeds nearly linearly for the first hour and continues for several hours. Its requirements, summarised in Table 1, identify the product as DNA made on an RNA template. Omission of any one of the four deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates greatly reduces incorporation, as expected for the synthesis of a long, base-heterogeneous DNA. Neither ribonucleoside triphosphates nor their omission affects the reaction.

The decisive observation is the effect of ribonuclease. If the disrupted virus is incubated with ribonuclease before the substrates are added, essentially all polymerase activity is lost. Ribonuclease added after synthesis has begun stops further incorporation. The template is therefore RNA, and the enzyme cannot use the product DNA alone as template under these conditions. Conversely, the product behaves chemically as DNA: it is resistant to alkali and to ribonuclease, but is rendered acid-soluble by deoxyribonuclease.

Nature of the product

Early in the reaction much of the labelled DNA is found associated with RNA. On caesium chloride gradients the initial product bands at densities between those of RNA and DNA and shifts to the position of free DNA after treatment with ribonuclease or alkali, showing that the new DNA is made as an RNA–DNA hybrid on the template strand and is later found as double-stranded DNA. The DNA product is heterogeneous in size, including molecules large enough to represent a copy of a substantial fraction of the viral genome.

The product is a copy of viral RNA

DNA made in the reaction hybridises efficiently and specifically with 70S RNA extracted from R-MLV virions: at saturation a large fraction of the product can be driven into hybrid by viral RNA. It does not hybridise appreciably with ribosomal RNA or with RNA of unrelated origin. The polymerase therefore copies the virus's own RNA, not a contaminating cellular template carried down in the preparation.

Occurrence of the activity

RNA-dependent DNA polymerase activity is not peculiar to Rauscher virus. Disrupted virions of Rous sarcoma virus show the same ribonuclease-sensitive DNA synthesis (as Temin and Mizutani also report), and activity is found in other RNA tumour viruses examined. Uninfected cells grown and processed in parallel show no comparable activity in the corresponding fractions, and the activity co-purifies with virus particles through gradient banding. The enzyme appears to be a normal constituent of the virion, present in every mature particle.

Discussion

The virion of an RNA tumour virus thus contains an enzyme which does what the provirus hypothesis requires: it transcribes the viral RNA into DNA. The natural course of infection can now be pictured concretely. After entry and uncoating, the polymerase, carried in with the particle, makes a DNA copy of the genome; this copy, in double-stranded form, is integrated into the host chromosome, where it persists as a provirus and directs the synthesis of viral RNA and proteins.

This flow of information, from RNA to DNA, is the reverse of the direction usually considered in the sequence DNA → RNA → protein. It does not violate any chemical necessity; it simply reveals that the transfer of sequence information between nucleic acids can proceed in both directions, and that cells and viruses may exploit either. Retrograde transfer also suggests an explanation for the DNA copies of cellular sequences and offers a new enzymatic tool: a polymerase that will copy any RNA into DNA will be of the greatest use in studying the sequences of RNA molecules themselves.

Many questions remain. The mechanism by which synthesis is initiated — whether a primer is required and what it is — is unknown. The form in which the DNA enters the chromosome, and the relation of the reaction described here to transformation as opposed to virus production, are problems now open to biochemical attack. But the existence of the enzyme is established, and with it the central step of the provirus hypothesis.
