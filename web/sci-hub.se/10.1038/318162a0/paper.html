Nature 318, 162–163 (14 November 1985)

C60: Buckminsterfullerene

H. W. Kroto, J. R. Heath, S. C. O'Brien, R. F. Curl & R. E. Smalley

School of Chemistry and Molecular Sciences, University of Sussex, Brighton BN1 9QJ, UK; and Rice Quantum Institute and Departments of Chemistry and Electrical Engineering, Rice University, Houston, Texas 77251, USA

doi:10.1038/318162a0

Abstract

During experiments aimed at understanding the formation of long carbon chains in interstellar space and in the atmospheres of carbon stars, we have produced carbon clusters by laser vaporisation of graphite in a pulsed supersonic beam. Under conditions favouring clustering, the mass spectrum is dominated by a peak at mass 720, corresponding to C60, together with a smaller peak at C70. We propose that C60 owes its special stability to a closed cage structure: a truncated icosahedron, in which sixty carbon atoms occupy equivalent positions at the vertices of a polyhedron of twelve pentagonal and twenty hexagonal faces — the pattern of a football. In honour of R. Buckminster Fuller, whose geodesic domes embody the same geometry, we name the molecule buckminsterfullerene.

Carbon clusters in the beam

Our experiments were designed for another purpose. Radio-astronomers have detected long cyanopolyyne chains in dark interstellar clouds, and the Rice apparatus — laser vaporisation of a solid target inside a pulsed supersonic nozzle, with the products swept into a time-of-flight mass spectrometer — had been built to simulate the chemistry by which such chains might grow in the outflow of a carbon star. Vaporising graphite, we expected to see small carbon clusters and chains. We found those, but when the vaporisation was arranged so that the hot carbon plasma was confined and allowed to cluster extensively before expansion, the mass spectrum changed character: most of the cluster distribution collapsed into a single, strikingly intense peak at C60, with a second, weaker feature at C70. The sixty-atom cluster survives conditions that destroy its neighbours; it resists fragmentation and is reluctant to react. Such prominence in the spectrum is the signature of an unusually stable species.

A closed cage of sixty carbons

What structure can make sixty carbon atoms so much more stable than fifty-nine or sixty-one? The number itself is the clue. Sixty is the number of vertices of a truncated icosahedron — the solid obtained by cutting the corners off an icosahedron, familiar as the pattern of panels on a football: twelve pentagons and twenty hexagons, every vertex equivalent to every other. A carbon atom at each vertex, bonded to its three neighbours, gives a closed, hollow cage in which every atom is in an identical environment, every atom has its valence satisfied within the shell, and there are no dangling bonds or strained reactive sites of the kind that make ordinary carbon clusters so reactive. The structure explains the magic number at a stroke: only for this closed cage do all sixty atoms enjoy the same, fully bonded environment.

The proposal has further attractions. A closed spheroidal shell of sp2 carbon is a natural extension of planar graphite-like bonding into three dimensions, and the π-electron system can delocalise over the whole cage. The companion peak at C70 corresponds to the next closed cage, a slightly elongated spheroid incorporating an extra belt of hexagons, exactly as the fullerene family picture predicts. Smaller even clusters, which cannot close, appear as chains and rings; the special numbers begin where closure becomes possible.

Fig. 2. The proposed structure of C60: a truncated icosahedron. Sixty equivalent carbon atoms lie at the vertices; the bonding pattern places double bonds in the hexagons such that no double bond is required in a pentagon. The diameter of the cage is approximately 7 Å.

The name, and what may follow

The truncated icosahedron is the geometry of the geodesic domes designed by R. Buckminster Fuller — most famously the United States pavilion at Expo 67 in Montreal — and of the modern football. We therefore propose the name buckminsterfullerene for C60, and the generic name fullerenes for the family of closed carbon cages.

If the structure is correct, C60 is not a laboratory curiosity but the first member of a new class of carbon material: a third molecular form of the element alongside diamond and graphite. Closed cages should be capable of encapsulating foreign atoms, of chemical functionalisation on their outer surface, and of existence wherever carbon vapour condenses — in flames, in soot, and in the outflows of carbon stars, where the molecule may already have been made in quantity by nature. Confirming evidence — a single-line 13C NMR spectrum, demanded by the equivalence of all sixty atoms, and ultimately a crystal structure — awaits only a method of preparing the molecule in macroscopic amounts. We suggest that such quantities may be easier to obtain than the exotic conditions of our beam might imply: a carbon arc, or even a sooty flame, may already be making fullerenes unnoticed.
