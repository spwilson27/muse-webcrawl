Nature 378, 355–359 (23 November 1995)

A Jupiter-Mass Companion to a Solar-Type Star

Michel Mayor & Didier Queloz

Observatoire de Genève, CH-1290 Sauverny, Switzerland

doi:10.1038/378355a0

Abstract

The presence of a Jupiter-mass companion to the solar-type star 51 Pegasi is deduced from periodic variations in its radial velocity. The companion moves in a nearly circular orbit of period 4.23 days and semi-major axis 0.05 astronomical units; its minimum mass is about half the mass of Jupiter. No variation in the shapes of the star's spectral lines or in its brightness accompanies the velocity variation, excluding stellar pulsation or surface inhomogeneities as the cause. The companion is therefore, in all probability, a giant planet — the first found around a normal star — although its very small orbital separation presents a severe challenge to theories of planet formation.

The radial-velocity survey

Since September 1994 we have carried out a survey of solar-type stars searching for the reflex motion caused by substellar companions, using the new fibre-fed échelle spectrograph ELODIE on the 1.93-m telescope of the Observatoire de Haute-Provence. By cross-correlating each stellar spectrum with a numerical mask, a mean line profile is obtained whose Doppler shift gives the radial velocity; calibration with a thorium lamp and continuous monitoring of velocity standard stars yields a precision of about 13 m s−1 for bright stars — sufficient to detect the signature of a Jupiter-mass companion in a close orbit, which theory said could not exist, but which nothing forbade us from finding.

Among the first stars of the survey, 51 Pegasi (HR 8729; HD 217014; spectral type G2–3 IV/V; apparent magnitude 5.5; distance about 15 parsecs; somewhat metal-rich and, by its low activity and slow rotation, an old and quiet star) stood out within weeks: its velocity was not constant but varied smoothly and repetitively with a period of little more than four days.

The orbit

The orbit is, within the errors, circular — as expected for so close a companion, since tides raised on the star will circularise the orbit over the star's lifetime. The mass function

f(m) = P K13 (1 − e2)3/2 / (2πG) ≈ 5 × 10−11 M☉

combined with an estimated stellar mass of about 1.05 M☉, yields a minimum mass for the companion (attained if the orbit is viewed edge-on) of

m2 sin i ≈ 0.47 MJ,

where i is the inclination of the orbit to the line of sight. For all but a narrow range of nearly face-on orientations, the true mass is within a factor of a few of this value; the probability that the inclination is small enough to push the companion into the brown-dwarf or stellar range (m2 > 0.08 M☉) is below 2 per cent. The star's own excursion about the common centre of mass is only 55 thousandths of an astronomical unit scaled by the mass ratio — a wobble of about 50 metres per second in velocity.

Excluding a stellar origin

Before claiming a planet, two alternatives must be excluded: that the star itself pulsates, and that dark or bright patches on its rotating surface distort the line profiles periodically. Both possibilities can be tested directly with the same data, because a true Doppler shift moves the whole spectral line without changing its shape, whereas pulsation and spots change the shape asymmetrically. The bisector of the cross-correlation profile — the locus of midpoints of the profile at successive depths — shows no variation at the 4.23-day period, in span or in curvature, to limits well below what the velocity amplitude would require from a spot model. Photometric monitoring (including Hipparcos data) shows no light variation at the period, and the calcium H and K emission, an index of surface activity, displays no modulation. The rotation period implied by the star's activity and its projected rotational velocity (v sin i ≈ 2 km s−1) is of order a month, not four days. We conclude that the velocity variation is a genuine orbital motion of the star.

Discussion: a giant planet where none was expected

The companion to 51 Pegasi is, by any reasonable accounting of the odds, a planet of about the mass of Jupiter orbiting its star at one twentieth of the Earth–Sun distance, with a year of 4.2 days and an equilibrium temperature above 1,200 K. No existing theory of planet formation predicted such an object. A giant planet is supposed to form beyond the snow line, several astronomical units out, where ices multiply the solid material available for a core; at 0.05 AU the disc is far too hot and too poor in solids to build a ten-Earth-mass core, and an atmosphere captured there would be lost. Either giant planets can migrate inwards through the disc after forming at a respectable distance — spiralling in by tidal interaction with the gas, and stopping, for some reason not yet understood, before falling into the star — or our picture of where and how giants form is incomplete.

Whatever the explanation, the detection changes the observational landscape. If close-in giants are common, precision radial-velocity surveys at the 10 m s−1 level will find them by the dozen, and each new system will constrain the theories of formation and migration that 51 Pegasi has so abruptly called into question. The companion is faint and only 0.4 arcseconds from its star at most, far beyond present imaging; but its existence demonstrates that other planetary systems are there to be found by patience and precision, and that they need not resemble our own.
