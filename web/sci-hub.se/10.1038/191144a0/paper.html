Coupling of Phosphorylation to Electron and Hydrogen Transfer by a Chemi-Osmotic Type of Mechanism

Peter Mitchell
Department of Zoology, University of Edinburgh
DOI: 10.1038/191144a0
Abstract. The coupling of phosphorylation to electron and hydrogen transfer in mitochondria and chloroplasts has for twenty years been sought in a soluble chemical intermediate of high group-transfer potential, formed by the respiratory chain and used to drive the synthesis of ATP. No such intermediate has been isolated. This article proposes a mechanism of a fundamentally different, chemi-osmotic, type: the electron-transfer chain is supposed to be organised vectorially across the coupling membrane, so that its operation translocates protons across the membrane and thereby sets up a difference of electrochemical potential — "proticity" — between the two aqueous phases the membrane separates. A proton-translocating ATPase, working in reverse as a synthase, then couples the return flow of protons to the phosphorylation of ADP. Oxidative and photosynthetic phosphorylation, and the active transport coupled to metabolism, are thereby brought within one scheme, and a number of testable consequences are indicated.

The problem: a missing intermediate

By 1961 the stoichiometry of oxidative phosphorylation was reasonably well established: oxidation of NADH by the respiratory chain of the mitochondrion yields about three molecules of ATP per atom of oxygen consumed. The accepted interpretation followed Lipmann's doctrine of energy-rich phosphate bonds: the energy of oxidation was supposed to be conserved first in a chemical compound (written ~I or ~X), an activated intermediate common to electron transfer and to ATP formation, by a sequence of group-transfer reactions analogous to substrate-level phosphorylation. Twenty years of intensive search, however, have failed to identify any such intermediate in oxidative phosphorylation; and the requirement that the process occur in structured membrane fragments, and cease when the fragments are disrupted, has never sat comfortably with a mechanism written as a sequence of reactions in homogeneous solution.

Vectorial metabolism and the chemiosmotic proposal

The alternative explored here begins from the observation that enzyme systems embedded in a membrane need not merely catalyse scalar chemical reactions: they can be anisotropic, so arranged in the plane of the membrane that reactants are taken up from one side and products discharged at the other. A system of this kind translocates matter across the membrane as an inseparable part of its catalysis. Suppose, then, that the respiratory chain is folded across the mitochondrial coupling membrane in such a way that hydrogen carriers and electron carriers alternate along its length: the limbs of the chain which carry hydrogen atoms draw them from the inner aqueous phase, while at each turn of the fold the electrons are passed back across the membrane alone. The net effect of one turn of the chain is the translocation of hydrogen — in effect of protons, with hydroxyl ions moving equivalently in the opposite sense — from the inner to the outer phase. Electron flow therefore generates, across the membrane, a difference of pH and of electrical potential, whose sum is the driving force available for coupled processes. We may call this stored osmotic energy of the proton proticity, in analogy with electricity.

respiratory chain (vectorial in membrane):   H (inside) → H+ (outside) + e− (returned inward),

so that the membrane is charged like the plates of a condenser, positive and acid outside.

The ATPase as a proton-translocating synthase

The second element of the scheme is the familiar Mg2+-activated adenosine triphosphatase of the membrane, conceived not as a hydrolytic enzyme in solution but as an anisotropic, proton-translocating system: an ATPase whose active centre takes ADP and phosphate from the inner phase and discharges the terminal phosphate chemistry towards the outer phase in such a way that hydrolysis is accompanied by the inward translocation of protons. An enzyme of this kind is reversible in principle: driven backwards by a sufficient proticity difference — protons flowing inward through it under the electrochemical gradient established by the respiratory chain — it must synthesise ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. Phosphorylation is thus coupled to electron transfer not chemically, through a common intermediate compound, but osmotically, through the common proton circuit; exactly as in an electrical circuit, generation and load are connected by the flow of the charge carrier, not by any material intermediate passed from hand to hand.

ADP + Pi + n H+out  →  ATP + H2O + n H+in.

Scope and testable consequences

The scheme applies unchanged, mutatis mutandis, to the chloroplast, where the light-driven electron-transfer chain substitutes for the respiratory chain, and to the bacterial cell, whose cytoplasmic membrane carries both systems; here the same proton circuit can drive not only phosphorylation but the active accumulation of metabolites, linking transport and metabolism in one framework. Several consequences may be tested. (i) Intact coupling membranes are obligatory: agents which make the membrane permeable to protons specifically (the uncouplers of phosphorylation, among which 2,4-dinitrophenol has long been known to discharge phosphorylation while stimulating respiration) should act as proton conductors across the membrane, short-circuiting the proton gradient. (ii) Respiring mitochondria should acidify the medium around them in stoichiometric relation to electron flow. (iii) Imposition of an artificial difference of pH and potential across the membrane, in the absence of electron transfer, should itself drive ATP synthesis. (iv) No soluble high-energy intermediate need exist, and its continued absence is evidence for, not against, the present mechanism. These predictions distinguish the chemi-osmotic mechanism sharply from the chemical coupling hypothesis it is offered to replace.
