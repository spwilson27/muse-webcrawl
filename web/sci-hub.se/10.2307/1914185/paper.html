Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky

The Hebrew University of Jerusalem

Econometrica 47 (2): 263–291 (March 1979). DOI: 10.2307/1914185

ABSTRACT

This paper presents a critique of expected utility theory as a descriptive model of decision making under risk, and develops an alternative model, called prospect theory. Choices among risky prospects exhibit several pervasive effects that are incompatible with the tenets of utility theory: people underweight outcomes that are merely probable in comparison with outcomes obtained with certainty (the certainty effect); they are risk averse for gains and risk seeking for losses (the reflection effect); and they discard components shared by the prospects under consideration (the isolation effect). In the theory proposed, value is assigned to gains and losses rather than to final assets — carriers of value are changes of wealth — and decision weights replace probabilities, overweighting small probabilities and underweighting moderate and large ones. 1. Introduction

Expected utility theory, since von Neumann and Morgenstern, has served as both a normative logic of rational choice and a descriptive account of how people actually choose. The descriptive claim is the paper's target. Through a series of simple hypothetical choices, posed to students and university faculty, the authors show that violations of the theory's axioms are not noise or confusion: they are systematic, shared across populations, robust to stakes and incentives, and they reverse when the same problems are reframed — evidence of stable psychological principles that any descriptive theory must build in.

2. Phenomena: certainty, reflection, isolation

The certainty effect. In Problem 3 (N = 95, as remembered), respondents chose between a gamble offering 4,000 with probability 0.80 (else nothing) and 3,000 for certain: 80 per cent took the certain 3,000, although the gamble's expected value (3,200) is higher. When both outcomes were scaled down by a common factor (Problem 7: 4,000 with p = 0.20 versus 3,000 with p = 0.25), the majority reversed and took the gamble — a scaling that expected utility theory, which applies a fixed utility function to final outcomes, cannot reconcile with the first choice. Allais's paradox is exhibited in the same family (Problems 1 and 2): the pull of certainty outweighs a difference of millions in expected value.

The reflection effect. Mirror the signs — offer −4,000 with p = 0.80 against −3,000 for certain — and preferences flip: the same respondents who were risk averse over gains become risk seeking over losses, preferring the gamble (92 per cent in Problem 3′, as remembered). Risk attitude is thus not a trait of the person but of the domain: gains are treated with caution, losses with desperation — loss aversion in embryo.

The isolation effect. In a two-stage game (Problem 10), subjects first advance with probability 0.75 to a second stage choice between (4,000, 0.80) and 3,000 certain; most chose the gamble — although, collapsed to unconditional probabilities, the problem is identical to Problem 7, where most chose the sure thing. People ignore components common to the alternatives, and with them, consistency.

3. The theory: editing and evaluation

Choice is modelled in two phases. In the editing phase the offered prospects are reorganised: outcomes are coded as gains and losses relative to a neutral reference point (usually the status quo); common components are cancelled; certain gains are segregated; dominated options are discarded. Editing explains the isolation effect and much of framing. In the evaluation phase, the edited prospect (x, p; y, q) is valued by

V = π(p)·v(x) + π(q)·v(y),

where v is a value function over changes of wealth, and π a decision-weight function over probabilities. The value function is concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper for losses than for gains of the same size — losses loom larger than gains:

Fig. 1 (placeholder). The hypothetical value function: an S-shaped curve through the reference point, concave above it (gains), convex below it (losses), and roughly twice as steep on the loss side — psychophysics applied to money.

The weighting function π is increasing, with π(0) = 0 and π(1) = 1, but it overweights small probabilities (π(p) > p for small p — why both lottery tickets and insurance sell) and underweights moderate and high ones, with subcertainty: π(p) + π(1 − p) < 1. The form accommodates the certainty and reflection effects simultaneously, which no re-specification of the utility function alone can do.

4. Discussion

The authors are explicit about scope: prospect theory describes choices among simple prospects with at most two outcomes; it is offered not as a normative theory but as an account of how decisions are in fact made — and its axioms are openly looser than utility theory's (later work, cumulative prospect theory of 1992, repaired the handling of dominance and extended the weighting to ranked outcomes). The deeper moral is that the objects of choice are not states of the world but descriptions: identical problems framed as gains or losses, or differing only in an irrelevant first stage, elicit opposite preferences. Rationality, descriptively, is frame-dependent. The paper founded behavioural economics as a quantitative discipline; Kahneman shared the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics (Tversky, who would surely have shared it, died in 1996), and its constructs — reference dependence, loss aversion, probability weighting — became the working vocabulary of half the social sciences.
