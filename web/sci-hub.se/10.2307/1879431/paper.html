The Market for "Lemons": Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism

George A. Akerlof

University of California, Berkeley

The Quarterly Journal of Economics 84 (3): 488–500 (August 1970). DOI: 10.2307/1879431

ABSTRACT

This paper relates quality and uncertainty. The existence of goods of many grades poses interesting and important problems for the theory of markets. Asymmetric information — sellers knowing more about quality than buyers — can drive good goods out of a market altogether: in the used-car example developed here, buyers' willingness to pay reflects average quality, owners of good cars withdraw them, average quality falls further, and the market may unravel entirely. The paper surveys the resulting failures in insurance, employment and credit markets, and the institutions — guarantees, brand names, franchising, licensing — that arise to counteract them. I. Introduction

Consider the market for used cars. Why do nearly-new cars sell for so much less than brand-new ones? The paper's answer is information. The owner of a car knows whether it is good or a "lemon"; the buyer knows only the fraction of each on the market and prices accordingly. This asymmetry, formalised below, is no curiosity: the same structure appears wherever quality is hard to verify before purchase, and it supplies a unified explanation for phenomena as disparate as the reluctance of insurers to cover the elderly, the unemployment of minority workers whose average productivity is distrusted, and the extortionate credit terms in underdeveloped economies. (Akerlof's preface to the paper's later fame: the manuscript was rejected by three leading journals — twice as trivial, once as wrong — before the Quarterly Journal of Economics accepted it.)

II. The model: used cars

Let cars be indexed by quality x, uniformly distributed on [0, 2], owned by sellers who value a car of quality x at x, while buyers value it at (3/2)x — so trade is mutually beneficial if quality can be observed. Sellers offer car x whenever the market price p satisfies x ≤ p; buyers, unable to observe x, will pay for the average quality μ(p) of the cars actually offered:

μ(p) = p/2,    demand price = (3/2) μ(p) = 3p/4 < p for every p > 0.

Hence at any positive price, demand is zero: no cars trade at all, although gains from trade exist at every quality level. Bad cars drive out the good — Gresham's law with a mechanism — because the price cannot discriminate, and the average quality offered deteriorates with the price until the market collapses. Adding more grades, probabilistic quality, or risk-averse buyers complicates the arithmetic without rescuing the conclusion; symmetric ignorance (nobody knows quality) does rescue it, which isolates asymmetric information, not uncertainty itself, as the culprit.

III. Applications

Insurance. Medical insurance for those over 65 is expensive or unobtainable because, as the premium rises to cover average risk, the healthy withdraw and the average risk of the remainder rises — adverse selection as the lemons mechanism in actuarial dress; the argument underlies the case for group coverage and Medicare.

Employment of minorities. An employer who judges applicants by group averages creates the lemons problem in labour: statistical discrimination depresses the return to individual investment in skills, confirming the statistics it feeds on.

Credit markets in underdeveloped countries. In India, the paper notes (from the author's fieldwork), village moneylenders charge several times the rates of modern banks, in part because outsiders cannot distinguish good borrowers from bad and local lenders monopolise the information.

The cost of dishonesty. Where sellers can misrepresent quality, honest dealing itself becomes a market that can fail; the premium buyers will pay for trustworthy sellers measures the social cost of dishonesty, and mechanisms that certify honesty create real value.

IV. Counteracting institutions

Markets fight back. Guarantees shift the risk of a lemon to the seller, who knows the quality. Brand names and chain stores put a reputation, accumulated over many transactions, hostage for the quality of each single one — the buyer punishes one bad meal by never returning to any outlet of the chain. Licensing and minimum-quality standards truncate the distribution from below, restoring the average. Each institution is an informational technology, and their prevalence across economies is evidence for the model: where they are absent, as in the bazaars of developing countries, quality uncertainty dominates trade arrangements.

V. Conclusion

The paper closes by suggesting that the lemons principle is a fundamental qualification to the invisible hand: markets presume that quality can be assessed, and where it cannot, the resulting failures are systematic, not incidental. The idea proved generative — Spence's signalling and Stiglitz's screening completed the trio of information economics honoured by the 2001 Nobel Prize, awarded to Akerlof, Spence and Stiglitz "for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information".
