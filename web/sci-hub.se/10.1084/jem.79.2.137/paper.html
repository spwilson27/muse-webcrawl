The Journal of Experimental Medicine 79 (2), 137–158 (1 February 1944)

Studies on the Chemical Nature of the Substance Inducing Transformation of Pneumococcal Types: Induction of Transformation by a Desoxyribonucleic Acid Fraction Isolated from Pneumococcus Type III

Oswald T. Avery, Colin M. MacLeod & Maclyn McCarty

From the Hospital of The Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research, New York

doi:10.1084/jem.79.2.137

Abstract

A biologically active fraction, capable of inducing the transformation of unencapsulated R variants of Pneumococcus Type II into fully encapsulated cells of Type III, has been isolated in highly purified form from heat-killed smooth cells of Pneumococcus Type III. The methods of isolation are described and the evidence is presented that the chemically inducible substance is a nucleic acid of the desoxyribose type. Elementary chemical analysis, physical behaviour, and serological and enzymatic tests concur: the purified transforming material contains no detectable protein, no capsular polysaccharide and no ribonucleic acid; its ultraviolet absorption spectrum, with a maximum at 260 mμ, its nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio, its positive Dische reaction for desoxyribose and its high viscosity and structural double refraction of flow are those of a highly polymerised desoxyribonucleic acid. It resists inactivation by trypsin, chymotrypsin and ribonuclease, but is completely and irreversibly inactivated by preparations containing desoxyribonucleodepolymerase (DNase). It retains its transforming activity at dilutions of 1 in 600,000,000. The evidence presented supports the belief that a nucleic acid of the desoxyribose type is the fundamental unit of the transforming principle of Pneumococcus Type III.

Introduction: the phenomenon of transformation

In 1928 Griffith reported that mice inoculated with a mixture of living unencapsulated (R) pneumococci of one type and heat-killed encapsulated (S) pneumococci of another type died of infection with living encapsulated organisms of the second type: the dead cells had somehow conferred on the living ones the heritable property of producing the type-specific capsular polysaccharide. Dawson and Sia confirmed the phenomenon and obtained the transformation in vitro, and Alloway showed that crude cell-free extracts of the S cells could serve as the inducing agent. The transformed cells breed true: the change is permanent, hereditary, and directed — the type acquired is always that of the cells from which the extract was prepared. The question to which the present work is addressed is a chemical one: what substance, in the extract of the dead S cell, carries this hereditary instruction?

The recipient organism throughout is the R36A strain, an unencapsulated variant derived from Pneumococcus Type II, which has remained stable in the R form through years of cultivation. The donor is Pneumococcus Type III. Transformation is assayed by adding the fraction under test to a culture of R36A growing in serum broth (the serum supplying a factor required for the reaction) and, after incubation, streaking on blood agar: transformed cells appear as large, glistening, mucoid colonies of Type III morphology, and their identity is confirmed by the type-specific capsular swelling (Quellung) reaction with Type III antiserum and by mouse virulence.

Isolation of the transforming substance

The fractionation was guided at every stage by the transforming assay; the aim was to strip away, in turn, every known constituent of the pneumococcal cell and to see what remained active. Large volumes (50–75 litres) of Type III culture are killed with heat (65–70°C), and the cells harvested and extracted. The capsular polysaccharide is removed by digestion with an enzyme, derived from a soil bacillus, that specifically depolymerises the Type III polysaccharide; its disappearance is followed serologically. Protein is removed by repeated shaking with chloroform and amyl alcohol (the method of Sevag) until no gel forms at the interface; the Biuret and Millon reactions of the product are negative. Lipids are extracted with alcohol and ether. The final active material is precipitated from aqueous solution by the addition of ethyl alcohol, in which it separates characteristically as a white, stringy, fibrous mass that can be wound on a glass rod (Fig. 1) — the classical description of the sodium salt of desoxyribonucleic acid prepared from thymus glands. The yield is small: of the order of 10–25 mg of active material from 75 litres of culture.

Every quantitative test agrees with the values expected for a purified desoxyribonucleic acid, and no test betrays any other cellular constituent. In particular the material gives no precipitin reaction with antisera against Type III capsular polysaccharide or against pneumococcal protein, in amounts far exceeding those that would be needed if the activity resided in those substances.

Enzymatic evidence

The decisive test is by enzymes of known specificity. Preparations of crystalline trypsin and of chymotrypsin, under conditions in which they digest protein completely, do not diminish the transforming activity; nor does ribonuclease, which depolymerises ribonucleic acid. In contrast, crude preparations containing desoxyribonucleodepolymerase — the enzyme that depolymerises desoxyribonucleic acid, as from pancreas or from pneumococcal autolysates — destroy the activity completely and irreversibly; heating the enzyme preparation destroys its power against the transforming principle as it does against authentic DNA. Inactivation by DNase preparations strictly parallels depolymerisation of the nucleic acid as measured by loss of viscosity. The transforming substance withstands everything except the one treatment that attacks desoxyribonucleic acid, and to that treatment it is exquisitely sensitive.

Potency

The purified material induces transformation at extraordinary dilution. Activity is demonstrable in solutions diluted to 1 part in 6 × 108; the number of DNA molecules actually required to transform a culture is minute. Equally significant is the proportionality: within wide limits the number of transformed colonies recovered varies with the amount of material added, as expected if each transformation is initiated by the uptake of a small number of molecules of the active substance by individual recipient cells, after which the property is transmitted to all their progeny without further addition.

Discussion and conclusions

The results are difficult to reconcile with any constituent of the cell other than desoxyribonucleic acid. The transforming activity accompanies the nucleic acid through every step of purification; it is obtained in a fraction whose elementary composition, spectrum, and physical properties are those of DNA; it survives removal of protein and digestion by proteases and ribonuclease; and it is destroyed specifically by DNase. If a trace of some specific protein were the true agent, it would have to be present below all chemical and serological detection, to resist proteolytic enzymes, and yet be inactivated by crude enzyme preparations exactly insofar as they depolymerise DNA — a combination of ad hoc assumptions. The parsimonious conclusion is that the substance inducing the transformation is itself the desoxyribonucleic acid.

The biological implications deserve cautious statement. A nucleic acid is here shown to carry a determinant of heredity in the strictest sense: it enters a cell, directs the synthesis of a complex heritable character — the enzymes needed to make a specific capsular polysaccharide — and is thereafter reproduced along with that character. If the results of the present study are confirmed, then nucleic acids must be regarded as possessing biological specificity, the chemical basis of which is as yet unknown. Induced alterations of this kind may prove to be a general property of nucleic acids, and the phenomenon may offer an experimental approach to the chemical nature of the gene.
