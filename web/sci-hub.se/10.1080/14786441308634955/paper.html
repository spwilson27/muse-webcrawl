Philosophical Magazine Series 6, 26 (151), 1–25 (July 1913)

On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules

N. Bohr, Dr. phil. (Copenhagen)

University of Manchester (work carried out at Manchester and Copenhagen)

doi:10.1080/14786441308634955

Abstract

It is shown that the Rutherford model of the atom, in which a massive positively charged nucleus is surrounded by electrons, cannot be reconciled with the stability of atoms or with the laws of their spectra so long as ordinary mechanics and electrodynamics are assumed to hold throughout the motion. By introducing the quantum hypothesis of Planck, in the form of two postulates — that an atomic system can exist permanently only in a series of discrete stationary states, and that radiation is emitted or absorbed not continuously but in a single complete transition between two such states, its frequency being determined by the difference of the energies — a theory of the hydrogen atom is obtained. The energies of the stationary states of an electron bound to a nucleus of charge E are found to be Wn = −(2π2m e4/h2)(1/n2), and the frequencies emitted in transitions between them reproduce exactly Balmer's formula for the spectrum of hydrogen, with a value of the Rydberg constant computed from e, m and h alone in close agreement with experiment. The same rule assigns the Pickering series, observed in stellar spectra, not to hydrogen but to ionised helium. Part I of a trilogy; Parts II and III treat systems containing several electrons and the structure of molecules.

§1. Introduction: the instability of the Rutherford atom

Rutherford, from the scattering of α-particles, has been led to an atomic model of great simplicity: at the centre sits a concentrated positive charge, the nucleus, of dimensions minute compared with the atom, containing nearly the whole mass; around it are distributed electrons whose number is about half the atomic weight. On this picture the atom resembles a planetary system held together by electric forces. But a difficulty arises at once, and it is fundamental. According to the laws of electrodynamics, an electron moving in a closed orbit is accelerated and must radiate; losing energy, it will spiral ever faster toward the nucleus, emitting radiation of continuously increasing frequency, and will collapse in a very short time. Atoms on the Rutherford model are therefore impossible: they could neither persist nor emit the sharp, constant spectral lines that are their most familiar characteristic.

Nor can ordinary mechanics account for the size of atoms. From the constants of the electron — its charge e and mass m — no length can be formed; yet atoms have definite sizes of order 10−8 cm. A new constant is required, and nature has already supplied one of exactly the right character: Planck's constant h, introduced in the theory of black-body radiation. The present paper is an attempt to show that the application of Planck's ideas to the Rutherford atom removes both difficulties at a stroke, and leads quantitatively to the laws of the hydrogen spectrum.

§2. The postulates

We assume:

(1) That the radiation absorbed or emitted by an atomic system does not occur in the continuous manner required by the ordinary theory of electromagnetism, but that the system can exist without radiating in a series of stationary states, in which the dynamics may be treated by ordinary mechanics, radiation being emitted only in a complete transition from one stationary state to another.

(2) That the frequency ν of the radiation emitted in a transition between states of energy E′ and E″ is determined, not by any frequency of motion within the atom, but solely by the energy difference, according to Planck's relation

hν = E′ − E″.

These assumptions depart radically from the classical theory; but they are not arbitrary. In the second part of the theory developed below it appears that the stationary states themselves are singled out by a condition involving h: considering an electron captured into a bound orbit, and computing the energy emitted during the capture by means of an average over classical orbits, one is led to the condition that in a stationary state the angular momentum of the electron about the nucleus is an integral multiple of h/2π:

m v r = n · h / 2π,    n = 1, 2, 3, …

Further, for transitions between neighbouring states of large quantum number n, the frequency given by the postulate (2) merges into the classical frequency of revolution — as it must, since in that limit the classical and quantum descriptions of radiation have to agree.

§3. The stationary states of hydrogen

Let an electron of charge −e and mass m circulate about a nucleus of charge E (= e for hydrogen) in a circular orbit of radius r with velocity v. Ordinary mechanics gives, for the force balance and the kinetic energy T,

m v2 / r = eE / r2,    T = ½ eE / r.

The total energy, measured relative to the state in which the electron is at rest at infinity, is the negative of the kinetic energy: W = −T. Imposing the angular-momentum condition m v r = n h / 2π and eliminating v and r, one obtains the energy of the n-th stationary state,

Wn = − (2 π2 m e2 E2 / h2) · (1 / n2),

and the radius of the orbit,

rn = (n2 h2) / (2 π2 m e E).

For hydrogen (E = e) and n = 1, the ground state — the permanent state of the atom — has radius r1 ≈ 0.55 × 10−8 cm, of just the order of atomic dimensions, and binding energy W1 such that the work needed to remove the electron altogether is about 13 volts, in agreement with the ionisation potential observed. The difficulty of forming an atomic size from e and m has disappeared: the size is fixed by h.

§4. The spectrum of hydrogen

A transition from state n2 to a state n1 of lower energy emits radiation of frequency

ν = (Wn1 − Wn2) / h = (2 π2 m e4 / h3) · (1 / n12 − 1 / n22)    (for E = e).

This is precisely the form of the empirical laws of the line spectra. With n1 = 2 the formula gives Balmer's series in the visible; with n1 = 1, a series in the extreme ultraviolet (since found by Lyman); with n1 = 3, the infra-red series of Paschen. Expressed as a wave-number, the universal constant of the formula is the Rydberg constant,

R = 2 π2 m e4 / (c h3).

Using the best known values of e, m and h, this expression gives R = 109,675 cm−1, against the spectroscopic value 109,678 cm−1 — agreement within the uncertainty of the fundamental constants. The Rydberg constant, that most accurately measured of all spectral quantities, is thus expressed in terms of the charge and mass of the electron and Planck's constant, and the empirical combination law of Ritz follows immediately: every spectral frequency is the difference of two terms, the terms being the energies of the stationary states divided by h.

§5. Ionised helium and the Pickering series

For a nucleus of charge E = 2e with a single electron — ionised helium, He+ — the same calculation gives, by the factor E2 = 4, terms four times as large; transitions to the state n1 = 4 then produce lines very close to, but not exactly coincident with, the Balmer lines of hydrogen, together with additional lines halfway between them. Such a series, observed by Pickering in the spectrum of the star ζ Puppis and later produced by Fowler in a discharge tube believed to contain pure hydrogen, has hitherto been attributed to hydrogen under unknown conditions. On the present theory it belongs to helium, and the slight displacement of its lines from the hydrogen positions is accounted for by the finite mass of the nucleus, which enters the formula through a reduced-mass correction differing for the two elements. The explanation has been tested by Evans: from a tube of helium purified so as to exclude hydrogen entirely, the Pickering lines are obtained without the Balmer lines. What spectroscopy assigned to hydrogen is therefore helium's, and a long-standing anomaly becomes a confirmation.

§6. Discussion

The theory rests on a deliberate break with the classical electrodynamics of moving charges: an electron in a stationary state does not radiate, although it is accelerated, and when radiation occurs its frequency is unrelated to the frequency of any motion in either state separately. The justification offered is empirical and complete of its kind — from these postulates follow the stability of atoms, their sizes, the Balmer formula with the correct Rydberg constant, the Ritz combination principle, and the reassignment of the Pickering series. It is not supposed that the postulates are final. Rather, they indicate the form a future rational quantum theory of atomic structure must take: mechanics and electrodynamics will hold inside the stationary states and at high quantum numbers, while the transitions between states are governed by laws of a new kind, of which Planck's relation hν = E′ − E″ is the first example.
