Theory of Superconductivity

J. Bardeen, L. N. Cooper, and J. R. Schrieffer

Department of Physics, University of Illinois, Urbana, Illinois

Physical Review, Vol. 108, No. 5, pp. 1175–1204 (1957) · DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.108.1175

Abstract

A theory of superconductivity is presented in which the electrons near the Fermi surface interact through an effective attraction mediated by the lattice vibrations (phonons). Building on Cooper's demonstration that two electrons above a filled Fermi sea form a bound state for arbitrarily weak attraction, the paper constructs a many-body ground state in which the electrons are organized into pairs of opposite momentum and spin, (k↑, −k↓), all pairs sharing a common phase. The ground state is separated from the excited states by an energy gap 2Δ(0) ≈ 3.5 kTc. Elementary excitations ("quasiparticles") have energies Ek = √(εk² + Δ²). The theory accounts quantitatively for the observed thermodynamics — the exponential low-temperature electronic specific heat and the parabolic critical field — for the Meissner effect, for an infinite conductivity, and for the isotope effect (Tc varying as M−1/2), which follows naturally from the phonon origin of the attraction.

I. Introduction: the Problem

Superconductivity — the abrupt disappearance of electrical resistance below a critical temperature Tc, together with perfect diamagnetism (the Meissner effect) — was discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911, and for forty-six years resisted microscopic explanation. Phenomenological theories (Gorter–Casimir; the London equations; Ginzburg–Landau) organize the facts but do not derive them. Three clues guide the microscopic search: (1) the transition is second order, with an electronic specific heat that falls exponentially at low temperature, suggesting an energy gap in the excitation spectrum; (2) Fröhlich proposed that the relevant interaction is between electrons and lattice vibrations, and the discovery in 1950 of the isotope effect — Tc varying as the inverse square root of the isotopic mass — shows the lattice is indeed involved; (3) Cooper showed in 1956 that the normal Fermi sea is unstable: a pair of electrons with opposite momenta, interacting through an attraction confined to a thin shell ħωD about the Fermi surface, binds no matter how weak the attraction.

II. The Ground State

The paper's central step is a variational wave function for the whole Fermi sea in which each pair state (k↑, −k↓) is occupied with amplitude vk and empty with amplitude uk, uk² + vk² = 1:

Ψ0 = Πk (uk + vk bk†) |0⟩,    bk† = ak↑† a−k↓†

Minimizing the expectation value of the Hamiltonian (kinetic energy minus the pairing interaction of strength V, taken constant within the Debye shell) gives

vk² = ½ (1 − εk/Ek),    Ek = √(εk² + Δ²)

with the gap Δ determined self-consistently by

1 = N(0) V ∫0ħωD dε / √(ε² + Δ²) tanh( √(ε²+Δ²) / 2kT )

At T = 0 this yields Δ(0) = 2ħωD exp[−1/N(0)V]; setting Δ = 0 gives the critical temperature kTc = 1.13 ħωD exp[−1/N(0)V], and hence the universal ratio

2Δ(0) / kTc = 3.52

independent of the material parameters — a prediction borne out by microwave and infrared measurements of the gap. Because ωD ∝ M−1/2, the isotope effect Tc ∝ M−1/2 follows at once.

III. Excitations and Thermodynamics

Elementary excitations are formed by breaking a pair or by occupying an unpaired state; they behave as fermions ("quasiparticles") with energy Ek = √(εk² + Δ²), so at least Δ of energy is needed to create one. The density of states consequently vanishes inside the gap and piles up at its edges, Ns(E) = N(0) E/√(E² − Δ²) for E > Δ. Thermal excitation across the gap gives an electronic specific heat varying roughly as exp(−Δ/kT) at low T, and a condensation energy Hc²/8π = ½ N(0) Δ², consistent with the measured thermodynamic critical field and its near-parabolic temperature dependence.

IV. Electromagnetic Properties

A decisive requirement is the Meissner effect: the theory must expel magnetic flux, not merely conduct perfectly. Computing the current response to a static vector potential (the kernel K(q)) in the paired state gives a finite, diamagnetic response at q → 0: the supercurrent screens a weak field over the London penetration depth, and the field of a strong perturbation is expelled as observed. The coherence of the ground state — all pairs locked to a single phase — is what distinguishes the response from that of a merely perfect conductor, in which flux would be frozen in. The same coherence factors govern dynamical probes: ultrasonic attenuation falls rapidly below Tc (as exp(−Δ/kT)), while the nuclear spin-lattice relaxation rate shows a characteristic enhancement just below Tc (the Hebel–Slichter peak) because of the pile-up of states at the gap edge — both in agreement with experiment.

V. Discussion

The theory reduces the phenomenology of superconductivity to a single mechanism: an attractive interaction, supplied by virtual phonon exchange and strong enough near the Fermi surface to overcome the screened Coulomb repulsion, pairs the electrons and opens a gap. Quantitative agreement for the specific heat, critical field, penetration depth, gap ratio and isotope effect, across metals whose Tc ranges over two orders of magnitude, supports the picture. Extensions indicated include the effects of impurities (non-magnetic impurities are predicted to be largely harmless to the pairing of time-reversed states), strong-coupling corrections, and the treatment of transition metals where the simple model is least accurate.
