Observation of Gravitational Waves from a Binary Black Hole Merger

B. P. Abbott et al. (LIGO Scientific Collaboration and Virgo Collaboration)

LIGO Hanford Observatory, Washington; LIGO Livingston Observatory, Louisiana; and collaborating institutions worldwide

Physical Review Letters, Vol. 116, No. 6, 061102 (2016) · DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevLett.116.061102

Abstract

On 14 September 2015 the two detectors of the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) simultaneously recorded a transient signal, designated GW150914, with a matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio of 24 and a false-alarm rate below one event per 203,000 years (significance greater than 5.1σ). The signal rises in frequency and amplitude over about 0.2 s, sweeping from 35 to 150 Hz — the signature "chirp" of two orbiting black holes spiralling together. Comparison with general-relativistic waveform models identifies the source as the merger of black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses into a single black hole of about 62 solar masses, with the equivalent of about 3 solar masses radiated away as gravitational waves, at a luminosity distance of roughly 410 Mpc (redshift z ≈ 0.09). This is the first direct detection of gravitational waves and the first observation of a binary black hole system.

1. Introduction

Gravitational waves — propagating ripples in the geometry of space-time — were predicted by Einstein in 1916, a century before this paper. Indirect evidence came from the Hulse–Taylor binary pulsar, whose orbital decay matches the energy loss expected from gravitational radiation, but a direct detection demanded instruments of extraordinary sensitivity: the passing wave changes the separation of LIGO's test masses by about 10−18 m, a thousandth of a proton diameter. Binary black holes are expected to be among the strongest sources; their final orbits and merger occur in the strong-field, dynamical regime of gravity where the theory had never been tested. The Advanced LIGO detectors began their first observing run in September 2015 — GW150914 arrived, during engineering operations just before the run formally began, almost at once.

2. The Detectors

Each LIGO detector is a modified Michelson interferometer with 4-km arms, each arm a Fabry–Pérot cavity that folds the laser light many times and multiplies the phase shift produced by a passing wave. The two sites — Hanford, Washington and Livingston, Louisiana — are separated by 3,002 km, so an astrophysical signal should appear in both within 10 ms, while local disturbances are uncorrelated. Seismic isolation stacks, ultra-high vacuum, and high-power stabilized lasers bring the strain sensitivity to about 10−23 per root hertz near 100–300 Hz in the 2015 configuration, roughly three times better than the first-generation instruments. Calibration and environmental monitoring (seismometers, magnetometers, microphones) allow instrumental artifacts to be identified and vetoed.

3. The Observation

The search pipelines found the event independently: a burst search flagged a coherent excess of power, and the matched-filter compact-binary search recovered it with combined signal-to-noise ratio 24. The Livingston signal preceded the Hanford signal by 6.9 ms, consistent with a wave crossing the continent. In each detector the strain rises smoothly for about eight cycles: frequency increasing from 35 Hz to a peak near 150 Hz, amplitude reaching a peak strain of about 1.0 × 10−21, then a decaying ringdown as the merged black hole settles. The background rate was measured by time-shifting the two data streams against each other over an equivalent of many millennia of coincident observing; no background event approaches GW150914, giving a false-alarm rate below 6 × 10−7 per year.

4. Interpreting the Source

Parameter estimation against banks of waveforms computed from numerical relativity yields: primary mass 36+5−4 M☉, secondary 29+4−4 M☉, final black hole 62+4−4 M☉ with spin 0.67; radiated energy 3.0 ± 0.5 M☉c²; peak luminosity ≈ 3.6 × 1049 W, momentarily exceeding the combined light of all stars in the observable universe; distance 410+160−180 Mpc. Residual analysis — subtracting the best waveform and checking that what remains matches detector noise — and dedicated tests with modified waveforms find no evidence for departures from general relativity, though the constraints are at this stage limited by the single event's signal strength.

5. Conclusion

The detection opens gravitational-wave astronomy. It establishes that binary black holes exist, merge within a Hubble time, and can be more massive than the stellar black holes known from X-ray binaries. The paper closes by noting the sensitivity already achieved and the programme ahead: with design sensitivity and the Virgo detector joining, detections were expected to become routine, and each would probe gravity, dense matter and cosmic populations in ways light cannot. Predictions aside, the paper's essential claim is austere: on 14 September 2015, for the first time, humanity registered the ringing of space-time itself.
