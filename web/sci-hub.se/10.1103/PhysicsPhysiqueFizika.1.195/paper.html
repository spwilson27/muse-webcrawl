On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen Paradox

J. S. Bell

CERN, Geneva, Switzerland (on leave from SLAC, Stanford)

Physics, Vol. 1, No. 3, pp. 195–200 (1964) · DOI: 10.1103/PhysicsPhysiqueFizika.1.195

Abstract

The Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen argument of 1935 held that quantum mechanics must be incomplete, because for a pair of particles in an entangled state, measurements on one particle seem to confer simultaneous reality on non-commuting quantities of the other — unless a measurement here can affect reality there instantaneously. Bohm's version of the example, with two spin-½ particles in the singlet state, makes the issue concrete. This paper asks whether the quantum-mechanical predictions could be reproduced by a more complete theory in which additional ("hidden") variables determine the outcomes, subject to a locality condition: the result of a measurement on one particle must not depend on the setting of the distant apparatus. It is proved that it cannot. Any local hidden-variable theory must satisfy a certain inequality among correlation functions, here derived as 1 + P(b, c) ≥ |P(a, b) − P(a, c)|, whereas quantum mechanics predicts violations of that inequality for suitable choices of directions. The question of completeness is thereby converted from philosophy into an experimental one — and experiment, not interpretation, must decide.

I. Introduction

The paradox of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen was sharpened by Bohm into the example of a pair of spin-½ particles formed in the singlet state (total spin zero) and then flying apart. Quantum mechanics predicts that if the spin components of the two particles are measured along the same direction, the results are perfectly anti-correlated: σ1·a = +1 forces σ2·a = −1, for every direction a. Since the particles no longer interact, EPR's argument runs, the second particle must all along have carried definite values of every spin component — values quantum mechanics does not record. Theories which supplement the wave function with additional variables, restoring determinism and locality in the spirit Einstein wanted, are therefore worth examining; von Neumann's famous proof that such theories are impossible rests on an assumption (additivity of expectation values for non-commuting observables) which need not hold for the hypothetical dispersion-free states. The door is open — but, this paper shows, it leads nowhere, provided locality is retained.

II. Formulation

Let the additional variables be denoted collectively by λ, distributed with a normalized density ρ(λ) independent of the apparatus settings. Let A(a, λ) = ±1 be the result of measuring σ1·a on particle 1 with analyser setting a, and B(b, λ) = ±1 the result of measuring σ2·b on particle 2. The locality hypothesis is encoded in the notation: A does not depend on b, nor B on a. The correlation of the two measurements is

P(a, b) = ∫ dλ ρ(λ) A(a, λ) B(b, λ)

To reproduce the quantum-mechanical perfect anti-correlation when the settings agree, we require A(a, λ) = −B(a, λ), so that P(a, a) = −1, as quantum mechanics demands.

III. The Inequality

With B(a, λ) = −A(a, λ), the difference of two correlations can be written as a single integral, and since A takes only the values ±1, elementary algebra gives

P(a, b) − P(a, c) = − ∫ dλ ρ(λ) A(a, λ) A(b, λ) [ 1 − A(b, λ) A(c, λ) ]

Taking absolute values and using |A| = 1, the bracket supplies the third correlation, P(b, c) = −∫ ρ A(b) A(c), and one obtains the bound

|P(a, b) − P(a, c)| ≤ 1 + P(b, c)

which any theory of the assumed local form must obey. Quantum mechanics, however, predicts for the singlet state the correlation P(a, b) = −a·b = −cos θab. Choose coplanar directions with a ⊥ b and c at 45° to both, in the arrangement most unfavourable to the inequality: then the left-hand side is |−cos 90° + cos 45°| ≈ 0.707 while the right-hand side is 1 − cos 45° ≈ 0.293. The inequality is violated. Hence no distribution ρ and no local functions A, B can mimic the singlet correlations: the quantum-mechanical result cannot be reproduced by any local hidden-variable theory, however ingenious.

IV. Discussion

The paper is careful about its assumptions. Real experiments are not the idealized ones: detectors miss particles, analysers are imperfect, and the derivation leans on the perfect anti-correlation P(a, a) = −1 (later work, notably CHSH, re-derived testable inequalities without that idealization). It also stresses that the result is not a proof that hidden variables are impossible, nor that quantum mechanics is complete: a theory with instantaneous connections between distant events — like Bohm's own non-local pilot-wave theory of 1952 — can reproduce everything. What is excluded is the specifically Einsteinian hope of a locally complete account. There remains, Bell notes, the possibility that quantum mechanics itself will fail in some new regime of measurement; the merit of the inequality is that it tells experimenters exactly where to look. Experiments by Clauser, Aspect and others, over the following decades, violated the inequality as quantum mechanics predicts — closing this paper's question in quantum mechanics' favour, and opening quantum information science in the process.
