Broken Symmetries and the Masses of Gauge Bosons

Peter W. Higgs

Tait Institute of Mathematical Physics, University of Edinburgh, Edinburgh, Scotland

Physical Review Letters, Vol. 13, No. 16, pp. 508–509 (1964) · DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevLett.13.508

Abstract

In a gauge theory, exact gauge invariance seems to demand massless vector bosons, while a spontaneously broken global symmetry seems to demand massless scalar bosons (Goldstone's theorem). This short letter examines a model in which a charged scalar field with a symmetry-breaking potential is coupled to the electromagnetic field, and shows that the two massless modes cancel one another: the would-be Goldstone boson is absorbed into the gauge field, which acquires a mass, and a massive scalar particle remains in the spectrum. Spontaneous symmetry breaking in a gauge theory therefore need not imply any massless particles — a mechanism by which short-range forces can be mediated by massive vector bosons within a gauge-invariant theory.

1. The Difficulty with Broken Symmetries

Goldstone's theorem states that if a continuous global symmetry of the Lagrangian is not a symmetry of the vacuum, the spectrum must contain massless spin-zero particles. This appeared to rule out spontaneous symmetry breaking in relativistic particle physics, where no such massless scalars are seen; yet symmetry-breaking solutions are attractive for explaining why symmetries of the equations (for instance the gauge symmetries behind the weak interactions) are not manifest in the observed particle spectrum. In an earlier letter the author argued that the theorem's proof leans on assumptions about the long-range behaviour of the fields that fail in gauge theories, where indefinite-metric or long-range Coulomb effects obstruct the argument. The present note exhibits the loophole explicitly in a solvable model.

2. The Model

Take a complex scalar field φ coupled to the electromagnetic potential Aμ, with the Lagrangian containing the usual gauge-invariant kinetic term and a potential with the symmetry-breaking ("Mexican hat") form:

V(φ) = −μ² φ*φ + λ (φ*φ)²,    μ² > 0, λ > 0

The minimum of V lies not at φ = 0 but on a circle |φ| = v/√2 with v² = μ²/λ; the vacuum picks one point on this circle, so the phase symmetry is spontaneously broken while the Lagrangian keeps it. Write φ = (1/√2)(v + η + iχ) eiθ/v-style decompositions — separating the field into a radial excitation η, whose mass is √(2)μ, and a phase excitation χ, which in a purely scalar theory would be the massless Goldstone boson.

3. How the Goldstone Mode Disappears

The crucial point is the coupling to Aμ. Because the theory is locally gauge invariant, a gauge transformation can be used to remove the phase field χ from the theory entirely (the "unitary gauge"): χ is not an independent particle but the longitudinal degree of freedom of the vector field in disguise. Expanding the kinetic term about the vacuum then produces a mass term for the photon:

mA = e v (in units ħ = c = 1)

together with interactions of the massive vector with the surviving scalar η. Counting degrees of freedom confirms the bookkeeping: before breaking, a massless vector (2 polarizations) plus a complex scalar (2 real fields) gives 4; after breaking, a massive vector (3 polarizations) plus one real scalar gives 4. Nothing massless remains. In the author's closing remark, he notes explicitly that the same mechanism applied to non-Abelian gauge fields leaves behind massive vector bosons and — his key addition in this second letter — a massive scalar boson, an apparently inescapable prediction of the scheme, waiting to be searched for experimentally.
