Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?

A. Einstein, B. Podolsky, and N. Rosen

Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton, New Jersey

Physical Review, Vol. 47, No. 10, pp. 777–780 (1935) · DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.47.777

Abstract

A satisfactory physical theory, the authors argue, must satisfy two requirements: its predictions must be correct, and its description of reality must be complete — every element of physical reality must have a counterpart in the theory. They propose a criterion of reality: if the value of a quantity can be predicted with certainty without in any way disturbing the system, then an element of reality corresponds to that quantity. Applying this criterion to a pair of particles that have interacted and then separated, they show that, for such a pair, both the position and the momentum of the second particle can be regarded as elements of reality — because a measurement performed on the first particle lets one predict either quantity for the second without disturbing it. But no wave function can assign definite values to both quantities at once, since position and momentum do not commute. Hence the quantum-mechanical description is incomplete — unless one accepts that a measurement on one particle can instantaneously affect the reality of the other, which the authors regard as unreasonable. They leave open the question whether a complete description exists.

1. Completeness and the Criterion of Reality

The paper begins by separating two questions that are often confused. Whether quantum mechanics is correct is settled, so far as experiment reaches, in its favour. Whether it is complete is a further question: a complete theory is one in which every element of the physical reality has a counterpart. To discuss completeness one needs a criterion for recognizing when an element of reality is present, and the authors offer one framed as a sufficient condition: whenever the value of a physical quantity can be predicted with probability equal to unity, and this is achieved without disturbing the system in any way, then there exists an element of physical reality corresponding to that quantity. The criterion is deliberately modest — it does not claim to exhaust all ways in which reality can be recognized.

Applied to ordinary quantum-mechanical examples, the criterion reproduces familiar doctrine. If a particle is in an eigenstate of momentum, its momentum is an element of reality; its position is not, and indeed cannot be predicted. If, conversely, one tries to assign simultaneous reality to two quantities represented by non-commuting operators, quantum mechanics refuses: no state is a joint eigenstate. For a single system the theory's bookkeeping is consistent. The tension appears only when two systems that once interacted are considered together.

2. The Two-Particle Example

Let two particles interact from time t = 0 to t = T, and afterwards move apart so far that no further interaction is possible. Schrödinger's equation in principle determines the state of the combined system at t > T, although in general the state of either particle separately cannot be written down. The authors exhibit an explicit example. Consider a state in which the relative coordinate x1 − x2 and the total momentum p1 + p2 are both sharp — a state represented (in the position representation for particle II) by a superposition of plane waves with phases fixed by the separation:

Ψ(x1, x2) = ∫ dp   exp{ (2πi/h) [ (x1 − x2 + x0) p ] }

Because the operators x1 − x2 and p1 + p2 commute, nothing in quantum mechanics forbids this state. Now perform a measurement on particle I alone. If one measures its position x1, the result fixes the position of particle II at once (x2 = x1 + x0 in the conventions above), without any interaction with particle II. If instead one measures the momentum p1 of particle I, the momentum of particle II is likewise fixed. Two different wave functions for particle II — a position eigenfunction and a momentum eigenfunction — are thus obtained from one and the same two-particle state, depending on which measurement is chosen far away.

3. The Dilemma

By the criterion of §1, both the position and the momentum of particle II qualify as elements of reality: each can be predicted with certainty, and neither prediction required disturbing particle II — the particles, by hypothesis, no longer interact. Yet quantum mechanics cannot attach both quantities to one wave function, and two different wave functions (Ψ and Φ in the paper's notation for the general argument) are made to describe the same reality. Hence one of two things must hold:

(1) the quantum-mechanical description of reality is not complete, or
(2) quantities with non-commuting operators cannot have simultaneous reality

Alternative (2) can only be maintained if the reality of particle II's position and momentum is allowed to depend on which measurement is performed on particle I — that is, on a procedure carried out at a place arbitrarily distant from particle II, with no signal or interaction connecting them. No reasonable definition of reality, the authors hold, could permit such a dependence. They therefore conclude that the wave function does not provide a complete description of physical reality — while leaving open, in the paper's closing lines, whether a more complete description may one day be found. They express the expectation that it will.
