Space-Time Approach to Quantum Electrodynamics

R. P. Feynman

Department of Physics, Cornell University, Ithaca, New York

Physical Review, Vol. 76, No. 6, pp. 769–789 (1949) · DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.76.769

Abstract

This paper reformulates quantum electrodynamics in an explicitly space-time language. Instead of following the Hamiltonian evolution of field operators, the theory is built from two propagation functions: for the electron, a kernel K+(2,1) giving the amplitude for an electron to travel from space-time point 1 to point 2; and for the photon, an analogous function describing the exchange of a quantum between charges. Interactions are treated by expanding in powers of the charge e: each term of the expansion corresponds to a definite pattern of emissions, absorptions and scatterings, and each pattern is pictured by a diagram in space-time — the origin of "Feynman diagrams". The rules are developed for scattering, radiation, pair creation and annihilation, and the method is applied to the self-energy of the electron and the polarization of the vacuum. The formalism is shown to be physically equivalent to the operator field theory of Tomonaga, Schwinger and Dyson, while making many calculations far more transparent; the positron is treated throughout as an electron travelling backwards in time.

I. Introduction

The conventional formulation of quantum electrodynamics, in the version perfected by Tomonaga and Schwinger, is powerful but opaque: physical processes are computed through long operator manipulations whose intermediate steps have no intuitive meaning, and the removal of infinities (renormalization of mass and charge) is entangled with the formalism itself. In earlier work the author showed, for non-relativistic quantum mechanics, that the transition amplitude between two configurations can be written as a sum over all paths joining them, each path weighted by a phase exp(iS/ħ) with S the classical action. The present paper develops the relativistic, space-time analogue for electrons and photons. The emphasis throughout is on what happens at individual space-time points — emission here, scattering there — rather than on the state of a system on a succession of time slices.

II. The Electron Propagator and the Positron

Let ψ satisfy the Dirac equation in an external potential. The solution is written with the help of a Green's function or kernel K+(2,1): ψ(2) equals the free propagation of ψ from earlier points plus a term in which the potential acts at an intermediate point and the electron then propagates onward. Iterating this integral equation generates the perturbation series, term by term: zeroth order, straight propagation; first order, one scattering; and so on. The kernel is chosen with a specific boundary prescription — it propagates positive-energy states forward in time and negative-energy states backward — and this choice has a striking interpretation. A negative-energy electron arriving from the future is equivalent, in all observable respects, to a positron travelling from past to future: pair annihilation is an electron scattered by a potential into a negative-energy state, and pair creation is the reverse. The theory thereby treats electrons and positrons with a single field and a single propagation function.

III. Interaction with Radiation; the Diagrams

Radiation is introduced by coupling the electron to the quantized electromagnetic field, but in the space-time picture the field's effect between two charges is summarized by a photon propagation function δ+(s²) depending on the invariant interval between the points of emission and absorption. Every process is then described by specifying a set of space-time points at which the electron line changes direction, emits, or absorbs. Such a specification is drawn directly as a diagram: a solid line for the electron (an arrow running backward in time for a positron), a wavy line for a photon, vertices where they meet. Each diagram translates mechanically into an integral: a factor −ieγμ at each vertex, a propagator for each internal line, and an integral over the positions of the internal vertices. External potentials — scattering by a nucleus, say — appear as special vertices.

IV. Applications; Divergences and Renormalization

The rules are exercised on the standard problems. Scattering of an electron by a Coulomb potential reproduces the Mott formula. Compton scattering and the annihilation of a pair into two photons come out as single diagrams of second order. The genuinely new labour begins with radiative corrections: the electron's self-energy, an electron emitting and reabsorbing its own photon, is logarithmically divergent, and the vacuum polarization — a photon momentarily materializing as a pair — modifies the effective charge at short distances. The paper shows how, in this formalism, the self-energy divergence is exactly of the form of a change in the electron's mass (so it can be absorbed into the observed mass, following the programme of renormalization), and the vacuum polarization into a change of charge. A relativistic cut-off prescription is given which renders intermediate results finite without violating gauge invariance or relativity, so that observable quantities — energy shifts, scattering corrections — emerge finite and unambiguous to the order considered. The relation to Dyson's demonstration of the equivalence with the operator theory is noted: nothing physical is changed, but the bookkeeping is made visible.

V. Conclusion

The space-time formulation replaces abstract operator algebra by a sum over histories that can be drawn. For the working physicist its value is practical: the diagrams enumerate without omission the contributions at each order, identify at a glance which will be large, and make the physical content of renormalization — that the electron we observe is electron plus its cloud — almost literal. The author is candid that the treatment is heuristic in places: the method was devised by guessing at the amplitudes for processes and checking them against the known theory, and its rigorous justification rests on the equivalence with the field-theoretic formulation.
