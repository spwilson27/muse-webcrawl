Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 74 (12), 5463–5467 (December 1977)

DNA Sequencing with Chain-Terminating Inhibitors

Frederick Sanger, S. Nicklen & A. R. Coulson

Medical Research Council Laboratory of Molecular Biology, Hills Road, Cambridge, England

doi:10.1073/pnas.74.12.5463

Abstract

A new method for determining nucleotide sequences in DNA is described. It is similar to the “plus and minus” method in being based on primed synthesis with DNA polymerase, but here the synthesis is terminated specifically at particular bases by the incorporation of the corresponding 2′,3′-dideoxy analogue of the normal deoxynucleoside triphosphate. Because the analogues lack the 3′-hydroxyl group needed for chain extension, each reaction produces a nested set of radioactive fragments, of every length, sharing a common 5′ end and terminating at the positions of one particular base. The four reactions (one for each base) are run in parallel, the products are separated side by side by electrophoresis on a thin polyacrylamide gel in order of length, and the sequence is read directly from the autoradiograph as a ladder of bands. The method is rapid and simple, and sequences of several hundred nucleotides can be read from a single gel; it is illustrated by the determination of a continuous stretch of more than one hundred nucleotides of the DNA of bacteriophage φX174.

Introduction

The determination of nucleotide sequences has lagged far behind the sequencing of proteins and of RNA. The two general approaches available for DNA are the method of Maxam and Gilbert, in which a labelled strand is cleaved specifically at particular bases, and our own “plus and minus” method, which uses DNA polymerase to copy a template from a primer and deduces the sequence from the lengths of the products and from the products formed when synthesis is carried out with only subsets of the four deoxynucleoside triphosphates. The plus and minus method is rapid but can be difficult to interpret and is subject to errors from non-specific termination. What is wanted is a method of specific termination: a way of stopping the growing chain at defined positions, so that each band on the fractionation gel corresponds to one base of the sequence and none is ambiguous.

Principle of the method

The chain-terminating inhibitors are the 2′,3′-dideoxynucleoside triphosphates, ddATP, ddCTP, ddGTP and ddTTP. A normal growing chain ends in a nucleotide bearing a free 3′-hydroxyl, onto which the polymerase adds the next nucleotide by forming a phosphodiester bond. A dideoxynucleotide lacks this 3′-hydroxyl, bearing hydrogen there instead; once it has been incorporated at the end of a chain, no further nucleotide can be added, and synthesis stops at that point. If, therefore, a primed synthesis is carried out in a mixture containing all four normal triphosphates, one of them radioactively labelled, together with a small proportion of (say) the dideoxy analogue of TTP, the polymerase extending the primer will occasionally insert a ddT opposite an A of the template — and there the chain ends. The products are chains of every length at which a T occurs in the sequence being synthesised, all labelled and all starting at the primer. Four such reactions, one with each dideoxy analogue, give four families of fragments; electrophoresed in four adjacent lanes of a denaturing gel that separates DNA molecules differing by a single nucleotide, they produce the band pattern of Fig. 1, from which the sequence is read off base by base.

The arabinosyl nucleotide araCTP (1-β-D-arabinofuranosylcytosine triphosphate) acts similarly, being incorporated by the polymerase and then blocking further extension; it was in experiments with such analogues that the principle was first established in this laboratory, and the dideoxy compounds have proved the more convenient and reliable inhibitors.

Methods

Single-stranded DNA of bacteriophage φX174 serves as template. A short restriction fragment (or a specific fragment of the replicative form) is annealed to the template as primer, and synthesis is carried out with the Klenow fragment of E. coli DNA polymerase I in the presence of the four deoxynucleoside triphosphates, one carrying α-32P label. The primer is extended briefly to label the products, after which the reaction is divided into four portions and each portion receives one dideoxynucleoside triphosphate at a concentration chosen (of the order of one dideoxy molecule per hundred normal incorporations) so that termination occurs, on average, once in every one or two hundred nucleotides. The products are denatured with formamide and applied to a thin (0.35 mm) 8–12% polyacrylamide gel containing 7 M urea, run at high voltage so that the heat maintains denaturing conditions throughout. After electrophoresis the gel is fixed, dried and autoradiographed. Bands differing by one nucleotide in lengths up to several hundred residues are cleanly resolved, especially on the long gels now in use, and the distance run is a smooth function of chain length.

Reading the bands from the bottom (shortest fragments) upward, and noting the lane (A, C, G or T) in which each band appears, spells out the sequence of the synthesised strand; the template sequence is its complement. In the experiment illustrated, a continuous stretch of over 100 residues can be read unambiguously from one gel.

Results

Applied to φX174 DNA primed from a defined restriction fragment, the method yields a ladder in which every position of the sequence is occupied by a band in exactly one of the four lanes; in favourable regions a single gel reading exceeds 100–200 nucleotides, and by using several primers along a sequence (or primers on both strands) sequences of arbitrary length can be accumulated. The sequences obtained agree with those determined for the same regions by the plus and minus method and, for the φX174 genome, form part of the complete 5,386-nucleotide sequence reported from this laboratory. Compared with the plus and minus procedure, the dideoxy method has three practical advantages: every band is interpretable, since termination is specific and complete; the reading is essentially independent of the interpretation of faint or missing bands, because a band is present in one lane only; and the same labelled products can be loaded after different times of electrophoresis to read both the near and the far end of a sequence from one set of reactions. Stops caused by template secondary structure (band compressions in GC-rich regions) still occur but are rare in φX174 and can usually be recognised.

Discussion

The method brings DNA sequencing within the reach of any laboratory equipped for work with radioactive nucleotides and gel electrophoresis. Because it requires only a primer and a single-stranded template, it combines naturally with the new techniques for cloning DNA fragments in single-stranded phage vectors: any cloned fragment can be sequenced directly from a universal primer site in the vector, without purification of the fragment itself. We expect that most sequences of biological interest — genes, control regions, whole viral genomes — will soon be determined this way, and that the accumulation of DNA sequences will transform the study of gene structure and evolution much as protein sequences did a generation ago.
