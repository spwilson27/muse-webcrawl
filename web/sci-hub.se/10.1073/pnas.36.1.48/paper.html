Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 36 (1), 48–49 (January 1950)

Equilibrium Points in n-Person Games

John F. Nash, Jr.

Department of Mathematics, Princeton University, Princeton, New Jersey

doi:10.1073/pnas.36.1.48  ·  Communicated by S. Lefschetz, November 16, 1949

Abstract

One may define a concept of an n-person game in which each player has a finite set of pure strategies and in which a definite set of payments to the n players occurs when the n players play their pure strategies. The players may also use mixed strategies, in which each player uses a probability distribution over his pure strategies, his choice being independent of the choices of the others. An equilibrium point is an n-tuple of mixed strategies such that each player's strategy maximises his expected payoff against the strategies of the others. It is shown, by means of a fixed-point theorem, that every finite n-person game possesses at least one equilibrium point. The concept generalises von Neumann's minimax solution for two-person zero-sum games to arbitrary n-person games, cooperative or not.

Definitions

An n-person game is specified by giving, for each of the players i = 1, …, n, a finite set of pure strategies, and a payoff function which assigns a real number, the payoff to player i, to every combination of one pure strategy from each player. A mixed strategy for player i is a probability distribution over his pure strategies: if his pure strategies are numbered 1, …, mi, a mixed strategy is a vector si = (si1, …, simi) with siα ≥ 0 and Σα siα = 1, the number siα being the probability with which he plays his α-th pure strategy, independently of the others. The expected payoff to player i from an n-tuple s = (s1, …, sn) of mixed strategies is the expectation of his payoff over the independently chosen pure strategies; it is a function of s which is linear in each si separately:

ui(s) = Σα1 … Σαn pi(α1, …, αn) · s1α1 ··· snαn,

where pi(α1, …, αn) is the payoff to player i when the players use the pure strategies α1, …, αn.

Definition. An n-tuple s of mixed strategies is an equilibrium point of the game if, for every player i and every alternative mixed strategy ti of that player,

ui(s) ≥ ui(s1, …, si−1, ti, si+1, …, sn),

that is, if no player can gain by departing unilaterally from his strategy while the others hold theirs fixed. At an equilibrium point each player's strategy is a best reply to the strategies of the rest; the n-tuple is, in this precise sense, self-enforcing.

Theorem

Theorem. Every finite n-person game has at least one equilibrium point.

Proof. Let s be an arbitrary n-tuple of mixed strategies. For player i and his pure strategy α, define the gain function

φiα(s) = max { 0, ui(s with α substituted for si) − ui(s) },

the amount by which player i could improve his payoff by switching to the pure strategy α, the other players' strategies being unchanged, or zero if he cannot improve. Each φiα is a continuous function of s. Now define a transformation T of the set of all n-tuples of mixed strategies into itself by

T(s)iα = ( siα + φiα(s) ) / ( 1 + Σβ φiβ(s) ).

The transformation adds to each pure strategy probability in proportion to the gain available from that strategy, and renormalises; thus the image of a mixed strategy is again a mixed strategy. T is continuous, and the set of all n-tuples of mixed strategies is a compact convex set (a product of simplices). By the Brouwer fixed-point theorem, T has a fixed point s*: T(s*) = s*.

We show that a fixed point is an equilibrium point. Suppose not: then for some player i some gain function is positive at s*. Since ui is linear in si, the weighted average over α of the gains φiα(s*), weighted by the probabilities si*α, equals the gain from the strategy si* itself against the others, which is zero; hence some α with si*α > 0 must have gain zero where another has positive gain. But at a fixed point, comparing T(s*)iα with si*α for such an α gives a contradiction: the normalising denominator exceeds 1 while the numerator is unchanged, so the probability of a zero-gain strategy in positive use would have to decrease. This contradicts the fixed-point property. Therefore all gain functions vanish at s*; no player can gain by any unilateral change of strategy, pure or mixed; and s* is an equilibrium point. Q.E.D.

Remarks

The equilibrium concept reduces, for two-person zero-sum games, to von Neumann's minimax theorem: in that case the equilibrium points are precisely the pairs of optimal strategies, and all equilibrium points yield the same payoff, the value of the game. For general games this agreeable uniqueness fails — equilibrium points need not be interchangeable, nor need an equilibrium point be optimal for the players collectively, since profitable joint deviations may exist which no single player can carry out alone. It is this distinction that separates the non-cooperative theory developed here from the cooperative theory of von Neumann and Morgenstern: if coalitions and side payments are admitted, additional structure must be imposed. Equilibria with special properties can also be singled out; in a symmetric game, for example, there always exists a symmetric equilibrium point. The concept applies directly to economic competition, to bargaining (where an equilibrium analysis of the non-cooperative form of the bargaining game selects the solution the author has proposed elsewhere), and in general to any situation of conflict or interdependence among a finite number of agents with finite choices.
