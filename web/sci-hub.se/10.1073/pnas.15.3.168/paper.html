Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 15 (3), 168–173 (March 1929)

A Relation Between Distance and Radial Velocity Among Extra-Galactic Nebulae

Edwin Hubble

Mount Wilson Observatory, Carnegie Institution of Washington, Pasadena, California

doi:10.1073/pnas.15.3.168

Abstract

Determinations of distance and of radial velocity are brought together for extra-galactic nebulae for which both quantities are available. For the 24 nebulae with individually estimated distances, the radial velocities, corrected for the Sun's motion, increase roughly linearly with distance, at a rate of about 500 km s−1 per million parsecs. The relation is confirmed when the nebulae are grouped and mean values used. The two most distant objects lie at about 2 million parsecs, and the greatest velocity exceeds +1,000 km s−1. The relation may be an effect of de Sitter's cosmology, and its establishment opens the way to a determination of the metric of the observable universe.

The data

The distances of the extra-galactic nebulae rest on the absolute magnitudes of their brightest resolved stars and, for the nearest systems, on Cepheid variables. Three criteria have been used. First, for the Magellanic Clouds, the Andromeda nebula (M31) and the Triangulum nebula (M33) and NGC 6822, Cepheids give distances directly. Second, for nebulae in which the brightest stars are measurable but Cepheids are not yet found, the assumption that the brightest stars have a mean absolute magnitude of −6.3 (the value calibrated in the nearer systems) gives distances probably good to within a factor of two. Third, for the more remote individual nebulae and for the clusters, the mean absolute magnitude of nebulae themselves, −15.2, calibrated on the nearer objects, is used. These indicators furnish the distances in Table I.

Correction for solar motion

The observed velocities contain the reflex of the Sun's own motion through the system of nebulae. Writing the observed velocity as the sum of the Sun's contribution and a term depending on distance,

vobs = (solar-motion term) + K · r,

the equations of condition for the 24 nebulae were solved by least squares for the components of solar motion and for the coefficient K. The solution gives solar-motion components in satisfactory agreement with Strömberg's (a motion of about 280 km s−1 toward an apex in the northern sky), together with a conspicuous positive distance term. When the nebulae are combined into groups so as to suppress the scatter of the individual distance estimates, the solution becomes still more stable.

In round numbers the radial velocity increases by about 500 km s−1 for each million parsecs of distance:

v = K · r,    K ≈ 500 km s−1 Mpc−1.

The relation is exhibited in Fig. 1, where the corrected velocities are plotted against distance. The scatter is considerable — it reflects both the errors of the distances and the peculiar motions of the individual nebulae — but the trend is unmistakable: apart from the members of the Local Group, whose velocities are small or negative, the nebulae are receding, and the recession is proportional to their distance. No such systematic effect appears if the velocities are plotted against apparent magnitude or diameter, which excludes an artefact of the measuring process.

Discussion

The most obvious interpretation is that the nebulae are receding from our region of space, the more rapidly the farther away they are. A relation of this form is anticipated in the relativistic cosmologies based on Einstein's theory: in de Sitter's model, the spurious “motions” produced by the metric increase with distance, and Lemaître has recently shown that de Sitter's world, suitably treated, predicts just such a proportionality between velocity and distance, with a coefficient related to the rate of expansion of the universe. The data now available are not sufficient to discriminate between cosmological models, nor to determine the curvature of space; the outstanding uncertainties are the absolute calibration of the distance scale and the paucity of velocities beyond 2 million parsecs. New spectrograms with the 100-inch reflector for the faintest accessible nebulae are being obtained by Mr Humason, and may extend the relation by an order of magnitude in distance. If the proportionality holds, astronomy will possess, in the redshifts of the nebulae, a direct measure of the scale and the history of the observable universe.
