Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 44 (7), 671–682 (July 1958)

The Replication of DNA in Escherichia coli

Matthew Meselson & Franklin W. Stahl

Gates and Crellin Laboratories of Chemistry, and Division of Biology, California Institute of Technology, Pasadena, California

doi:10.1073/pnas.44.7.671

Abstract

Bacteria grown for many generations in a medium in which the nitrogen is the heavy isotope 15N have their DNA uniformly labelled with the heavy isotope. When such bacteria are transferred to ordinary (14N) medium and allowed to divide, the distribution of the heavy atoms among the DNA molecules of successive generations can be followed by equilibrium density-gradient centrifugation in caesium chloride, which resolves DNA molecules according to their density. After one generation, all the DNA is found in a single band of density exactly intermediate between that of fully heavy and fully light DNA. After two generations, two bands of equal intensity are present, one intermediate and one light. Thereafter the intermediate band persists undiminished in absolute amount while the light band grows. Denaturation of the intermediate (hybrid) DNA splits it into its two subunits, one heavy and one light. The nitrogen atoms of a DNA molecule are thus divided equally between two sub-units which are conserved through many successive replications. This is precisely the behaviour required by the hypothesis of semiconservative replication suggested by the Watson–Crick structure: each parental strand is conserved in one daughter molecule.

Three possible modes of replication

The structure proposed for DNA by Watson and Crick suggests at once how the molecule might be duplicated: the two complementary strands separate, and each serves as a template for the synthesis of a new partner, so that each daughter molecule consists of one old strand and one new. But other schemes are logically possible, and at the time of writing no direct evidence distinguishes them. Three modes may be defined. In conservative replication the parental molecule remains intact as a whole and a wholly new molecule is somehow formed alongside it. In semiconservative replication the two sub-units (strands) of the parental molecule separate and are individually conserved, each entering one daughter molecule and acquiring a newly synthesised complement. In dispersive replication the parental material is broken into pieces and distributed, more or less at random, among all the progeny molecules, so that every daughter molecule is a mosaic of old and new material in proportions that halve each generation. The experiment reported here was designed to decide among these alternatives by following parental atoms through successive duplications.

Method: density labelling and equilibrium centrifugation

The strategy is to make parental DNA distinguishable by weight. E. coli is grown for about fourteen generations in a medium whose sole nitrogen source is 15NH4Cl, so that essentially every nitrogen atom of the cellular DNA is the heavy isotope. DNA containing 15N is measurably denser than ordinary DNA: in a caesium chloride gradient the two band at densities of about 1.724 and 1.710 g cm−3 respectively, a difference of 0.014 g cm−3 which the method resolves easily. The technique, developed with Vinograd, is equilibrium sedimentation in a concentrated CsCl solution spun in the analytical ultracentrifuge: the salt redistributes in the centrifugal field to form a density gradient, and each DNA species concentrates in a narrow band at the position where its own density equals that of the surrounding solution. The bands are recorded photographically by their ultraviolet absorption, and their positions and amounts measured with a microdensitometer.

The uniformly heavy bacteria are washed and resuspended in medium containing ordinary 14N in excess, and growth is allowed to proceed. Samples are withdrawn at intervals chosen from the measured rate of increase of cell number, so that samples correspond to 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 doublings (“generations”) of the culture after transfer. The bacteria of each sample are lysed (with sodium dodecyl sulphate and digestion), and the DNA, without further fractionation, is examined in the density gradient. Control mixtures of fully heavy and fully light DNA show two sharp bands, demonstrating that the method can resolve the species whose appearance the experiment seeks.

Results

One generation: a single hybrid band

Before transfer, the DNA of the labelled culture forms a single band at the heavy position. After exactly one doubling in 14N medium, the heavy band has disappeared entirely and has been replaced by a single band whose density is precisely halfway between the heavy and light positions (Figs. 1 and 2). Every molecule of DNA in the culture is therefore a hybrid containing equal amounts of heavy and light material. This single observation already eliminates conservative replication, under which half of the DNA after one generation would be fully heavy (the conserved parents) and half fully light.

Fig. 1. Ultraviolet absorption photographs of CsCl density-gradient equilibrium runs showing DNA bands from cultures sampled at successive generations after transfer from 15N to 14N medium. Generation 0: one band at the heavy position. Generation 1: one band at the intermediate position. Generation 2: two bands, intermediate and light, of equal intensity. Generation 4: the intermediate band persists while the light band predominates.

Two and more generations: hybrid molecules persist

After two generations, two bands are present in equal amounts: one at the hybrid position and one at the fully light position. In later generations the absolute amount of hybrid DNA remains constant (so that its proportion of the total falls by half at each doubling), while all new DNA appears in the light band. The hybrid molecules are therefore not diluted or dispersed: a definite sub-unit of each original molecule survives indefinitely, passed intact into exactly one progeny molecule per generation. Dispersive replication, which predicts a single band whose density moves steadily toward the light position, is excluded.

Fig. 2. Microdensitometer tracings of the absorption photographs. The tracings quantify the band positions and intensities described in the text: the single intermediate band at generation 1; the equal intermediate and light bands at generation 2; and the constant intermediate band against the growing light band thereafter.

The hybrid consists of one heavy and one light sub-unit

To learn how the heavy material is arranged within the hybrid molecules, hybrid DNA from a one-generation sample was denatured by heating (and, in parallel experiments, by alkali), which separates the two strands of the duplex, and then banded again. The single hybrid band is replaced by two bands, at the densities of denatured heavy and denatured light DNA respectively. The hybrid molecule thus contains just two sub-units, one heavy and one light, each of the size of a single strand of the double helix; and since these sub-units are conserved without degradation through subsequent generations, they are the units of replication.

Discussion

The results may be summarised in one sentence: the nitrogen atoms of the DNA of a bacterium are distributed equally between two sub-units, and each sub-unit is conserved intact through indefinitely many replications, serving repeatedly as the template-associated partner of a newly synthesised complement. This is the semiconservative mode, and it is exactly the replication scheme written into the Watson–Crick structure, in which the two sub-units are the two strands, wound together in complementary fashion, each containing the information needed to construct the other. The experiment thus converts a structural hypothesis into an observed mechanism, and establishes at the molecular level the mode of inheritance of the genetic material: when a chromosome divides, each daughter receives one strand of the parental duplex, conserved entire.

Details remain to be worked out — whether replication proceeds continuously along the molecule, how the strands unwind, and how many replication origins a bacterial chromosome employs. The density-transfer method can be applied to these questions, and to the replication of DNA in viruses and in the chromosomes of higher organisms, where the same semiconservative pattern may be expected.
