Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 74 (11), 5088–5090 (November 1977)

Phylogenetic Structure of the Prokaryotic Domain: The Primary Kingdoms

Carl R. Woese & George E. Fox

Department of Genetics and Development, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Urbana, Illinois 61801

doi:10.1073/pnas.74.11.5088

Abstract

Comparative analysis of the oligonucleotide catalogues of the small-subunit ribosomal RNAs shows that the organisms traditionally lumped together as “prokaryotes” in fact comprise two phylogenetically distinct primary groups. One is the familiar assemblage of true bacteria — the eubacteria. The other, here called the archaebacteria, comprises the methanogenic bacteria, the extreme halophiles and certain thermoacidophiles; by the ribosomal RNA criterion these organisms are no more closely related to the eubacteria than either group is to the eukaryotes. The living world therefore divides at the deepest level into three primary kingdoms — the eubacteria, the archaebacteria and the eukaryotes — and the term “prokaryote”, though descriptively convenient, corresponds to no natural phylogenetic grouping.

A molecular criterion of relatedness

The classification of the bacteria has never rested on a secure phylogeny. Microbes offer few of the morphological characters by which the descent of plants and animals is traced, and bacterial taxonomy has consequently been an arrangement of convenience rather than a family tree. What is needed is a universal molecular chronometer: a molecule present in all organisms, functionally constant, and changing slowly enough in sequence that its present-day form records very ancient divergences. The ribosomal RNAs meet these requirements. Because the ribosome is everywhere the same machine doing the same job, its RNA sequences have evolved slowly and largely by drift; the degree of sequence resemblance between the small-subunit (16S/18S) rRNAs of two organisms measures the depth of their common ancestry.

The practical method is oligonucleotide cataloguing. The 16S rRNA is labelled with 32P, digested completely with ribonuclease T1, and the resulting oligonucleotides are separated by two-dimensional fingerprinting and sequenced, yielding a catalogue of the oligonucleotides of six or more residues characteristic of the molecule. Catalogues from two organisms are compared by the association coefficient

SAB = 2NAB / (NA + NB),

where NAB is the number of residues accounted for in oligonucleotides common to the two catalogues and NA, NB the total residues in each. SAB is 1 for identical molecules and falls toward 0.05–0.10 for the most distant comparisons yet made (bacterial 16S against eukaryotic 18S rRNAs).

Results: two unrelated radiations within the prokaryotes

Catalogues have now been obtained for more than sixty organisms. The typical bacteria — Escherichia coli, the bacilli, pseudomonads, clostridia, cyanobacteria and their kin — form a coherent group: pairwise coefficients within the group are of order 0.25–0.5 and higher, and the group as a whole is characterised by shared signature oligonucleotides. The chloroplasts of plants fall inside this group, near the cyanobacteria, as the endosymbiotic theory of their origin predicts.

A second set of organisms behaves altogether differently. The methanogens (Methanobacterium, Methanosarcina and relatives), the extreme halophiles (Halobacterium), and the thermoacidophile Sulfolobus give catalogues that resemble one another (SAB values among them comparable to those within the typical bacteria) but show only distant association — coefficients of order 0.1 — with every eubacterial catalogue, a value no higher than that between bacteria and the eukaryotic cytoplasmic lineage. These organisms are not an eccentric branch of the bacteria; they are a separate primary radiation, as old and as distinct as the split between prokaryote and eukaryote was once supposed to be.

Discussion

The molecular evidence is consistent with what little was known of the peculiar biochemistry of the second group. The methanogens reduce carbon dioxide to methane with unique coenzymes and are poisoned by oxygen; the halophiles and Sulfolobus live in environments of extreme salt or of hot acid; the cell walls of these organisms lack the peptidoglycan universal among eubacteria; their membrane lipids are ether-linked rather than ester-linked; and their patterns of antibiotic sensitivity and of ribosomal protein composition differ from the eubacterial norm. These are not a collection of oddities but the marks of a separate and ancient lineage. We propose the name archaebacteria for the group, in the belief that its members' extreme habitats and anaerobic, autotrophic tendencies may reflect the conditions under which life's primary lineages diverged.

The consequence is a re-drawing of the base of the tree of life. In place of a single prokaryotic kingdom opposed to the eukaryotes, there are three primary kingdoms — eubacteria, archaebacteria, and eukaryotes (the last descended from a third ancestral line, the urkaryotes) — each presumably descended from a common ancestral population less differentiated than any modern cell, which we have elsewhere called the progenote. The word “prokaryote” may be retained as a description of cellular organisation, but it names no phylogenetic entity and should cease to be used as if it did. If the rRNA record is read correctly, the deepest divisions among living things lie hidden inside the microbial world, and the familiar differences between a bacterium and an oak tree are, by comparison, recent and superficial.
