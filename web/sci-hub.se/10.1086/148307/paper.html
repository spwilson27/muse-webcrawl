A Measurement of Excess Antenna Temperature at 4080 Mc/s

A. A. Penzias and R. W. Wilson

Bell Telephone Laboratories, Inc., Crawford Hill, Holmdel, New Jersey

The Astrophysical Journal, Vol. 142, pp. 419–421 (1965) · DOI: 10.1086/148307

Abstract

Measurements of the effective zenith noise temperature of the 20-foot horn-reflector antenna at Crawford Hill, Holmdel, New Jersey, at a frequency of 4080 Mc/s (wavelength 7.35 cm) have been made with a travelling-wave maser radiometer referred to a cold load. The total antenna temperature measured at the zenith is 6.7 K, of which 2.3 K is attributable to atmospheric emission and about 0.9 K to ohmic losses in the antenna and ground radiation entering the sidelobes. There remains an excess of about 3.5 ± 1.0 K over the expected contributions. This excess is, within the limits of the observations, isotropic, unpolarized, and free from seasonal variation.

1. The Antenna and Radiometer

The receiving system used for satellite communications experiments at Crawford Hill consists of a 20-foot horn-reflector antenna — the same antenna used by Ohm and by DeGrasse and others in early absolute-flux work — followed by a travelling-wave maser amplifier of very low noise temperature. For absolute measurements the receiver is switched, in the manner of a Dicke radiometer, between the antenna and a reference termination held at the temperature of liquid helium (about 4.2 K). The comparison is made with a precision attenuator, so that the antenna temperature can be transferred to the cold load standard with an instrumental accuracy of a few tenths of a degree.

The present measurements were made at 4080 Mc/s, where the antenna beam width is about 1.6 degrees and the losses in the antenna structure are small and calculable. Great care was taken to account for every known contribution to the received noise: the maser and waveguide losses, emission from the ground picked up in the back lobes, and radiation from the atmosphere along the beam.

2. The Measurement

With the antenna pointed at the zenith, the effective antenna temperature was found to be:

Tzenith = 6.7 ± 0.3 K

The expected contributions are itemized in the following table.

ContributionTemperature (K)
Measured total, at zenith6.7
Atmospheric emission2.3 ± 0.3
Ohmic losses in antenna0.8 ± 0.4
Ground radiation (back lobes)≤ 0.1
Unexplained excess3.5 ± 1.0

The atmospheric contribution, 2.3 K, follows from the measured attenuation and the standard atmosphere; it varies with elevation in the expected way, and allowance for it was checked by tipping the antenna. The residual excess of about 3.5 K does not vary with direction of pointing, within the accuracy of the observations, and repeated measurements over the period from October 1964 to the spring of 1965 show no seasonal change. Tests with the antenna rotated in azimuth and with a polarized source show the excess to be unpolarized at the level of the measurements.

3. Possible Sources of the Excess

Known discrete radio sources contribute far too little to account for the excess. Radiation from our own galaxy at 7.35 cm, extrapolated from lower-frequency surveys, is estimated at less than 0.1 K away from the galactic plane. Emission from the halo of the galaxy, if any, would show a directional dependence that is not observed. The excess also cannot be ascribed to any instrumental effect known to us: the calibration was repeated with different arrangements of the reference load, and the travelling-wave maser system was checked against a second receiver.

A possible explanation of the excess antenna temperature is given in the accompanying letter by Dicke, Peebles, Roll, and Wilkinson, who point out that radiation from a hot, dense early stage of the universe, red-shifted into the microwave region, would appear as an isotropic background of roughly this magnitude. We have no direct evidence bearing on that interpretation, and we report the measured excess as it stands.
