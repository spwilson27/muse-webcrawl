Observational Evidence from Supernovae for an Accelerating Universe and a Cosmological Constant

Adam G. Riess, Alexei V. Filippenko, Peter Challis, Alejandro Clocchiatti, Alan Diercks, Peter M. Garnavich, Ron L. Gilliland, Craig J. Hogan, Saurabh Jha, Robert P. Kirshner, B. Leibundgut, M. M. Phillips, David Reiss, Brian P. Schmidt, Robert A. Schommer, R. Chris Smith, J. Spyromilio, Christopher Stubbs, Nicholas B. Suntzeff, and John Tonry (High-Z Supernova Search Team)

University of California, Berkeley; Harvard–Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics; Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory; and collaborating institutions

The Astronomical Journal, Vol. 116, No. 3, pp. 1009–1038 (1998) · DOI: 10.1086/300499

Abstract

We present observations of 10 Type Ia supernovae at high redshift (0.16 ≤ z ≤ 0.62), discovered during the first two years of the High-Z Supernova Search, together with a reanalysis of 27 nearby Type Ia supernovae. Distances are measured from multicolor light curves using the MLCS method, which corrects each supernova for its luminosity–light-curve-shape relation and for extinction. Comparing the distant and nearby samples, we find that the high-redshift supernovae are systematically fainter — by roughly a quarter of a magnitude — than expected in a universe whose expansion is decelerating under the gravity of ordinary matter. Cosmological fits over a broad class of models favour a presently accelerating expansion, driven by a positive cosmological constant. For a flat universe, the data give a matter density ΩM ≈ 0.24 and a cosmological-constant density ΩΛ ≈ 0.72, each with uncertainties of roughly ±0.1 from statistics alone. The probability that the acceleration is an artifact of the known systematic effects examined is small; grey dust or simple luminosity evolution cannot easily mimic the signal, because such effects would also disturb the agreement at intermediate redshift.

1. Introduction

The deceleration of the cosmic expansion has long been a central target of observational cosmology: measuring it would reveal the mean density of matter, ΩM, and whether the universe will expand forever or recollapse. A second parameter, the cosmological constant Λ introduced by Einstein in 1917 and long dismissed as an embarrassment, has lately returned to theoretical favour — it can reconcile the expansion age of the universe with the ages of the oldest stars, and it arises naturally as the energy density of the vacuum in quantum field theory. If ΩΛ > 0, the expansion today may be accelerating rather than decelerating, and the two cases can be distinguished by measuring distances to very remote standard candles.

Type Ia supernovae are the best such candles. They are luminous enough to be seen across half the observable universe, and after correction for the empirical relation between peak luminosity and light-curve shape (broader light curves are intrinsically brighter) and for dust reddening, their distances can be measured to about 7 per cent per object. Our team has been conducting a systematic search for high-redshift supernovae since 1995, scheduling discovery runs on dark nights at large telescopes and following the candidates with photometry and spectroscopy on a predetermined cadence — a strategy that yields well-sampled light curves rather than single snapshots.

2. Observations

The high-redshift sample analysed here comprises 10 spectroscopically confirmed Type Ia events at redshifts between about 0.16 and 0.62 (including SN 1995K, SN 1996bo, SN 1996bv, SN 1997ap, SN 1997cj, SN 1997ce and others), imaged principally with the CTIO 4 m and other northern and southern telescopes in the R and I bands, with template images taken after the supernovae had faded. Spectra obtained near maximum light with the Keck and MMT telescopes confirm the Type Ia classification and provide the redshifts from host-galaxy features. The nearby comparison sample of 27 supernovae rests on the Calán/Tololo survey and other low-redshift photometry of comparable quality.

3. The MLCS Method

Distances are estimated with the Multicolor Light Curve Shape (MLCS) method. A family of model light curves is parameterized by a single luminosity/shape parameter Δ; observed colours simultaneously determine the amount of host-galaxy extinction, assumed to follow a standard Galactic reddening law. Fitting each supernova's multiband photometry yields its distance modulus, its extinction, and its intrinsic luminosity class in one self-consistent procedure, with the training of the method anchored on nearby supernovae having independent distances. Because the high- and low-redshift samples are processed identically, method-specific systematics largely cancel in the differential comparison.

4. Results

The Hubble diagram constructed from the two samples shows the effect plainly. At redshifts near z ≈ 0.5, the distant supernovae lie about 0.25 mag below (fainter than) the locus expected for a low-density, matter-only universe (ΩM ≈ 0.2, ΩΛ = 0), and still farther below the prediction of a critical-density matter-only model. Larger luminosity distance at given redshift means the expansion has sped up: looking back in time, the universe was expanding more slowly relative to its present rate than any decelerating model permits.

5. Systematic Uncertainties

Because the claim is extraordinary, the paper devotes extensive discussion to alternative explanations. (i) Extinction by grey dust in the intergalactic medium could dim distant supernovae without reddening them; however, such dust would have to be implausibly smooth and would re-radiate the absorbed energy in the far infrared, and it would need to produce the observed turnover of the effect with redshift. (ii) Evolution of the supernova population with cosmic time — changes in progenitor metallicity or mass — might make distant events intrinsically fainter; against this, the spectra and light-curve properties of the distant sample closely match those nearby, and any evolution would have to conspire to vary with redshift in just the way Λ predicts, growing from z ≈ 0.1 to z ≈ 0.5. (iii) Selection effects (Malmquist bias) are estimated by simulation of the search and act, if anything, to make distant supernovae appear brighter, opposite to the signal. (iv) Photometric zero points and K-corrections were checked by independent reductions. None of these effects, singly or in plausible combination, reproduces both the amplitude and the redshift dependence of the observed dimming.

6. Discussion

The natural reading of the evidence is that the expansion of the universe is accelerating, and that roughly 70 per cent of its present energy density is in a component with negative pressure, most simply a cosmological constant — vacuum energy. The result is concordant with independent indications accumulating at the same time: the low matter density from cluster dynamics and large-scale structure, the flatness suggested by the cosmic microwave background, and the age problem of an Einstein–de Sitter universe. Confirmation with supernovae at z > 1, where Λ and matter-only models make opposite predictions about dimming, is the obvious next test: at those redshifts the accelerating model expects supernovae to be brighter than the naive extrapolation of the z ≈ 0.5 trend, since they sample the earlier, decelerating era.
