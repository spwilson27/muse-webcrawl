Isolation of a T-Lymphotropic Retrovirus from a Patient at Risk for Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS)

F. Barré-Sinoussi, J. C. Chermann, F. Rey, M. T. Nugeyre, S. Chamaret, J. Gruest, C. Dauguet, C. Axler-Blin, F. Vézinet-Brun, C. Rouzioux, W. Rozenbaum, L. Montagnier

Unité d'Oncologie Virale, Institut Pasteur, Paris; Hôpital de la Pitié-Salpêtrière, Paris

Science 220 (4599): 868–871 (20 May 1983). DOI: 10.1126/science.6189183

ABSTRACT

A retrovirus was isolated from the cultured T lymphocytes of a patient with the persistent generalised lymphadenopathy that frequently precedes acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS). The virus, detected by its reverse transcriptase activity and seen budding from the cell surface by electron microscopy, infects and grows in T lymphocytes from healthy donors. It is not immunologically or morphologically identical to the known human T-cell leukaemia viruses HTLV-I and HTLV-II, although it belongs to the same broad family; it is provisionally named lymphadenopathy-associated virus (LAV). The findings suggest that this new human retrovirus may be connected with the lymphadenopathy syndrome and with AIDS. [Reconstruction from memory; paraphrased.]

1. Introduction

Since 1981 a new syndrome of profound cellular immunodeficiency, presenting with opportunistic infections and Kaposi's sarcoma in previously healthy young men, had been recognised in the United States and Europe. Epidemiological evidence — spread by sexual contact and blood, concentration in defined risk groups — pointed to a transmissible agent, and the destruction of helper (T4) lymphocytes suggested a T-lymphotropic virus. Human retrovirology had just produced its first isolates: HTLV-I (1980) and HTLV-II (1982) from Gallo's laboratory, associated with adult T-cell leukaemia. The Pasteur group therefore looked for a retrovirus in a patient at an early, pre-AIDS stage — a man (designated BRU) with persistent lymphadenopathy, a prodrome suspected to precede full AIDS — reasoning that in advanced cases the target cells might already be destroyed.

2. Culture and detection of reverse transcriptase

Lymph-node lymphocytes from the patient were cultured with phytohaemagglutinin and interleukin-2 (then called T-cell growth factor), with antiserum to human interferon-α to suppress an antiviral state that had masked retrovirus replication in earlier attempts. After about two weeks the culture supernatant developed reverse transcriptase activity — incorporation of labelled nucleotide into DNA on a poly(rA)·oligo(dT) template, with the Mg²⁺ preference characteristic of retroviral polymerases — peaking around day 15 and declining as the producing cells died. The activity banded in sucrose gradients at a density of about 1.16 g/ml, as retrovirus particles do, and labelled particles could be precipitated from the supernatant. The virus could be transmitted to stimulated T lymphocytes from healthy donors, in whose cultures it again grew transiently and killed the cells.

3. Electron microscopy and characterisation

Electron microscopy of the infected cultures showed particles budding from the plasma membrane and free virions of typical retroviral form, about 100–140 nm in diameter, but with a distinctive morphology: a dense, eccentric, often bar- or cone-shaped core, differing from the rounder cores of HTLV particles and resembling rather the lentiviruses of animals (such as visna virus of sheep). Viral proteins were labelled metabolically and analysed; antiserum from the patient reacted with proteins of the isolate, notably a p25 core protein, while reference antisera to HTLV-I core proteins (p24) reacted only weakly or not at all. Cross-comparisons of the major core protein by competition radioimmunoassay showed the new isolate to be distinct from HTLV-I and HTLV-II — related to them as family, not identical as strain.

4. Discussion

The paper is carefully tentative: one isolate, from one prodromal patient, establishes existence, not causation. The authors note that the virus infects T lymphocytes and can kill them in culture, that patients with lymphadenopathy syndrome stand at risk of AIDS, and that the isolate merits screening in AIDS patients and controls — studies the group reported within the year, finding the virus (as LAV, and in parallel isolates by Gallo's group as HTLV-III, and by Levy as ARV) in the great majority of AIDS cases and rarely in healthy donors. The virus, renamed human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in 1986, became the accepted cause of AIDS; its discovery enabled blood screening (1985) and every subsequent therapy. Authorship of the discovery was contested for years between Paris and Bethesda — a dispute settled diplomatically in 1987 and revisited by later historical inquiries — and in 2008 Barré-Sinoussi and Montagnier (with Harald zur Hausen) received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
