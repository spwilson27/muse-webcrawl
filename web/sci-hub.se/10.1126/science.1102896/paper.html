Electric Field Effect in Atomically Thin Carbon Films

K. S. Novoselov, A. K. Geim, S. V. Morozov, D. Jiang, Y. Zhang, S. V. Dubonos, I. V. Grigorieva, A. A. Firsov

Department of Physics, University of Manchester, UK; Institute for Microelectronics Technology, Chernogolovka, Russia

Science 306 (5696): 666–669 (22 October 2004). DOI: 10.1126/science.1102896

ABSTRACT

We describe monocrystalline graphitic films that are a few atoms thick but are nonetheless stable under ambient conditions, metallic, and of remarkably high quality. The films are found to be a two-dimensional semimetal with a tiny overlap between valence and conduction bands, and they exhibit a strong ambipolar electric field effect such that electrons and holes in concentrations up to 1013 cm−2 and with room-temperature mobilities of about 10,000 cm2/V·s can be induced by applying gate voltage. 1. Introduction

It has long been assumed that strictly two-dimensional crystals cannot exist in free form: theory (the Landau–Peierls argument, formalised by the Mermin–Wagner theorem) holds that thermal fluctuations destroy long-range order in 2D, and thin films grown experimentally roughen or decompose well before reaching monolayer thickness. Yet quasi-2D layers had been approached for decades — as the surface of graphite, as intercalation compounds, as the hypothetical building blocks of fullerenes and nanotubes. The authors report the direct isolation of the limiting case: single layers of carbon atoms, graphene, exfoliated from bulk graphite and supported on a substrate, stable in air and measurably metallic.

2. Preparing atomically thin films

The method is disarmingly simple — mechanical cleavage. A graphite mesa is etched into platelets, pressed against a surface and peeled repeatedly with adhesive tape until the thinnest flakes transfer to an oxidised silicon wafer. Most flakes are tens of layers thick, but some are one to three layers. The 300-nm SiO₂ layer is not a passive support: by thin-film interference it makes even a one-atom-thick flake faintly visible under an optical microscope, which is what makes the flakes findable at all. Candidate monolayers are confirmed by atomic force microscopy (with care: adsorbed water makes the apparent height read ~1 nm rather than 0.34 nm) and by Raman spectroscopy. Standard electron-beam lithography then patterns Hall-bar devices with metal contacts, the doped silicon substrate serving as a back gate.

3. The ambipolar field effect

The central result is in the gate response. At zero gate voltage the films are near charge neutrality, with small intrinsic carrier densities. Sweeping the gate voltage Vg positive induces electrons; negative, holes — and the resistivity traces a sharp peak, of order h/4e², at the neutrality point between the two regimes. The induced density follows the parallel-plate relation

n = ε₀ε Vg / (t e) ≈ 7.2 × 1010 · Vg cm−2 (Vg in volts)

reaching 1013 cm−2 at 100 V. Both carriers show mobilities around 10,000 cm²/V·s at room temperature — extraordinary for a material one atom thick, with every atom a surface atom — and the mobility depends only weakly on temperature, being limited by extrinsic scattering rather than phonons. Hall measurements confirm the sign change of the carriers across the neutrality point.

4. Magnetotransport and band structure

In perpendicular magnetic fields the samples show Shubnikov–de Haas oscillations periodic in 1/B, from which effective carrier masses of about 0.03–0.06 of the free-electron mass are extracted near the densities studied, and a quantum Hall effect develops at low temperature. The data are consistent with graphene's known band structure: two bands touching at points of the Brillouin zone, near which the dispersion is conical, E = ℏvF|k|, with vF ≈ 10⁶ m/s — so the carriers behave as massless relativistic particles (an analogy developed in the group's following papers). Even at neutrality the conductivity does not fall to zero but saturates near the quantum unit 4e²/h, the celebrated minimum conductivity.

5. Conclusions

Atomically thin carbon films exist, can be made by a technique accessible to any laboratory, and behave as a gate-tunable two-dimensional semimetal at room temperature in air. As devices, they suggest field-effect transistors scaled to a single atomic layer; as physics, they open the study of relativistic-like charge carriers in a tabletop system. The paper — with its companion works on the anomalous quantum Hall effect the next year — founded graphene research and two-dimensional materials; Novoselov and Geim received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2010.
