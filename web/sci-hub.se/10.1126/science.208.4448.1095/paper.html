Extraterrestrial Cause for the Cretaceous–Tertiary Extinction

Luis W. Alvarez, Walter Alvarez, Frank Asaro, Helen V. Michel

Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory and Departments of Physics and of Geology and Geophysics, University of California, Berkeley

Science 208 (4448): 1095–1108 (6 June 1980). DOI: 10.1126/science.208.4448.1095

ABSTRACT

Platinum-metal analyses of sedimentary rocks at the Cretaceous–Tertiary (K–T) boundary show a large enrichment of iridium — as high as roughly 30 times background at Gubbio, Italy — confined to a thin clay layer exactly at the faunal break. Because iridium is severely depleted in the Earth's crust but relatively abundant in extraterrestrial material, the anomaly is interpreted as the fall-out of debris from the impact of an asteroid some 10 km in diameter. The authors estimate the global extent of the layer and argue that the resulting dust cloud, darkening the Earth for years, suppressing photosynthesis and collapsing food chains, is the cause of the mass extinction that ended the Cretaceous. A supernova origin is considered and rejected on isotopic grounds. [Reconstruction from memory; figures as remembered, paraphrased.]

1. The problem

The Cretaceous period ends, 66 million years ago, with one of the great mass extinctions: the dinosaurs, the ammonites, the marine reptiles and most planktonic foraminifera vanish from the record within a geologically brief interval. Terrestrial explanations — climate cooling, sea-level change, disease, competition — struggle with the extinction's speed and with its simultaneity across land and sea. The authors approached the boundary not as palaeontologists but as physicists: they proposed to measure how long the boundary clay at Gubbio took to accumulate, using the steady rain of cosmic dust as a clock, via the iridium that dust delivers.

2. The iridium anomaly

Samples of limestone and clay across the K–T boundary near Gubbio — where a 1-cm clay seam marks the break between Cretaceous limestones rich in large foraminifera and Tertiary limestones with small ones — were analysed by neutron activation at Berkeley. Instead of the modest, constant iridium level the cosmic-clock idea required, the clay carried an iridium concentration of about 9 parts per billion against roughly 0.3 ppb in the limestones above and below: an enrichment of a factor of about 30, precisely at the boundary. The same signature was then found at Stevns Klint, Denmark (the "fish clay"), and at Woodside Creek, New Zealand — three widely separated sites, marine and different in facies, all showing the spike exactly at the extinction horizon.

3. Book-keeping: how much extraterrestrial matter?

Integrating the anomaly over the boundary layer gives a global fall-out of iridium that, at chondritic abundances, corresponds to an impacting body of diameter about 10 km (10 ± 4 km), mass of order 1015 kg, striking at a velocity of order 20 km/s — releasing energy of order 108 megatons of TNT, thousands of times the world's nuclear arsenals. Only a small fraction of the projectile would be vaporised fine enough to spread worldwide; the calculation is consistent with a stratospheric dust veil persisting for 3–10 years.

4. Alternative sources considered

Could the iridium be terrestrial, concentrated by chemistry or biology? The authors argue no concentrating mechanism is known that operates simultaneously in Italian, Danish and New Zealand sediments while sparing the beds a metre away. Could a nearby supernova have delivered it, along with a lethal radiation dose? A supernova would also deliver plutonium-244, whose absence in the boundary clay — measured sensitivity being adequate — rules the hypothesis out. The asteroid interpretation alone fits all three facts: the element ratios (chondritic), the global thinness of the layer, and its exact coincidence with the extinction.

5. How an impact kills

The proposed mechanism is darkness. Fine ejecta and dust in the stratosphere would cut sunlight to a small fraction of normal for a period of years — long enough to stop photosynthesis on land and in the surface ocean. Food chains collapse from the bottom: plants die, herbivores starve, then carnivores. Organisms with dormant stages, seeds, or detritus-based diets (the freshwater and benthic communities) survive disproportionately — matching the observed pattern, in which mammals, birds, crocodiles and turtles persist while the great reptiles and the ammonites perish. Acid rain from shock-heated air and a pulse of wildfire are discussed as auxiliary stresses.

6. Conclusions and aftermath

The paper's claim — that a cosmic accident, not Earth-bound gradualism, decapitated the Mesozoic world — was initially resisted by much of palaeontology, and the authors say so, inviting the search for the crater. Confirmation accumulated through the decade: shocked quartz with planar deformation features at boundary sites worldwide (Bohor and colleagues, 1984), tsunami deposits around the Gulf of Mexico, soot layers, and finally the crater itself — the ~180–200 km Chicxulub structure buried under Mexico's Yucatán, identified in 1990–91 from gravity data and dated to 66 Ma. Impact winter, modelled by the nuclear-winter calculations of the same era, became the standard mechanism, and the paper the founding document of impact geology.
