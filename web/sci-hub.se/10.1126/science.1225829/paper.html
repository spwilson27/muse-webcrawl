A Programmable Dual-RNA-Guided DNA Endonuclease in Adaptive Bacterial Immunity

Martin Jinek, Krzysztof Chylinski, Ines Fonfara, Michael Hauer, Jennifer A. Doudna, Emmanuelle Charpentier

Howard Hughes Medical Institute and Department of Molecular and Cell Biology, University of California, Berkeley; Institute of Biochemistry, Vilnius University; Umeå University; Laboratory of Microbiology, Wageningen (as remembered)

Science 337 (6096): 816–821 (17 August 2012, published online 28 June 2012). DOI: 10.1126/science.1225829

ABSTRACT

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated (Cas) systems provide bacteria and archaea with adaptive immunity against viruses and plasmids by RNA-guided DNA cleavage. In the type II system, the Cas9 endonuclease is guided by a CRISPR RNA (crRNA) annealed to a trans-activating crRNA (tracrRNA). The paper shows, with purified components in vitro, that Cas9-crRNA-tracrRNA complexes cleave target DNA containing a sequence complementary to the crRNA and an adjacent protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM), cutting both strands within the protospacer, and that the dual RNA can be replaced by a single engineered guide RNA. Cas9 is thus a programmable DNA endonuclease whose target is specified by a short RNA sequence. [Reconstruction from memory; paraphrased.]

1. Background: an RNA-guided immune system

Bacteria accumulate, in CRISPR arrays, short "spacer" sequences copied from invading phage and plasmid DNA, interleaved with repeats. Transcribed and processed into crRNAs, these spacers program Cas protein complexes to find and cut matching foreign DNA — a heritable, sequence-specific immune memory. Systems fall into types I–III. The type II system is the simplest: a single large protein, Cas9, guided by two small RNAs — the crRNA carrying the spacer, and the tracrRNA, which base-pairs with the crRNA's repeat portion and is required for its maturation by host RNase III. What Cas9 actually does to DNA — where it cuts, what it requires, and whether its parts suffice in isolation — was unresolved. The paper settles the mechanism with biochemistry.

2. Cas9 cleaves DNA guided by the dual RNA

Recombinant S. pyogenes Cas9 was purified and reconstituted with synthetic crRNA and tracrRNA. On plasmid and oligonucleotide substrates carrying a matching protospacer, the ternary complex cleaved the DNA in a reaction requiring Mg²⁺ and the presence of both RNAs; Cas9 alone, or with either RNA alone, was inactive. Cleavage occurred on both strands at a fixed position three base pairs upstream of the PAM (5′-NGG-3′ in the target), generating predominantly blunt ends. The PAM — a short motif in the DNA target that is absent from the bacterium's own CRISPR array — is essential: substrates lacking it, or bearing a mutated PAM, were not detectably cut, providing the self/non-self discrimination the biology demands.

Fig. 2 (placeholder). Denaturing gel assays of cleavage products, mapping the cut site to a position 3 bp upstream of the PAM on both the complementary and non-complementary strands.

3. Two nuclease domains, one strand each

Cas9 carries two nuclease motifs: an HNH domain and a RuvC-like domain. Point mutations dissect their roles cleanly. Inactivating the HNH domain (H840A) leaves cleavage of the strand complementary to the crRNA intact while abolishing cleavage of the other strand; inactivating RuvC (D10A) does the converse. Thus HNH cuts the complementary strand and RuvC the non-complementary strand, and each mutant is a nickase cutting only one strand — a remark of the paper that later became a tool in its own right. Mismatches between guide and target, and the position of mismatches relative to the PAM-proximal "seed" region, modulate cleavage, foreshadowing the specificity questions that would dominate applications.

4. A single guide RNA replaces the pair

The decisive engineering step: joining crRNA and tracrRNA into one transcript — a single-guide RNA (sgRNA), in which the guide sequence is fused through a tetraloop to the tracrRNA scaffold — yields complexes that cleave target DNA comparably to the natural dual-RNA system. Specificity becomes modular: to retarget Cas9, one changes only the 20-nucleotide guide segment of a single RNA. The authors state the conclusion plainly in the paper's closing words (as remembered): the system "provides a powerful and versatile tool" — a family of RNA-guided endonucleases that could, in principle, be programmed to cut any DNA sequence in any cell. Five different guide sequences cutting five different sites demonstrated the generality.

5. Discussion

With mechanism established — dual-RNA guidance, PAM dependence, twin nuclease domains, blunt cleavage three bases from the PAM — the type II system stands apart from the multi-subunit type I and III complexes as a single-protein programmable nuclease. Within months, companion papers (Mali, Yang, Esvelt, Aach, Guell, DiCarlo, Norville and Church; Cong, Ran, Cox and colleagues, Science, January 2013) showed Cas9 editing genomes in human and mouse cells, and the field of genome editing was reorganised around it. Charpentier and Doudna received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for the development of a method for genome editing", the citation resting on this paper.
