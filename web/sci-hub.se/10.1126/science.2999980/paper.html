Enzymatic Amplification of β-Globin Genomic Sequences and Restriction Site Analysis for Diagnosis of Sickle Cell Anemia

Randall K. Saiki, Stephen Scharf, Fred Faloona, Kary B. Mullis, Glenn T. Horn, Henry A. Erlich, Norman Arnheim

Cetus Corporation, Emeryville, California; Department of Biological Sciences, University of Southern California, Los Angeles

Science 230 (4732): 1350–1354 (20 December 1985). DOI: 10.1126/science.2999980

ABSTRACT

A segment of the human β-globin gene was amplified enzymatically from genomic DNA by repeated cycles of primer-directed synthesis with the Klenow fragment of Escherichia coli DNA polymerase I — the polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Twenty cycles increased the amount of the target sequence, a 110-base-pair fragment spanning the sickle-cell mutation site, by a factor of more than 200,000. After amplification, the genotype at the sickle locus could be read directly by restriction digestion: the normal βA sequence contains a site for the enzymes DdeI and MstII that the sickle βS mutation abolishes, so normal, carrier and affected individuals give distinct, easily scored fragment patterns from a fraction of a microgram of DNA. The method offers a rapid prenatal and carrier diagnosis of sickle cell anemia without the radioactivity and labor of Southern blotting. 1. Introduction

Sickle cell anemia results from a single nucleotide substitution (A→T) in the sixth codon of the β-globin gene, changing glutamate to valine. Diagnosis by DNA analysis was possible in principle — the mutation destroys a recognition site for the restriction enzymes DdeI and MstII — but in unamplified genomic DNA the β-globin locus is one part in millions, so detecting it required cloned probes, radioactive labelling and Southern blotting of large amounts of DNA over days. Mullis's idea, developed at Cetus, was to make the target abundant first: flank it with two short synthetic primers and let a polymerase copy the segment between them, then melt, anneal and copy again, doubling the target in every cycle. This paper is the first published demonstration that the scheme works on real genomic DNA and solves a real diagnostic problem.

2. The amplification method

Two oligonucleotide primers (PC03 and PC04 in the paper's nomenclature, each about 20 nucleotides) were synthesised complementary to opposite strands of the β-globin gene, bracketing a 110-bp region containing the sickle mutation site. A reaction containing human genomic DNA, primers, the four deoxynucleotide triphosphates, buffer and the Klenow fragment of DNA polymerase I was subjected to repeated cycles of: heat denaturation (about 95 °C) to separate strands; cooling to allow primer annealing; and extension at 37 °C, in which polymerase synthesised new strands from each primer across the target region. Fresh enzyme was added each cycle, the Klenow fragment being inactivated by the denaturation step — a labour later removed by the thermostable Taq polymerase of the group's 1988 Science paper, which turned PCR into a closed-tube, automatable process.

Fig. 1 (placeholder). The principle of the polymerase chain reaction: primers (arrows) on opposite strands delimit the segment; each cycle of denaturation, annealing and extension doubles the number of molecules spanning both primer sites, so the target grows exponentially, 2n after n cycles.

3. Efficiency and specificity

Amplification was monitored by dot-blot hybridisation with radiolabelled oligonucleotide probes specific to the amplified region. After 20 cycles the target sequence had increased some 220,000-fold — close to the theoretical 2²⁰ ≈ 10⁶ discounted by imperfect efficiency — and the product was a discrete fragment of the expected 110 bp on gel electrophoresis. Human DNA was required only in sub-microgram amounts, and the reaction tolerated crude preparations. A single cycle added detectable signal; twenty cycles brought a single-copy gene into the range of direct enzymatic analysis.

4. Genotyping the sickle locus

The diagnostic read-out exploits the site loss. In the normal βA gene the sequence CCTGAGGAG, read by DdeI (and, in a longer register, by MstII), spans codons 5–7; the sickle mutation changes it to CCTGTGGAG, destroying the site. Digest the amplified 110-bp fragment with DdeI: DNA from a normal homozygote (A/A) is cut into small fragments of roughly 80 and 30 bp (together with invariant control bands); DNA from a sickle homozygote (S/S) remains largely uncut at that position; a carrier (A/S) shows both patterns. The paper displays the three genotypes side by side — including DNA from cultured cells and clinical samples of known genotype — and all were scored correctly, blind. In a complementary approach, allele-specific oligonucleotide probes (RS06 and companions) hybridised only to their perfectly matched allele after amplification, pointing toward probe-based genotyping generally.

5. Discussion

The significance claimed is deliberately practical: prenatal diagnosis of sickle cell anemia from chorionic villus DNA becomes a one-day, non-radioactive procedure, and the same recipe — pick primers around any locus of interest — generalises to any Mendelian disease whose lesion is known, to HLA typing (the group's immediate next application), and to the detection of pathogens. The deeper significance unfolded later: PCR became the default front end of molecular biology and medicine — cloning, sequencing, forensics, phylogenetics, diagnostics of infection, and eventually the RT-PCR tests of pandemic scale — and Mullis received the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the invention.
