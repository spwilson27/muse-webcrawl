The Sequence of the Human Genome

J. Craig Venter, Mark D. Adams, Eugene W. Myers, Peter W. Li, Richard J. Mural, … and 273 co-authors

Celera Genomics, Rockville, Maryland, USA

Science 291 (5507): 1304–1351 (16 February 2001). DOI: 10.1126/science.1058040

ABSTRACT

A 2.91-billion base pair consensus sequence of the human genome was generated by whole-genome shotgun sequencing: some 27 million sequence reads from five anonymous donors, sampled at roughly fivefold coverage, assembled computationally into scaffolds spanning the euchromatic genome. The assembly and annotation yielded a surprisingly small catalogue of about 26,000–30,000 protein-coding genes — far fewer than the 100,000 once expected — together with maps of repeats, segmental duplications and more than two million single-nucleotide polymorphisms. The results demonstrated that whole-genome shotgun assembly, previously thought unworkable for a genome of this size and repetitiveness, is a viable strategy for large genomes. 1. Introduction: two strategies for one genome

When the Human Genome Project began in 1990, the public consortium adopted a hierarchical strategy: first build a physical map of large clones (BACs), then sequence clone by clone. Celera, founded in 1998 around the new high-throughput capillary sequencers, wagered on the opposite approach — the whole-genome shotgun (WGS), proven on bacterial genomes and on Drosophila in 2000: shred the entire genome at random, sequence the fragments, and let algorithms reassemble the jigsaw. Critics objected that human DNA, half of it repetitive sequence, would defeat assembly; the paper is the wager's settlement. It appeared in the same week as the consortium's draft in Nature, the two efforts having agreed on simultaneous publication.

2. Data production

DNA was collected from five donors (two male, three female) recruited under protocols designed to keep the sample anonymous and ethnically diverse; one donor's DNA supplied the majority of the material. Plasmid libraries with inserts of 2, 10 and 50 kilobases were constructed, and both ends of each insert were sequenced ("mate pairs"), the long-range pairing information proving essential for ordering contigs and bridging repeats. In all, about 27.3 million reads averaging some 540 bases were generated — 14.8 billion bases of raw sequence, a 5.11-fold coverage of the genome — supplemented by the public project's deposited data in the compartmental assembly variant reported alongside.

3. Assembly

Three assembly strategies were pursued. The Celera Assembler proceeded in stages: screen out vector and known repeats; find all overlaps between reads (the Overlapper); lay out contigs of uniquely assembled reads ("unitigs"); then use mate-pair constraints to order unitigs into scaffolds and fill gaps. Repeat copies were resolved statistically, by coverage depth and mate-pair consistency. The resulting consensus spanned about 2.91 Gbp in scaffolds; comparison with finished reference regions showed accuracy exceeding 99.99% over most of the assembly. Two assembly products were reported: a strictly Celera-only assembly and a "compartmental" assembly incorporating public data — the latter used for the annotation presented.

4. What the genome contains

Genes. Combining ab initio prediction (the Otto program), homology to known proteins and EST evidence, the annotation found evidence for roughly 26,383 genes, with additional lower-confidence predictions raising the total toward 30,000 — scarcely twice the count of the worm or the fly. The difference between human and worm, evidently, lies less in the parts list than in regulation and splicing: alternative splicing was found to be extensive, with many genes producing several transcripts.

Landscape. Genes are not spread evenly: there are gene-rich chromosomes (19 is the densest) and gene deserts stretching for megabases, notably on chromosomes 4 and 13. GC content varies in multi-hundred-kilobase blocks (isochores) correlating with gene density.

Repeats and duplications. About half the genome derives from transposable elements — LINEs, SINEs (including some 1.1 million Alu copies), retrovirus-like elements and DNA transposons — alongside large segmental duplications, blocks of 10–300 kb duplicated with over 90% identity, covering roughly 5% of the sequence and complicating both assembly and medicine.

Variation. Comparison of the donors' sequences yielded on the order of 2.1 million single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), a density near one per kilobase-plus, forming a map usable for association studies of disease.

5. Comparison and concordance

Where the two drafts overlapped, they agreed: more than 95% of the euchromatic sequence was present in both, gene predictions concurred on the great majority of loci, and the SNP sets overlapped substantially. The residual differences — Celera's longer scaffolds versus the consortium's clone-based finishing — were argued over vigorously in subsequent commentary, but the scientific core, the sequence itself and its low gene count, was common ground.

6. Implications

The paper closes by recasting the genome as the beginning rather than the end of the project: the parts list enables proteomics, pharmacogenomics and comparative sequencing of other vertebrates; the SNP map enables population-scale genetics; and the demonstrated success of WGS sequencing on a mammalian genome reordered the economics of sequencing everything else. Within a few years essentially every new genome — mouse, rat, dog, chimpanzee — would be a shotgun genome.
