Novel Proteinaceous Infectious Particles Cause Scrapie

Stanley B. Prusiner

Departments of Neurology and of Biochemistry and Biophysics, University of California, San Francisco

Science 216 (4542): 136–144 (9 April 1982). DOI: 10.1126/science.6801762

ABSTRACT

The evidence is reviewed that the infectious agent of scrapie — and by extension of the related transmissible spongiform encephalopathies kuru, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease and transmissible mink encephalopathy — contains no essential nucleic acid and may consist essentially of a single protein. Infectivity resists nucleases, ultraviolet and ionising radiation at doses lethal to viruses, zinc ions, psoralens and hydroxylamine, yet is destroyed by proteases and by reagents that denature or modify proteins. To mark the break with viruses and viroids, the author proposes the term prion — "proteinaceous infectious particle" — for these agents, and discusses models in which a protein replicates by imposing its conformation, or its expression, on a host counterpart. 1. The scrapie problem

Scrapie, a fatal neurodegenerative disease of sheep and goats known for centuries, can be transmitted by inoculation with brain extracts after incubation periods of months to years. The human diseases kuru — spread among the Fore people of New Guinea by ritual cannibalism and shown transmissible by Gajdusek — and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease behave identically in the laboratory, as do transmissible mink encephalopathy and chronic wasting disease of deer. Yet the responsible agent fits no known category: it provokes no immune response, no inflammation, no detectable virus particles by electron microscopy, and it survives treatments — formalin, boiling in part, radiation — that destroy every virus. Alper's radiation experiments of the 1960s had already hinted that the agent's target size was far smaller than any virus genome and that it might contain no nucleic acid at all; Griffith in 1967 had speculated in print that a protein might replicate itself. The present paper gathers a decade of purification work into a single claim.

2. Purification and the protein requirement

Starting from scrapie-infected Syrian hamster brain — the hamster model having shortened incubation times to a workable ~70 days — the author's laboratory developed purification schemes (detergent extraction, differential centrifugation, nuclease and protease-controlled digestions) enriching infectivity many thousandfold. At each stage, infectivity tracked with protein and not with nucleic acid: extensive digestion with nucleases (micrococcal nuclease, DNases, RNases) left titres unchanged, while proteases (proteinase K, trypsin under appropriate conditions), sodium dodecyl sulphate at sufficient concentration, chaotropic salts such as guanidinium thiocyanate, phenol, urea, and reagents modifying amino acid side chains (diethylpyrocarbonate, with reversal by hydroxylamine paralleling loss and return of infectivity patterns) destroyed or reduced infectivity. No scrapie-specific nucleic acid could be found in the most purified fractions, at limits excluding genomes of viroid size.

3. Properties inconsistent with a virus

The paper tabulates the agent's defiance: resistance to UV at 254 nm with an action spectrum unlike nucleic acid absorption; target size by ionising radiation of the order of a small protein (tens of kilodaltons); insensitivity to psoralens and to zinc ions that inactivate viruses; resistance to procedures (nucleases, harsh denaturants in part) no virus survives; and, on the other side, a strict dependence on protein integrity. Several distinct "strains" of scrapie, with stable incubation periods and lesion profiles in defined mouse genotypes, exist — a fact the protein-only hypothesis must somehow accommodate, by conformational differences, the paper suggests, though the difficulty is candidly acknowledged.

4. The prion proposal

The author therefore proposes a new term and a new class of pathogen: "prions are defined as small proteinaceous infectious particles which are resistant to inactivation by most procedures that modify nucleic acids" (as remembered). Models for prion replication current in the paper include: a protein that activates a dormant cellular gene for its own synthesis; a protein that, once present, is copied by reversal of the normal flow (protein → protein templating of conformation); and small proteins complexed with host components. The paper favours no single mechanism but insists the chemistry of the particle is protein. (The major prion protein, PrP 27–30, was identified by the group the same year; its gene proved to be a normal host gene, and the mature theory — PrPSc templating the misfolding of PrPC — reconciles strain behaviour with protein polymorphism.)

5. Implications

If proteins alone can be infectious, the classification of pathogens gains a third great category beside viruses and cells, and several mysterious diseases — kuru, CJD, Gerstmann–Sträussler syndrome — gain a unifying chemistry. The paper closes by urging that the scrapie agent's structure be solved, since "the elucidation of the molecular structure of prions may have important implications" (in substance) for the dementias of old age more broadly. The reception was hostile at first — "heresy", a protein that replicates — but the identification of PrP, the genetics of inherited prion disease, the species-barrier phenomenon, and finally BSE ("mad cow disease", 1986 onward) and its human counterpart vCJD converted the field. Prusiner received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1997, unshared, "for his discovery of prions — a new biological principle of infection".
