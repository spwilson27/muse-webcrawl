The Quantum Theory of the Electron

P. A. M. Dirac

St John's College, Cambridge

Proceedings of the Royal Society of London A, Vol. 117, No. 778, pp. 610–624 (1928) · DOI: 10.1098/rspa.1928.0023

Abstract

The relativistic wave equation previously used for the electron (the Klein–Gordon equation) is unsatisfactory: being quadratic in the time derivative, it does not yield a positive-definite probability density and it cannot describe electron spin. This paper seeks a wave equation that is first order in ∂/∂t — as the Schrödinger equation is — while remaining consistent with the relativistic relation between energy and momentum. Such an equation is obtained by writing the Hamiltonian as a linear form in the momentum components, with coefficients that are new dynamical variables represented by 4 × 4 matrices. The wave function accordingly has four components. From the resulting equation, the electron's spin angular momentum of ½ħ and its magnetic moment of one Bohr magneton emerge automatically, without being inserted by hand, and the equation gives the correct fine structure of the hydrogen spectrum. Difficulties remain: the theory admits states of negative kinetic energy, whose physical meaning is not yet clear.

1. Introduction: the Defects of the Existing Theory

Quantum mechanics in its wave form accounts well for non-relativistic electrons, and Pauli has shown how spin can be incorporated by giving the wave function two components and adding terms for the magnetic moment. Pauli's scheme, however, is an addition to the theory rather than a consequence of it, and when relativity is introduced through the Klein–Gordon equation — which replaces the energy by an operator quadratic in the time derivative — the probability density can become negative, a fatal defect for a theory that interprets |ψ|² as a density. The root of the trouble is the asymmetry between space and time derivatives: relativity demands that the equation treat them on the same footing, while a sensible probability interpretation demands that the equation be linear in ∂/∂t. The way out is an equation linear in all four derivatives, whose Hamiltonian is a linear form in the momentum.

2. A Hamiltonian Linear in the Momentum

For a free electron the relativistic energy satisfies

E² = c²(p1² + p2² + p3²) + m²c⁴

We require a Hamiltonian of the form

H = c(α1p1 + α2p2 + α3p3) + βmc²

where the quantities α1, α2, α3, β are independent of position and momentum and must be chosen so that H² reproduces the relativistic energy relation. Squaring H shows this is possible if and only if the new variables obey

αi² = β² = 1,    αiαj + αjαi = 0 (i ≠ j),    αiβ + βαi = 0

— that is, they anticommute in pairs and square to unity. Ordinary numbers cannot do this, but matrices can: the smallest representation is by 4 × 4 matrices, built from the Pauli matrices σ (with ρ matrices supplying the fourth). The wave function ψ is therefore a column of four components, and the wave equation reads

(E + c α·p + βmc²)ψ = 0,   with E → iħ ∂/∂t, p → −iħ ∇

The probability density ψ†ψ is now positive definite, as required, and current conservation follows in the usual way.

3. Spin and the Magnetic Moment as Consequences

Place the electron in an electromagnetic field by the standard substitution p → p + (e/c)A, E → E − eφ. Computing the orbital angular momentum L = x × p from the equations of motion shows that L by itself is not conserved even for a free electron — a shocking result for an isolated particle. The deficit is exactly made good by adding a new angular momentum

Σ = ½ħ σ′,   so that J = L + ½ħ σ′ is conserved

where σ′ is a four-component generalization of the Pauli matrices. The theory therefore requires the electron to carry an intrinsic angular momentum of magnitude ½ħ. Expanding the equation to non-relativistic accuracy, the same calculation yields an interaction energy with a magnetic field corresponding to a magnetic moment of exactly eħ/2mc — one Bohr magneton — with the gyromagnetic ratio 2 that Pauli had to postulate. Nothing about spin has been assumed; it falls out of the demand that the wave equation be at once relativistic and linear.

4. Application to Hydrogen

The equation is applied to an electron in the Coulomb field of a nucleus. Separation in polar coordinates, using the conserved total angular momentum J and a further constant of the motion arising from the operator β(σ·L + ħ), reduces the problem to radial equations that can be solved exactly. The energy levels obtained agree with the fine-structure formula of Sommerfeld — the formula that Sommerfeld derived from the old quantum theory and that has been verified spectroscopically — now obtained from a theory with a consistent probability interpretation and with spin included automatically.

5. The Negative-Energy States

One blemish stands out. For a free electron the equation admits solutions with E = −√(c²p² + m²c⁴) as well as the expected positive energies, and no redefinition can remove them, since the matrix algebra forces both signs. Classically such states would merely be ignored; quantum mechanically, transitions between positive- and negative-energy states would be expected, which are not observed. The paper notes the difficulty without resolving it: perhaps the negative-energy states correspond to some state of the electron not yet understood. (Dirac's later attempt, in the companion work and in 1930–31, was to fill the negative-energy sea and identify its holes with a new particle of positive charge — the positron, found by Anderson in 1932.)
