New Directions in Cryptography

Whitfield Diffie and Martin E. Hellman

Department of Electrical Engineering, Stanford University, Stanford, California

IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, Vol. 22, No. 6, pp. 644–654 (1976) · DOI: 10.1109/TIT.1976.1055638

Abstract

Classical cryptography requires sender and receiver to share a secret key, delivered beforehand over a secure channel — a requirement that becomes impractical when communication networks grow large, and one that makes digital signatures impossible in principle, since whatever the receiver can verify the receiver can also forge. This paper proposes a radical alternative: public-key cryptography, in which each user publishes an encryption key while keeping the matching decryption key secret. Two strangers can then communicate privately over a wholly public channel, and a user can produce signatures that anyone can verify but no one else can create. The mathematical engine is the one-way function, and the paper gives a concrete protocol — key agreement by exponentiation modulo a prime — whose security rests on the apparent difficulty of computing discrete logarithms. It also analyses the security of existing ciphers, notably the new Data Encryption Standard, against exhaustive key search, arguing that its 56-bit key is uncomfortably short.

I. Introduction: the Key Distribution Problem

The spread of computer networks and electronic mail is about to make cryptography a civilian necessity rather than a purely military art. But the classical scheme has a structural weakness. If n users are to communicate pairwise in secret with conventional ciphers, they need of order n² keys, each delivered by courier or some other protected channel before use; and a user who joins the network must somehow be supplied with all of them. Worse, the protection collapses if the traffic between two users who have never met must begin at once. A second, deeper defect is the impossibility of a true digital signature: in a symmetric system, any message the recipient can verify as authentic he could equally well have fabricated himself, so a signed electronic document can never bind its signer before a third party, as a handwritten signature does. Both defects vanish if encryption and decryption need not use the same key.

II. Public-Key Cryptosystems

Suppose each user A generates a pair of transformations: a public enciphering procedure EA, published in a public directory like a telephone book, and a private deciphering procedure DA, kept secret, with DA(EA(M)) = M for every message M, while knowledge of EA gives no practical way to compute DA or to invert EA. Then anyone can send A a private message by applying EA; only A can read it. No prior secret contact is needed, and the number of keys grows linearly, not quadratically, with the number of users. If in addition the pair can be applied in the reverse order, EA(DA(M)) = M, A can sign M by computing S = DA(M): anyone can check EA(S) = M, but only A could have produced S. The pair (E, D) must be built from a trapdoor one-way function — easy to compute, infeasible to invert, except with the secret trapdoor information used in its construction. (Unknown to the authors' readers, Rivest, Shamir and Adleman would exhibit, two years later, a concrete trapdoor based on the difficulty of factoring.)

III. One-Way Functions and a Key-Exchange Protocol

A candidate one-way function is exponentiation in a finite field. Let q be a large prime and α a primitive root modulo q. The map

Y = αX mod q

is cheap to compute (a few hundred multiplications), while recovering X from Y — the discrete logarithm — had, in 1976, no known algorithm much better than trying all possibilities. Two users who know nothing of each other can exploit this to agree a secret key over a public channel. A picks a secret random XA and sends YA = αXA mod q; B likewise sends YB. Each then raises the received value to his own secret exponent:

K = YBXA = YAXB = αXAXB mod q

Both arrive at the same K, which they use as a conventional session key. An eavesdropper sees only YA and YB, and deriving K from them appears to require solving a discrete logarithm. The scheme is not, by itself, authentication — a man in the middle could interpose two exchanges — and the paper is frank about this; signatures from Section II are the remedy.

IV. Cryptanalysis and the Cost of Exhaustive Search

The paper then turns to the security of conventional systems, and to a warning. Security against exhaustive key search is an economic quantity: the work factor must exceed what technology makes affordable, and computing costs fall exponentially. A machine built for about $20 million, in the technology of the day, could search the 256 keys of the proposed Data Encryption Standard in about a day, bringing the cost per recovered key within the reach of intelligence agencies and large corporations. The authors therefore argue that key lengths must be chosen with a generous margin — and that the DES standard, at 56 bits, is inadequate for high-value traffic. History sided with them: DES fell to public brute-force demonstrations in the late 1990s and was superseded by AES.

V. Conclusion

The conceptual reorientation is the paper's lasting contribution: secrecy does not require a shared secret to begin with, because computation itself can be asymmetric — easy in one direction, hard in the other, and easy again with a trapdoor. From this follow key agreement between strangers, digital signatures with non-repudiation, and the whole architecture of secured electronic commerce. The authors close by acknowledging the independent and then-unpublished work of Ralph Merkle on key distribution, and by noting how much of the programme — concrete trapdoor functions, proofs of security — remained to be done.
