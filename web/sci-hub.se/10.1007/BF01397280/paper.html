Über den anschaulichen Inhalt der quantentheoretischen Kinematik und Mechanik
(On the Intuitive Content of Quantum Theoretical Kinematics and Mechanics)

Werner Heisenberg
Institute for Theoretical Physics, Copenhagen
DOI: 10.1007/BF01397280
Abstract. The concepts with which quantum mechanics operates — position, path, velocity and energy of a particle — can acquire a precise physical meaning only through the measurements by which they are determined. Examining these measurements shows that the position and the momentum of a particle cannot both be defined with arbitrary sharpness: for their mean errors the relation Δq · Δp ≈ h holds in every case, as the examples of the γ-ray microscope, the Compton effect and the Stern–Gerlach experiment demonstrate. The relation is derived from the Dirac–Jordan transformation theory, in which canonically conjugate variables obey pq − qp = h/(2πi), and it is shown to be a direct expression of that commutation law. As a consequence, the classical ideal of causality loses its foundation: since the initial conditions of a mechanical process can never be known exactly, the law that the future follows necessarily from the present cannot even be formulated, let alone applied.

§1. The basic concepts: position, path, velocity, energy

A physical theory is intelligible (anschaulich) only if one can think through all its consequences qualitatively in simple examples. Quantum mechanics will therefore not be understood until one knows what the words "position" and "velocity" of an electron are to mean within it. These words must be defined by the operations that measure them, and the definitions must not transgress the formalism.

Consider the position of an electron. To fix it, one must illuminate it and observe it under a microscope. The resolving power of the microscope limits the accuracy attainable: Δq ≈ λ, where λ is the wavelength of the light used. But the observation itself disturbs the electron: in the Compton scattering at least one light quantum of momentum h/λ strikes it, so after the observation its momentum is uncertain by an amount Δp ≈ h/λ. The product is therefore

Δq · Δp ≈ h.

Shorter wavelength improves the determination of position and worsens that of momentum in the same proportion. This is no technical imperfection of the apparatus; it is a property of the concepts. The same accounting in every measurement of canonically conjugate quantities — energy and time, angle and angular momentum — leads to relations of identical form. We may take as the precise statement: the position and momentum of a particle can be spoken of simultaneously only with the characteristic inaccuracy specified by Δq Δp ≈ h.

§2. Dirac–Jordan theory and the commutation law

The intuitive considerations of §1 are the physical content of the formal theory of Dirac and Jordan. There, all physical quantities are represented by matrices (operators) whose multiplication is non-commutative; for a coordinate q and its conjugate momentum p the fundamental relation is

pq − qp = (h / 2πi) · 1.

A state is described by a probability amplitude; the most favourable case for the simultaneous knowledge of q and p is a Gaussian error distribution,

ψ(q) ∝ exp( −(q − q0)2 / 4(Δq)2 ) eip0q/h̄,

whose Fourier transform in the momentum representation is likewise Gaussian, with the product of the two widths attaining its minimum, Δq · Δp = ℏ/2 in the mean-square sense, and of the order h in any practical reckoning. No amplitude can make both widths smaller: the commutation law forbids it. The uncertainty relation is thus not an additional postulate but a theorem of the transformation theory, and the thought experiments merely exhibit it in concrete form.

§3. Examples

Determination of position. The γ-ray microscope of §1. To define the position of a free electron still more accurately by repeated observation is impossible in principle, since every observation is also a discontinuous intervention.

The Compton effect. In scattering, the conservation laws of energy and momentum connect the electron's recoil with the change of wavelength of the radiation; but which particular electron is struck, and in which direction its recoil carries it within the cone allowed by the conservation laws, the quantum theory does not and cannot specify beyond the probabilities. Here the statistical element is manifest in a single elementary process.

Determination of velocity. The velocity of a free electron may be measured by two position determinations separated in time; but the first determination has already disturbed the momentum, so the velocity so obtained refers to no undisturbed motion, and its inaccuracy is governed by the same product rule relative to the two measurements.

Stern–Gerlach experiment. The quantisation of direction in the magnetic field illustrates the complementary point: the magnetic moment's component along the field is sharp precisely because the conjugate azimuthal angle is wholly undetermined.

Passage through a slit. An electron diffracted at a slit of width Δq acquires transverse momenta spread over Δp ≈ h/Δq, in exact correspondence with the optical diffraction pattern: the wave picture and the particle picture agree in the accounting of what can be known.

§4. Discussion: the critique of the classical concepts and causality

The classical ideal was this: the world is distributed into objects existing independently of observation, whose positions and motions at any instant determine, through the laws of mechanics, their positions and motions at every later instant. Quantum mechanics dissolves both halves of the ideal. The concepts of position and path, applied to atomic objects, retain meaning only within the inaccuracy of §1; and because the present can never be exactly known, the strict formulation of the causal law — "if we know the present precisely, we can calculate the future" — is false not in its conclusion but in its premise. It is not that the law fails within the theory; it is that its preconditions cannot be created. In the author's view, quantum mechanics thereby establishes the definitive failure of causality in its classical form, while the statistical laws of the theory remain exact and complete of their kind.

Postscript. Recent remarks of Professor Bohr have drawn the author's attention to essential points which he had overlooked in the above discussion — in particular that the uncertainty in observation does not rest exclusively on the occurrence of discontinuities, but directly on the demand to give equal weight to the wave and corpuscular pictures, which are complementary descriptions of the same phenomena. The author gladly acknowledges that Bohr's considerations remove certain inaccuracies in the treatment given here, without altering its results.
