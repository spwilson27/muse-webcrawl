The Nature of the Chemical Bond. Application of Results Obtained from the Quantum Mechanics and from a Theory of Paramagnetic Susceptibility to the Structure of Molecules

Linus Pauling
Gates Chemical Laboratory, California Institute of Technology, Pasadena, California
DOI: 10.1021/ja01355a027
Abstract. With the application of the quantum mechanics to chemistry, notably through the work of Heitler and London on the hydrogen molecule and of Slater on many-electron systems, it has become possible to formulate rules governing the formation of the shared-electron (covalent) bond which rationalise the structural facts of chemistry. In this paper six rules for the shared-electron bond are developed. A bond is formed by a pair of electrons held jointly by two atomic nuclei, the electrons being unpaired and their spins opposed. The strongest bonds are formed by orbitals of greatest directional concentration; by combining s and p (and, in transition elements, d) eigenfunctions an atom prepares hybrid bond orbitals whose strengths exceed those of any pure orbital — the tetrahedral sp3 orbitals of carbon (strength 2.00) explain at once the tetrahedral direction of its four bonds. When several Lewis structures can be written for a molecule, the actual structure resonates among them and is stabilised by the resonance energy. Finally a magnetic criterion of bond type is given: the number of unpaired electrons, measured by the paramagnetic susceptibility, distinguishes planar (dsp2) from tetrahedral (sp3) coordination in four-coordinate complexes, and ionic from covalent bonding in general.

Introduction

In the twenty-five years since G. N. Lewis proposed that the chemical bond consists of a pair of electrons held jointly by two atoms, the electron-pair bond has become the foundation of structural chemistry; but the reasons why two electrons, rather than one or three, form a stable bond, and why the bonds of an atom point in definite directions — tetrahedrally in carbon, at right angles in the hydrides of sulphur — remained matters of empirical rule. The quantum mechanics now supplies the reasons. Heitler and London showed for H2 that the singlet state, in which the wave function is

ψ = ua(1) ub(2) + ua(2) ub(1),

with opposed spins, corresponds to strong attraction, while the triplet state corresponds to repulsion at all distances. Slater's method of constructing many-electron wave functions from single-electron eigenfunctions permits the argument to be extended to complex molecules. The purpose of the present paper (the first of a series) is to draw from these results a set of rules for the shared-electron bond, and to apply the rules to molecular geometry and to the magnetic properties of compounds.

The six rules of the shared-electron bond

The conclusions of the wave-mechanical treatment may be summarised as follows. (1) The electron-pair bond is formed by the interaction of an unpaired electron on each of two atoms. (2) The spins of the two electrons must be opposed, so that they can be described by the same spatial eigenfunction. (3) Once paired, the two electrons cannot take part in additional bonds. (4) The electron-pair bond is formed in the direction of maximum overlapping of the atomic orbitals: other things being equal, the strongest bond is the one of greatest concentration in the internuclear direction. (5) Of two orbitals in an atom, the one that can overlap the most will form the strongest bond, and the bond so formed will tend to lie in the direction of that orbital. (6) Of the two most important kinds of orbital available in an atom, s orbitals (spherically symmetric) and p orbitals (concentrated along an axis), the p bonds, being formed by the more concentrated orbital, are stronger than s bonds.

An atom is not confined to its pure ground-state orbitals. From the carbon atom's one 2s and three 2p functions there can be formed four equivalent linear combinations, each directed toward a corner of a regular tetrahedron, each of strength 2.00 — greater than that of a pure p orbital. These sp3 hybrids explain the tetrahedral carbon atom of van't Hoff and Le Bel, the angle 109°28′ between the bonds, and the great strength and uniformity of the C–H and C–C bonds in the paraffins. Three hybrids in a plane at 120° (sp2, strength 1.99) describe boron compounds and the carbon of the double bond; two collinear hybrids (sp, 1.93) describe acetylene and beryllium compounds. Geometry is thus not postulated but derived: an atom prepares, from its available functions, the set of orbitals of maximum strength in the directions of its bonds.

Resonance

It frequently happens that several electron-pair structures (Lewis structures) of about the same energy can be written for one molecule — two for benzene, three for the carbonate ion, two for carbon dioxide's rival description O=C=O together with charged structures, and so on. The true state of the molecule is then not any one of them but a hybrid, a resonance among the structures; its energy is lower than that of any single structure by the resonance energy, and its properties are intermediate, e.g. the six equal C–C distances in benzene (1.39 Å, between the single-bond 1.54 Å and double-bond 1.34 Å). Resonance requires that the structures have the same positions of the atomic nuclei and the same number of unpaired electrons, and it is most effective when the contributing structures are of comparable energy and the atoms concerned lie in one plane, as in the amide group and in conjugated systems.

The magnetic criterion of bond type

Wave mechanics assigns to each unpaired electron a paramagnetic moment; the molar susceptibility therefore counts unpaired electrons, μeff = √(n(n + 2)) Bohr magnetons for n unpaired electrons. This gives a criterion of bond structure independent of chemical intuition. In four-coordinate complexes of the transition metals, two arrangements are possible: bonds formed from 4s and 4p orbitals only (sp3, tetrahedral; the 3d electrons remain unpaired) or bonds using one 3d orbital as well (dsp2, square planar; the d electrons are forced to pair). Nickel(II) supplies the test: [Ni(CN)4]2− is diamagnetic — planar dsp2 — while [NiCl4]2− has two unpaired electrons and is tetrahedral. Similarly [Fe(CN)6]3− has one unpaired electron (d2sp3 covalent bonds) against five in [FeF6]3− (essentially ionic). Susceptibility measurements thus decide, in case after case, between structures which classical valence theory could only enumerate.

Summary

Rules for the formation of the shared-electron bond have been drawn from the quantum mechanics: bonds are made by opposed-spin electron pairs concentrated in the internuclear direction; atoms hybridise their orbitals to the strongest set compatible with the desired geometry; structures of like energy resonate; and the paramagnetic susceptibility provides a magnetic criterion distinguishing the possible bond types. Applications to particular molecules, and the quantitative treatment of bond energies, interatomic distances and partial ionic character, will follow in subsequent papers of this series.
